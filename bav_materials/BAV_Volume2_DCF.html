01 Bridge

From multiples to money

A multiple tells you what the crowd pays. DCF tells you what the thing is worth. This module rebuilds the machinery of discounting from zero.

1.1 Two honest ways to price anything

Suppose you want to price a flat. You have exactly two philosophies available, and there is no third:

Relative — Volume I
  • Find what similar flats nearby sold for per square foot.
  • Multiply by your flat's square footage.
  • Fast, market-anchored, needs almost no forecasting.
  • But you have simply inherited the market's price. If the whole street is mispriced, so are you.
Absolute — Volume II
  • Forecast the rent the flat will earn, year by year.
  • Discount those rents back to today at your required return.
  • Slow, assumption-heavy, but the number is yours and you can defend every input.
  • This is Discounted Cash Flow. It answers "what is this worth?" without asking the neighbours.

Volume I lived entirely on the left. Everything from here lives on the right. The professor's phrase for the DCF philosophy: value is the cause (it comes out of the cash flows), and the multiple is merely a consequence you can back out afterwards. Relative valuation reverses that arrow — it treats the multiple as the input. Neither is "wrong," but only one lets you say a stock is mispriced.

Why bother, if multiples are faster?

Because a multiple cannot tell you the crowd is wrong — it is the crowd. If Nifty trades at 22× earnings and you want to know whether that is expensive or cheap, no multiple can answer; you need an independent estimate of what the earnings are actually worth. That independent estimate is DCF. Every "the market is overvalued" claim, to mean anything, is secretly a DCF claim.

1.2 The one idea underneath everything: money has a timestamp

Ask the foundational question first, before any formula. Why is a rupee today worth more than a rupee next year? Not because of inflation (that is a separate, second reason). The deep reason: a rupee today can be put to work. Park it at your required return r and next year it is worth 1 + r rupees. So a rupee next year must be worth less than a rupee now — specifically, it is worth whatever amount, invested today, would grow into that rupee.

Run that backwards and you have discounting. If ₹1 today grows to ₹(1+r) next year, then ₹1 next year is worth 11 + r today. Compounding and discounting are the same machine driven in opposite directions.

Present value of a single future cash flow
PV = Ct(1 + r)t

Here r is your required return — the annual return you could earn elsewhere at the same risk. It is also called the discount rate, the cost of capital, or the opportunity cost. Same thing, four names. A riskier cash flow needs a higher r, which shrinks its PV. That single sentence — risk enters valuation only through the discount rate — is the spine of this whole course.

1.3 Two shortcuts you will use in every single problem

A company does not throw off one cash flow; it throws off a stream, forever. Adding up an infinite discounted stream sounds impossible, but two clean formulas collapse it. Let us derive them rather than memorise, because a formula you have built once you never forget.

The level perpetuity (no growth)

Imagine a cash flow of C arriving at the end of every year, forever. Its value is:

Geometric sum
PV = C1+r + C(1+r)2 + C(1+r)3 + …

Multiply the whole thing by (1+r), subtract the original from it, and every term cancels except the first. What survives is PV × r = C, so:

Value of a level perpetuity
PV = Cr

This is why a stable, no-growth company is worth its cash flow divided by r. You will meet 1/r again dressed up as "the P/E of a zero-growth firm."

The growing perpetuity (the Gordon formula)

Now let the cash flow grow at a constant rate g forever: next year C, then C(1+g), then C(1+g)2… The same multiply-and-subtract trick gives:

Value of a growing perpetuity — Gordon Growth Model
PV = C1r − g

C1 is next year's cash flow (the first one you receive), not this year's. The intuition for r-g: growth partially self-funds the discounting, so the effective discount rate is only the gap between what you demand and what the cash flow grows at. If g ≥ r the formula explodes — a business that grows forever faster than you discount would be worth infinity, which is a signal your assumptions are impossible, not that the company is priceless.

◆

Almost every valuation in this course is one of these two formulas applied to the right cash flow at the right discount rate. Ninety percent of the difficulty is defining the cash flow and choosing the discount rate consistently. That is the entire rest of the volume.

1.4 The thing we discount is cash, not profit

Here is the trap that catches every beginner. We just said "discount the cash flows." But when you open a company's accounts, you do not find cash flows — you find accounting profit (net income, EBIT, EPS). These are not the same thing, and discounting profit gives the wrong answer.

Why the mismatch? Because accounting runs on the accrual principle: it records revenue when it is earned and costs when they are incurred, regardless of when cash actually moves. A sale on credit is "profit" today but no cash arrives for 60 days. A machine bought for ₹100 is cash gone today but is expensed slowly over ten years as depreciation. Profit is an opinion about performance; cash is a fact about the bank balance. Valuation runs on facts.

The one-line reason this module exists

We have accrual-based accounting numbers, but we need cash-based numbers to discount. Module 2 is nothing but the translation dictionary between the two — why depreciation is added back, what capex is, and what working capital does to cash.

02 Foundations

Accrual vs cash: the translation problem

The accounts give you profit. Discounting needs cash. Here is the four-line dictionary that converts one into the other — and why each line is there.

2.1 Start from EBIT and walk to cash

We begin at EBIT (Earnings Before Interest and Tax) — the operating profit of the whole business, before we split anything between lenders and owners. Starting here (rather than at net income) is deliberate: EBIT belongs to everybody who funded the firm, and that neutrality matters later. Our goal: turn this accrual figure into the actual cash the business generates. Four adjustments do it.

Adjustment 1 — add back depreciation

When a company buys a ₹100 machine, the ₹100 leaves the bank today. But accounting refuses to call that ₹100 an expense today; it spreads it as depreciation of, say, ₹10 per year for ten years. So each year the income statement subtracts a ₹10 "expense" for which no cash actually leaves — the cash left years ago. Depreciation is a bookkeeping echo of a past outflow, not a present one.

Therefore, to get from profit back to cash, you must add depreciation back. You are not saying depreciation is fake; you are saying it is not this year's cash outflow, so it has no business reducing this year's cash figure.

Adjustment 2 — subtract capital expenditure (capex)

The flip side: the ₹100 the company spends buying new machines and buildings this year is real cash gone, but the income statement barely notices it (it will only appear slowly, as future depreciation). So cash-based valuation must subtract capex — the actual money spent on long-lived assets this year, which you find as the change in gross fixed assets on the balance sheet plus this year's depreciation.

Depreciation and capex are two views of the same asset

Depreciation is the accounting shadow of assets bought in the past. Capex is the cash reality of assets bought now. We remove the shadow (add depreciation back) and insert the reality (subtract capex). In a steady, no-growth firm the two roughly cancel — the company replaces exactly what wears out — which is why "capex = depreciation" is the standard no-growth assumption you will see in every base-case problem.

Adjustment 3 — subtract the increase in working capital

Working capital is the cash trapped in day-to-day operations: money owed by customers (receivables) plus goods sitting in stores (inventory), minus money you owe suppliers (payables). When a growing company sells more, it must carry more inventory and wait on more receivables — cash goes in to fund that, even though profit looks fine. So a rise in working capital is a cash outflow and must be subtracted. In the no-growth base case, working capital does not change, so ΔWC = 0.

Adjustment 4 — tax on operating profit, handled cleanly

We tax EBIT as if the firm had no debt, giving EBIT × (1−t). Why pretend there is no debt? Because the tax saving that debt actually creates (interest is tax-deductible) is a financing benefit, and we want to keep the operating cash flow pure — free of any financing decision. We will hand that tax saving back later, deliberately, as the "tax shield." Keeping operating and financing effects in separate boxes is the single most important discipline in this volume.

2.2 The result: Free Cash Flow to the Firm

Stack the four adjustments and you have the master cash-flow definition of the course:

Free Cash Flow (to the Firm) — FCFF, a.k.a. operating cash flow
FCF = EBIT × (1 − t)  +  Depreciation  −  Capex  −  ΔWorking Capital

Read it as a sentence: take operating profit, tax it as if unlevered, undo the depreciation shadow, pay for this year's real asset purchases, and fund the extra cash trapped in operations. What remains is genuinely free — cash the firm could hand to all its investors (lenders and owners together) without harming operations.

A useful compression for growth problems

Notice that Capex + ΔWC − Depreciation is exactly what the course calls Net Investment — the money ploughed back into the business over and above replacing what wore out. So the whole FCF formula collapses to a form you will use constantly once growth appears:

FCF = EBIT × (1 − t)  −  Net Investment

Net Investment = Gross Investment − Depreciation, where Gross Investment = Capex + increase in working capital. When growth is zero, net investment is zero and FCF is simply EBIT×(1−t).

◆

You now have the bridge Module 1 promised: profit → cash. Every valuation from here forward discounts some version of this cash flow. The next question is the subtle one — whose cash is it, and therefore at what rate do we discount it? That is Module 3.

03 Core

FCFF, FCFE & Capital Cash Flow

Three cash flows, three owners, three discount rates. Get the pairing right and valuation is bookkeeping. Get it wrong and every number is garbage.

3.1 The question that fixes everything: whose cash is this?

A company is funded by two kinds of people: lenders (who get interest and their money back) and owners (who get whatever is left). A cash flow is only meaningful once you say which group receives it, because that group's required return is the rate you must discount at. Mismatch the cash flow and the rate and you are discounting shareholders' money at lenders' rate, or worse. This is the "consistency" rule from Volume I, now doing real work.

There are three cash flows worth naming.

FCFF — Free Cash Flow to the Firm (what we built in Module 2)

Cash available to everyone — lenders and owners pooled together — after operations and reinvestment. It is computed before any financing, which is why we taxed the full EBIT and ignored interest entirely. Because it belongs to all fund providers, it must be discounted at the return all of them collectively demand: the WACC.

FCFE — Free Cash Flow to Equity

Cash available to owners alone, after the lenders have been paid and after any new borrowing has come in. Derive it from FCFF by settling with the lenders: subtract the after-tax interest they take, add back any fresh cash they lend you.

From firm cash flow to equity cash flow
FCFE = FCFF  −  Interest × (1−t)  +  Net Borrowing

Or, equivalently and more usefully, start from the bottom line. Since net profit is already after interest and tax, equity cash flow is simply profit, minus the slice of reinvestment the owners fund, plus the cash lenders inject:

FCFE, the practical form
FCFE = PAT  −  Net Investment  +  Net Borrowing

Being owners-only money, FCFE is discounted at the owners' required return, the cost of equity Ke. Never at WACC.

CCF — Capital Cash Flow

This one is subtle and it is where students lose marks, so slow down. When we computed FCFF we taxed the whole EBIT at rate t. But in reality the firm does not pay that much tax, because interest is tax-deductible — the government effectively refunds t on every rupee of interest. That refund is the interest tax shield, worth Interest × t each year, and FCFF quietly threw it away. Capital Cash Flow puts it back:

Capital Cash Flow
CCF = FCFF  +  Interest × t   =   (Cash to owners) + (Cash to lenders)

CCF is the true total cash the firm hands to all its investors, tax shield included. Because that shield is now sitting inside the cash flow, you must discount CCF at a rate that does not also count the shield — the pre-tax cost of capital (a plain weighted average of Ke and Kd, with no (1−t) on the debt).

3.2 The one insight that ties the three together

Look at what just happened. The interest tax shield is a real chunk of value, and it has to be counted exactly once. You have a choice of where to put it:

Put the shield in the discount rate
  • Use FCFF (shield excluded from cash flow).
  • Discount at after-tax WACC — the (1−t) on debt is the shield, baked into the rate.
  • The world's default method.
Put the shield in the cash flow
  • Use CCF (shield included in cash flow).
  • Discount at pre-tax cost of capital — no tax term in the rate.
  • Cleaner when leverage changes over time.
The double-counting trap

The shield goes in the cash flow or the discount rate, never both and never neither. Discount CCF at the after-tax WACC and you count the shield twice (over-valued). Discount FCFF at the pre-tax rate and you count it zero times (under-valued). This single mistake is the most common way to blow up a DCF, and the examiner knows it.

Why is FCF so popular, if CCF is "truer"?

Two reasons the professor gives. First, FCF keeps the messy tax shield out of the cash-flow forecast, so the forecast reflects pure operating performance — easier to sanity-check. Second, WACC is the number everyone already quotes and benchmarks. CCF is arguably more honest when debt levels swing around, but FCF-at-WACC is the market standard, so we lead with it and keep CCF and APV in reserve for the hard cases.

04 The payoff

Three roads, one value

If your three methods do not converge on the same number, one of them is wrong. Here they converge — on the professor's own OLP example — and watching how is the whole point.

4.1 The rented-flat picture, one more time

You own a flat. You bought it partly with a bank mortgage and partly with your own savings (equity). There are two honest ways to answer "what is my equity in this flat worth?"

  • Value your equity directly: forecast the rent, subtract the mortgage EMI, and value the leftover stream at the return you demand on your own money.
  • Value the whole flat, then subtract the loan: value the entire property as if it threw off all its rent to a single owner, discount at a blended rate reflecting both the bank's and your required returns, then subtract the outstanding mortgage.

Both must give the same equity value — it is the same flat. Valuing a company is identical: value equity directly, or value the enterprise and subtract debt. The professor's instruction is blunt: "Do the same thing while valuing a business." Let us prove the roads meet.

Quietly, we just met the star of Module 9

That pre-tax rate, 15.833%, has a name: it is the unlevered cost of equity, written ρ (rho) — the return shareholders would demand if the firm carried no debt at all. It is the weighted average of Ke and Kd with no tax adjustment: ρ = (Ke·E + Kd·D)/V = (0.20·700 + 0.10·500)/1200. Hold that number; APV is built on it, and so is the link WACC = ρ − (D/V)·Kd·t = 0.15833 − 0.0125 = 14.583%, which reproduces our WACC exactly.

05 Rates

WACC, properly understood

Everyone can write the WACC formula. Almost no one can say why the tax term is there or why the weights must be market value. Both fall out of one idea: the discount rate is a blend of what your funders demand.

5.1 Deriving the blend

A firm is funded by lenders demanding Kd and owners demanding Ke. The firm's overall required return must be whatever it takes to keep both groups satisfied — a weighted average, where the weights are how much of the total capital each group put in:

Weighted Average Cost of Capital — first pass
WACC = EV Ke + DV Kd

Two things are still missing, and each has a reason.

Why the weights are market value, not book value

You are discounting future cash to find today's worth. The weights must reflect how the firm is financed today, at today's prices — that is market value. Book value is a historical accounting record, often years stale, and using it silently assumes the market agrees with the accountant, which it rarely does. The professor's warning is precise: use market cap for equity, and market (not book) value for debt — though for debt the two coincide when the coupon equals the current cost of debt, which is exactly OLP's assumption.

Why the debt term is multiplied by (1−t)

Because interest is tax-deductible, the government pays part of your interest bill for you. If you owe ₹100 of interest and the tax rate is 30%, your taxable profit falls by ₹100, so your tax bill falls by ₹30 — the interest only truly costs you ₹70. The effective, after-tax cost of debt is therefore Kd(1−t). Baking the (1−t) into WACC is precisely how the tax shield gets placed in the discount rate — the same shield Module 3 said you could put here or in the cash flow. This gives the formula you actually use:

WACC — the working formula
WACC = EV Ke + DV Kd (1 − t)

5.2 Linking WACC to ρ — the bridge into APV

Recall ρ, the unlevered cost of equity, is the plain (no-tax) blend ρ = (E/V)Ke + (D/V)Kd. Watch what happens when we expand WACC and substitute:

Deriving the WACC–ρ link
WACC = DVKd + EVKe  −  DVKd·t
= ρ  −  DV Kd · t

Read that: WACC is just ρ discounted downward by the value of the tax shield per rupee of firm value. Debt does not make the firm's operations less risky — ρ is fixed by the business. Debt only adds the tax subsidy, and that subsidy is the entire gap between ρ and WACC. Check on OLP: 15.833% − (500/1200)(0.10)(0.30) = 15.833% − 1.25% = 14.583%. Exactly our WACC.

A subtlety that returns in Module 9 — which rate discounts the tax shield?

The formula WACC = ρ − (D/V)Kdt quietly assumes the firm keeps its debt-to-value ratio constant — so the tax shield is as risky as the firm itself and is discounted at ρ. If instead you assume the rupee amount of debt is fixed forever (the original Modigliani–Miller assumption from Volume I), the tax shield is as safe as the debt and is discounted at Kd, giving a different link: WACC = ρ(1 − t·D/V). Same firm, two financing policies, two WACCs. Which policy you assume is not a detail — it changes the answer, and it is the heart of the APV discussion.

◆

You can now compute WACC, justify every piece of it, and translate freely between WACC and ρ. That translation is the door to APV. But first we have to let the company grow — and growth is where students' cash flows silently break.

06 Growth

Adding growth

Growth is never free — it must be paid for with reinvestment. And the innocent act of using a constant discount rate secretly commits you to a growing pile of debt. Both facts trip up almost everyone.

6.1 Growth costs money: net investment

To sell more next year, a company must buy more machines and carry more inventory and receivables. That reinvestment is real cash leaving the business, so it must come out of the cash flow available to investors. The professor splits reinvestment into two ideas:

  • Gross Investment = Capex + increase in Working Capital — every rupee ploughed into long-term and operating assets.
  • Net Investment = Gross Investment − Depreciation — investment over and above merely replacing what wore out.

Some analysts separate maintenance capex (needed just to stand still) from growth capex (needed to expand). In that language, depreciation is roughly the maintenance capex, and net investment is roughly the growth capex — the part that actually buys future growth. In the zero-growth base case, net investment was zero. Now OLP will grow at 5% and need ₹30 m of net investment next year, so:

FCF with growth
FCF = EBIT(1−t) − Net Investment  =  250(0.70) − 30  =  175 − 30  =  145

6.2 The hidden clause in "use a constant discount rate"

Here is the idea that separates a B-plus from an A. Whenever you discount at a constant rate — a constant WACC or a constant Ke — you are implicitly assuming the firm's debt-to-equity ratio stays constant over time. Why? Because WACC and Ke both depend on D/E; the only way they can stay fixed is if D/E stays fixed.

But if equity value grows at 5% and D/E must stay constant, then debt must also grow at 5%. So each year the firm borrows more — specifically D0 × g = 500 × 5% = ₹25 m of fresh debt next year. That new cash flows to the shareholders as part of their dividend. Read that twice: under a constant discount rate, part of the dividend is being paid out of freshly borrowed money.

The professor's trap, verbatim in spirit

"Do not criticise this assumption — that D/E is constant and dividends are partly paid from borrowed money — and at the same time use a constant discount rate." The two are a package. If you dislike the assumption, you must change the discount rate every single year to match the changing D/E — and then you must ask, honestly, for how many years you can realistically forecast a shifting capital structure. This is exactly the pain that motivates APV in Module 9.

Value rose from ₹1,200 m to ₹1,400 m. But say why in business terms, not "because g went up." Growth added value here only because the ₹30 m reinvested earns more than the 20% shareholders demand — the Volume I rule "growth creates value only when ROE > r" is still governing everything. If OLP reinvested at a return below its cost of capital, adding growth would have destroyed value and the enterprise value would have fallen below ₹1,200 m. Growth is a magnifier of the sign of (ROE − r), not a good in itself.

07 Horizon

Terminal value

You cannot forecast a company year-by-year forever. So you forecast explicitly for a while, then capture "everything after that" in a single terminal value — which quietly ends up being most of the answer.

7.1 Why a two-stage model exists

The Gordon formula assumes a constant growth rate forever. Real companies do not oblige: a young firm might grow 25% for five years, then settle. You cannot apply one growth rate to that. The fix is a two-stage DCF:

  • Stage 1 — explicit forecast: project FCF year by year for a horizon (typically 5–10 years) while the business is still changing, and discount each year individually.
  • Stage 2 — terminal value: once the firm reaches a steady state — modest, constant growth — collapse all remaining cash flows into one number using Gordon, then discount that number back too.
Two-stage enterprise value
EV = ∑ FCFt(1+WACC)t  +  TVn(1+WACC)n
where   TVn = FCFn+1WACC − g

Note the terminal value TVn is computed as at the end of year n, so it still has to be discounted back n years to today. Forgetting that second discounting is a classic slip.

7.2 The steady-state logic behind the terminal growth rate

What growth rate goes into the terminal value? In steady state a company has largely run out of exceptional growth opportunities, so it reinvests little and pays most earnings out — a high payout ratio, low growth. The terminal g is usually capped near the long-run growth of the economy: no firm can outgrow its economy forever, or it would eventually become the economy. That is why terminal g is typically a low single-digit number, not the exciting near-term rate.

7.3 The exit-multiple alternative — Volume I returns

Instead of a Gordon terminal value, many analysts apply a peer EV/EBITDA multiple to the terminal-year EBITDA. The reasoning: by the terminal year the firm is a stable, mature business, and stable mature businesses in its sector trade at some observable multiple — so borrow it.

Terminal value via exit multiple
TVn = EBITDAn × (steady-state peer EV/EBITDA)
Data point from the course material

Across a sample of stable-growth companies, the EV/EBITDA multiple ran roughly: average 8.6×, median 7.9×, spanning about 4.1× to 14.3×. A terminal multiple picked from this kind of range is defensible precisely because the firm is assumed to have reached the steady state those peers already occupy.

A quiet inconsistency to notice

The exit-multiple method mixes two philosophies: an absolute DCF for stage 1 and a relative multiple for stage 2. That is fine — but remember Charlie Munger's verdict on EBITDA from Volume I. EBITDA ignores capex, working capital, interest and tax, so leaning your terminal value entirely on an EBITDA multiple imports all of EBITDA's blind spots. Best practice is to compute the terminal value both ways (Gordon and exit multiple) and check they roughly agree.

◆

In most real DCFs the terminal value is 60–80% of the total enterprise value — the tail wags the dog. That is not a flaw; it reflects that most of a company's value lies in its distant, steady future. But it does mean small changes in terminal g or the exit multiple move the valuation a lot, so those are the assumptions to stress-test hardest.

08 The knot

The circularity problem

To value the firm you need WACC. To compute WACC you need the firm's value. Each input is the other's output. This is a real logical knot — and PYQ 2 asks you to untie it.

8.1 Where the snake eats its tail

Write the two facts we have built and stare at them together:

The two equations that create the loop
Enterprise Value = FCFWACC − g   ← needs WACC
WACC = DVKd(1−t) + EVKe   ← needs V (because E = V − D and the weights are E/V, D/V)

The weights in WACC must be market values (Module 5), but the market value of equity is precisely what the valuation is trying to produce. You need the answer to compute the input that produces the answer. That is circularity, and it is genuine, not a trick of notation.

8.2 The four ways practitioners deal with it

  1. Ignore it. The professor's dry observation: "Most people do this." They plug in some reasonable weights and move on. Crude, but often close enough because WACC is not very sensitive to small weight changes.
  2. Use market cap for equity and book value for debt to compute WACC, then ignore the mismatch between the D + E you used for the weights and the final D + E the model spits out. A pragmatic hack that works when debt is modest.
  3. Assume a target D/E ratio. Decide up front the capital structure the firm is aiming for — say 30% debt — and compute WACC from those target weights. Because the weights are now fixed by assumption, WACC is a single number and the loop is cut. This is the cleanest defensible approach and the one PYQ 2 wants you to demonstrate.
  4. Solve the two equations simultaneously. Treat V and WACC as two unknowns in two equations and solve algebraically (or iterate: guess WACC → get V → recompute weights → recompute WACC → repeat until it stops moving). This yields the internally consistent answer with no assumption imposed.
The simultaneous solution has a clean closed form (zero growth)

Set V·WACC = FCF and expand the weights. The V's inside the weights cancel and the circularity dissolves:

V · WACC = D·Kd(1−t) + (V−D)·Ke = FCF
⇒ V = FCFKe + D · Ke − Kd(1−t)Ke

Check on OLP base case: V = 175/0.20 + 500·(0.20 − 0.07)/0.20 = 875 + 325 = ₹1,200 m. The knot untied gives exactly the enterprise value the three roads found.

8.3 The elegant escape: value equity directly

The professor plants a question: "Do we face circularity while valuing equity directly?" The answer is no — and seeing why is deeply satisfying. When you value equity directly you discount FCFE at Ke, and Ke is a given number that does not require knowing V. There are no market-value weights to compute, so there is nothing circular. That is not a coincidence: notice the closed-form solution above is algebraically identical to E = FCFE/Ke plus debt. The equity road was the untangled version of the WACC road all along.

The honest caveat

Equity-direct escapes circularity only if Ke is genuinely given. If instead you must derive Ke by re-levering a beta — and re-levering needs D/E, which needs E — the loop quietly returns through the back door. In this course Ke is handed to you, so the equity road is clean. In practice, the target-D/E assumption (route 3) is what keeps re-levering from reintroducing the circle.

◆

You can now state the circularity precisely and untie it four ways. Route 3 (target D/E) is your answer to PYQ 2's approach question. But there is a fifth, more elegant escape from the whole tangle — refuse to blend the rates at all, value the unlevered firm, and add the debt benefit separately. That is APV.

09 The method

Adjusted Present Value (APV)

Stop blending operating and financing risk into one muddy rate. Value the business as if it had no debt, then add the value the debt creates — separately, transparently, one piece at a time.

9.1 The idea, and when you reach for it

WACC bundles everything — business risk, tax shield, capital structure — into a single rate, and we saw that bundle create a circularity and hide a financing assumption. APV takes the opposite stance: unbundle. Value the firm in two clean, separately-defensible pieces:

The APV identity
VL = VU + Value of the Tax Shield

where VU is the value of the firm as if it were entirely equity-financed (pure business value, no debt), and the tax shield is the extra value the deductibility of interest bolts on top. This is a direct descendant of Volume I's Modigliani–Miller-with-tax result, VL = VU + tD — APV is that identity turned into a working valuation procedure.

Why APV earns its keep — two real cases from the course

Case 1 — Tata Tea & Tetley (2001). Tata acquired Tetley for £271 m, of which £205 m was debt raised specifically for the deal, to be paid down on a schedule. When debt is being deliberately repaid year by year, the D/E ratio is changing constantly — so a constant WACC is simply wrong. APV handles a bespoke debt schedule effortlessly: value the target unlevered, then add the PV of each year's specific tax shield.

Case 2 — the Nano subsidy. Gujarat lent Tata Motors ₹419.54 crore at 0.1% to site the Nano plant at Sanand. That below-market rate is a financing gift worth real money — but it has nothing to do with the plant's operating value. APV is built to price exactly this: value the project on its own merits, then add the PV of the interest saving as a separate financing benefit. The professor's rule: "APV is recommended whenever debt — or a change in it — causes problems."

9.2 Step one: the unlevered cost of equity, ρ

To value the unlevered firm you need the rate an all-equity version of it would be discounted at — the unlevered cost of equity ρ. It is a property of the business, not its financing. Three routes reach it; they are the same idea in three costumes.

  1. Unlever the beta, then use CAPM. Strip the leverage out of the equity beta to get the asset (unlevered) beta, then price it:
    βU = βL / [1 + (1−t)(D/E)]   →   ρ = rf + βU × MRP
    Use this when you have a beta but no clean Ke.
  2. Back it out of Ke. The relevering relationship links the two directly; solve it for ρ:
    Ke = ρ + (ρ − Kd)(D/E)
  3. Back it out of WACC, if you already have WACC, via the link from Module 5.

In practice, when you are handed Ke, Kd and the capital structure, the fastest correct route is the market-value blend — which is exactly the pre-tax cost of capital (the CCF rate) you already met in Module 4:

ρ as the market-value blend (the professor's working formula)
ρ = Ke + Kd(D/E)1 + (D/E) = Ke·E + Kd·DV

For OLP: ρ = (0.20·700 + 0.10·500)/1200 = 15.833% — the very number Module 4 flagged. It reproduces Ke when re-levered: 15.833% + (15.833% − 10%)(500/700) = 20%. ✓

9.3 Step two: value the unlevered firm, then add the shield

With ρ in hand, valuing OLP by APV is two Gordon formulas. Take the zero-growth base case (FCF = 175, annual interest tax shield = 50 × 30% = 15):

Unlevered value VU = FCF / ρ = 175 / 0.158331,105.3
Value of tax shield = 15 / 0.1583394.7
Levered value VL = VU + VTS1,200

₹1,200 m — identical to all three roads in Module 4. APV is not a different answer; it is the same answer, decomposed so you can see where value comes from: about ₹1,105 m of pure business, about ₹95 m of tax subsidy.

9.4 The fork in the road: how risky is the tax shield?

Now the deep point, the one the professor circles repeatedly and the one that makes APV subtle. To discount the tax shield you must pick a rate, and that rate depends on how safe the future tax shields are — which depends on your assumption about future debt. There are two canonical assumptions, and they give different shield values:

Debt held constant (classic MM)
  • The rupee amount of debt is fixed forever.
  • So the tax shield is as safe as the debt → discount it at Kd.
  • For a perpetuity: VTS = (D·Kd·t)/Kd = D·t = 500×0.30 = 150.
  • Gives WACC = ρ(1 − t·D/V).
D/E ratio held constant (rebalancing)
  • Debt grows with the firm to keep D/E fixed.
  • So the shield is as risky as the firm → discount it at ρ.
  • VTS = (Interest·t)/(ρ−g) = 15/0.15833 = 94.7.
  • Gives WACC = ρ − (D/V)Kd·t.
"But both gave 1,200 — how can VTS be both 150 and 94.7?" (resolve this before moving on)

Excellent objection, and here is the resolution. At zero growth with debt sitting at a fixed ₹500 m, the two assumptions physically coincide — the debt is constant and the D/E ratio is constant, because nothing is moving. When the assumptions coincide, they must give the same firm value, ₹1,200 m, and they do. The apparent conflict is only in the decomposition: MM splits 1,200 as 1,050 unlevered + 150 shield (using a true unlevered ρ of 16.67%); rebalancing splits it as 1,105 + 95 (using the blended ρ of 15.83%). From the outside — observing only Ke, WACC and total value — you literally cannot tell the two apart at zero growth. They are observationally identical. The difference only becomes real, and only starts to change the total value, once the firm grows or its debt schedule changes — which is precisely when APV becomes indispensable.

The "two equations" insight, stated plainly

Look at the identity FCF/(WACC−g) = FCF/(ρ−g) + VTS. It contains two unknowns you care about — WACC and VTS — but it is one equation. You therefore cannot solve for both independently. You must assume a financing policy (which fixes the tax-shield discount rate and hence VTS), and only then does WACC follow — or vice versa. This is the same lesson as the circularity module wearing different clothes: valuation with leverage always requires one financing assumption to be imposed by hand. There is no assumption-free answer.

◆

You can now run APV end to end: find ρ, value the unlevered firm, add the tax shield under a stated financing policy, and reconcile against WACC. This is exactly the machinery PYQ 1 demands — a growing OLP valued by APV. We will execute that PYQ fully in Module 11. 10 Edge cases

Preference capital & the tax nuance

Preference shares look like a third kind of funding, but through the tax-shield lens they are just debt with the one magic ingredient removed. And the US caps the shield entirely, forcing an "adjusted" tax rate.

10.1 Preference capital: leverage without the tax break

Preference (preferred) shareholders sit between lenders and owners: they receive a fixed dividend before common shareholders, but rank behind lenders. The single fact that governs their valuation treatment:

◆

Preferred dividends are not tax-deductible. Unlike interest, they are paid out of after-tax profit, so preference capital creates no tax shield. That one difference cascades through every formula.

Two consequences follow immediately:

  • FCFE changes: equity cash flow is now measured after the preferred dividend, because common shareholders only get what is left once preference has been served. FCFE = Net Income − Preferred Dividend (− net investment + net borrowing, as before).
  • FCF does not change: free cash flow to the firm is computed before any financing, so it is blind to whether financing is debt, preference, or common. The preferred dividend, like interest, is a financing outflow — it simply happens not to carry a shield. In the cost of capital, the preference term gets no (1−t) adjustment.

10.2 PYQ 2, alternative part — valuing an equity-plus-preference firm by APV

One PYQ 2 option asks: a company has only equity and preference capital (no debt) — value it by APV. This is a beautiful question because APV makes the answer almost obvious once you see it. Reason it through rather than reaching for a formula:

  1. Value the firm as unlevered. VU = FCF / (ρ − g), where ρ is the firm's asset return — the market-value blend of the common-equity and preference costs (no tax term, since neither carries a shield): ρ = (Ke·E + Kp·P) / V.
  2. Add the financing benefit. For debt this was the tax shield. Preference has no tax shield, so the benefit is zero. Therefore VL = VU + 0 = VU. Preference does not change the firm's value at all — it only reslices the pie.
  3. Carve out common equity. Value of common equity = VU − Value of preference, where Value of preference = Preferred dividend / (Kp − g).
The clean numeric illustration (no debt, zero growth)

The lesson APV makes visible: because preference carries no tax shield, the firm is worth exactly its unlevered value, and adding preference simply transfers claims from common holders to preferred holders without creating or destroying enterprise value.

10.3 The US wrinkle: when the shield is only partial

Everything so far assumed interest is fully deductible. Since the US Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017), it is not: business interest deductions are capped at 30% of adjusted taxable income (from 2024, roughly 30% of EBIT). Interest above the cap is disallowed this year (carried forward), so the firm gets less shield than the full Interest × t. adj t = TaxUnlevered − TaxLeveredInterest

PYQ 2 (option A) · the circularity approach

The circularity problem arises because WACC depends on the firm's value through its D/E ratio. Show how you would find both the enterprise value and the implied WACC when you assume a target market-value D/E ratio. (No numbers required — show the approach.)

Reveal the solution approach

Why there is a problem. WACC = (D/V)Kd(1−t) + (E/V)Ke needs market-value weights, but E is the output of EV = FCF/(WACC−g). Value depends on WACC; WACC depends on value.

The escape — impose the ratio, do not derive it. If you assume a target market-value ratio D/E (equivalently target weights wd and we = 1−wd), the weights become fixed constants that no longer depend on V. The loop is cut. Procedure:

  1. From the target D/E, fix the weights: wd = D/(D+E), we = E/(D+E). If Ke must be re-levered, re-lever it at this same target D/E — still a fixed number.
  2. Compute WACC once, directly: WACC = weKe + wdKd(1−t). It is now a single number, independent of V.
  3. Compute enterprise value: EV = FCF1 / (WACC − g).
  4. Recover the value split consistent with the target: E = we·EV, D = wd·EV. By construction these reproduce the assumed D/E, so the result is internally consistent.

The alternative — solve the two equations simultaneously. Keep D known and treat V and WACC as unknowns in the pair {V = FCF/(WACC−g); WACC = (D/V)Kd(1−t) + ((V−D)/V)Ke}. Substituting the second into the first, the V inside the weights cancels and you get one linear equation in V:

V = FCF + D·[Ke − Kd(1−t)]Ke − g  → solve for V, then back out WACC = FCF/V + g

Either method delivers both the enterprise value and the implied WACC without ever needing the answer in advance. The first imposes a policy; the second lets the numbers settle themselves. In an exam, present the target-D/E procedure as the main approach and mention the simultaneous solution as the exact alternative.

Enterprise value, done properly

The three roads gave you "debt + equity." The textbook is fussier: enterprise value is what it would actually cost you to buy the operating business — which means stripping out anything you could sell the next morning without touching the business at all.

A.1 What you actually pay: the flat, again

Return to a flat, because the professor's own analogy is the cleanest way in. Suppose a flat costs ₹50 lakh. You put in ₹10 lakh of your own savings (equity) and borrow ₹40 lakh from the bank (debt). You now own an asset worth ₹50 lakh, financed 40 debt + 10 equity. So far the whole ₹50 lakh looks like the "value" of the flat.

But the flat comes with furniture worth ₹8 lakh that you could sell in the open market tomorrow, without affecting the flat one bit. So what did it really cost you to own the flat? ₹50 lakh out, ₹8 lakh recoverable → an effective cost of ₹42 lakh. That ₹42 lakh — not the ₹50 lakh — is the analogue of a company's enterprise value.

Enterprise value, the careful definition
EV = Mkt value of Debt + Mkt value of Equity − Cash − Marketable securities & other non-operating investments

Equivalently: EV = (what it costs to buy every claim on the firm) − (everything inside the firm you could immediately dispose of without harming operations). The three roads in Module 4 quietly assumed there were no such disposable assets — so their "D + E" already was the enterprise value. This module is what changes when that assumption fails.

One method to rule them all

FCF and CCF are not rival methods. The textbook shows they are two settings of a single dial — and that you may set the dial anywhere and still get the same value, provided you count the tax shield exactly once.

B.1 The identity underneath everything

Start from an accounting fact that is true for any firm, no valuation involved. Net operating profit after tax can be sliced two ways:

The unifying identity
EBIT(1−t) = (PBT + I)(1−t) = PAT + I(1−t)

Read left to right: since PBT = EBIT − I, the term (PBT + I)(1−t) is just EBIT(1−t); and expanding PAT = PBT(1−t) gives PAT + I(1−t). Nothing has been assumed — it is algebra. But it is the hinge on which every method turns, because it says the firm's operating cash (left side) equals the shareholders' cash plus the after-tax cost of the debt (right side).

B.2 The generalised formula — a "fictitious tax rate" you may choose freely

Here is the professor's payoff. Any zero-growth firm can be valued by

The generalised valuation formula
V = PAT + I(1 − tᵢ)Ke·(E/V) + Kd(1 − tᵢ)·(D/V)

where tᵢ is a fictitious tax rate — a dial you may set to any value you like. (PAT itself is always computed at the firm's real tax rate; the dial only appears on the interest term, top and bottom.) The remarkable claim: the value V does not depend on tᵢ at all. Turn the dial to 0, to 30%, to 50% — the same enterprise value falls out every time, because the tᵢ in the numerator and the tᵢ in the denominator cancel in the algebra. The tax shield is being accounted for once; tᵢ merely chooses where you park it.

Two values of tᵢ have names you already know:

That is the whole secret of Module 3 and Module 4 in one line: FCF puts the tax shield in the rate (the (1−t) on debt), CCF puts it in the cash flow (the +I·t), and both are the same firm seen from two settings of one dial. The three roads converge for the same reason the dial is free to turn.

When leverage won't sit still

Module 9 gave you two financing policies. The textbook gives three, a master table of every formula each one implies, and a proper worked valuation where the debt is paid down year by year — the LBO case where a constant WACC is simply illegal.

C.1 Three financing policies, not two

Everything hinges on one question: how does the firm's debt behave in the future? Your answer fixes how safe the tax shields are, and therefore the rate you discount them at. There are three canonical answers:

  1. Case 1 — the rupee value of debt is held constant. Classic Modigliani–Miller. Debt is a fixed number of rupees forever, so the shield is as safe as the debt → discount it at Kd.
  2. Case 2 — the D/E ratio is held constant, rebalanced continuously. Debt grows with the firm, so the shield is as risky as the firm → discount it at ρ. This is the working assumption behind the FCF/WACC approach.
  3. Case 3 — the D/E ratio is held constant, but rebalanced only once a year. A hybrid (Miles–Ezzell): the first year's shield is already known (this year's debt is known), so discount it at Kd; every later shield is risky, so discount at ρ.

C.4 Practice from the source text

Prof. Mohanty's own end-of-chapter questions, reworded. Attempt each before revealing the line of attack.

Textbook Q · preference, two roads

OLP has issued 12% preference shares worth ₹100 m and has zero growth. Value the company by both the CCF and the FCF approaches, and show the two give the same answer.

Reveal the line of attack

Preferred dividend = 12% × 100 = ₹12 m, and it carries no tax shield (paid from after-tax profit). So preference never touches FCF (which is pre-financing) — it only reduces the cash reaching common shareholders. FCFE = PAT − preferred dividend. Build ρ as the no-tax market blend of Ke, Kd and Kp; the CCF numerator adds back interest (with its shield) but not the preferred dividend. Both roads land on the same enterprise value because, as Appendix B showed, the shield is counted once either way. See Module 10 for the preference mechanics in full.

Textbook Q · growth without new debt

OLP grows at 5% but does not intend to raise any fresh debt. Its levered beta is 1. Value it by APV — and explain the difficulty the "no new borrowing" assumption creates.

Reveal the line of attack

Here is the trap. If equity grows at 5% every year but the rupee debt is frozen, the D/E ratio falls year after year. Falling leverage means Ke and the cost of capital are not constant — so the tidy constant-rate Gordon formulas (which assume constant D/E) do not strictly apply. This is precisely Case 1 territory (rupee debt constant), and it is why APV shines: value the growing unlevered firm at ρ, then add the PV of the shield on the fixed debt schedule separately. You cannot use a single WACC, because there isn't one.

Textbook Q · conceptual

Why is a levered firm's unlevered cost of capital ρ different from its cost of equity Ke?

Reveal the line of attack

Ke is the return equity holders demand once debt sits ahead of them — it carries business risk plus financing risk. ρ is the return the same business would carry with no debt — business risk only. Leverage magnifies equity's risk, so Ke > ρ whenever there is debt, linked by Ke = ρ + (ρ − Kd)(D/E). Strip the debt and the two collapse into one. (This is the MM Proposition II relationship you met in Volume I, now doing real work.)

Textbook Q · the cash debate

Some analysts define enterprise value as D + E ± cash. When does subtracting the closing cash make sense, and when would you not?

Reveal the line of attack

Subtract cash when it is genuinely non-operating — idle balances you could pay out tomorrow without harming the business (the usual analyst default, as in the Tata Steel calc in Appendix A). Do not subtract the portion the business truly needs to operate — the minimum working cash is an operating asset, part of the engine you are buying, not a disposable one. The honest version subtracts only surplus cash. As always, state the assumption; there is no single correct treatment.

Textbook Q · project appraisal

In project appraisal we assume the entire NPV is distributed to shareholders immediately. Why is that assumption harmless?

Reveal the line of attack

Value is created the moment a positive-NPV project is accepted, not when the cash is handed out. Whether the firm pays that value out now or reinvests it, the present value to shareholders is unchanged — because any reinvestment at the required rate of return is itself zero-NPV. So "distribute it all immediately" is a harmless bookkeeping convenience: it changes the timing of cash to shareholders, never its present value. (Stretch version: prove, for a firm growing at constant g, that unlevered cash flow must be discounted at WACC — not ρ — to recover enterprise value, using VL = VU + VTS.)

Getting the cost of capital right

The cash flows get all the attention, but the discount rate is where most valuations quietly go wrong. Prof. Mohanty's four technical points, one governing discipline, and the two cases the earlier modules never combined.

D.1 Four technical points about the cost of capital

The professor flags four mistakes as the ones that silently wreck a WACC. He notes all four are in the book — learn them as a checklist.

  1. The pre-tax cost of debt is the YTM, not the coupon rate. The coupon is fixed at issue and lives on the book; the yield to maturity is what the market demands for that debt today. They coincide only when the bond trades at par — which is exactly the condition under which market value of debt equals book value. Whenever a bond trades above or below par, use its market yield, not its coupon.
  2. The cost of equity is dividend yield plus capital gains, not the dividend yield alone. Ke is the shareholder's total expected return. A low-dividend, high-growth stock does not have a "cheap" cost of equity — most of its return is expected as price appreciation, and that counts.
  3. The weights must be market-value, not book-value. Titan's book value per share was around ₹230 while it traded near ₹4,629; shareholders expect their ~12% return on the ₹4,629 they could actually sell at, not on the book figure. Even a promoter should care about market value, because market value is the price at which the shares actually change hands.
  4. The discount rate must match the riskiness of the cash flow. This is the big one, and it gets its own section below.

D.2 Match the discount rate to the cash flow

A discount rate is not a property of who is doing the discounting — it is a property of the cash flow being discounted. Three ways this bites:

Match the risk (acquirer vs target)
  • An acquirer with a 12% WACC is buying a riskier target whose cash flows warrant 15%.
  • Discount the target's cash flows at 15% — the rate that matches their risk — not at the acquirer's 12%.
  • Discounting riskier cash flows at a lower rate overstates value. "Riskier cash flows at lower rates" is the classic error.
  • A project that returns exactly your cost of capital is zero-NPV — the share price does not move.
Match the currency & the inflation basis
  • Currency: rupee cash flows → a rupee cost of capital; dollar cash flows → a dollar cost of capital. To value Emirates' USD cash flows you need a USD cost of capital — convert, don't mix.
  • Inflation: nominal cash flows → nominal rate; real → real.
  • If you forecast revenue growth, ΔRevenue must carry both ΔPrice (inflation) and ΔQuantity — a nominal forecast paired with a nominal rate.

D.3 Where the cost of equity actually comes from

Ke is not a single opaque number — it is three risks stacked on top of one another. For OLP (rf = 5%, MRP = 10%, βU = 1.167, D/E = 5/7, t = 30%):

The first two rows are ρ — the return the business carries, debt or no debt. The last row is the premium leverage bolts on. Strip the debt and the 3.33% vanishes, collapsing Ke back to ρ. This is the same ρ = 16.67% and the same Ke = 20% from Module 9, now shown as a stack — a clean way to see why Ke > ρ whenever there is debt.

The governing principle

Whatever sits inside your cost of capital, its ratio must stay constant for a single discount rate to be legitimate. With debt, equity and preference in the mix, keeping WACC constant means holding D : E : P all constant — which forces you to raise fresh debt and fresh preference each year (and distribute them), exactly as Module 6 raised fresh debt to hold D/E.

Footnote — when the payout itself is taxed (Dividend Distribution Tax)

If a dividend-distribution tax applies, handing shareholders ₹1 actually costs the firm ₹1 × (1 + DDT). So the effective cost of a payout rises: at a 10% DDT, a 15% preference dividend really costs 15% × 1.10 = 16.5%, and you gross the figure up before valuing (to deliver value 100 you must service 100 × 1.1). India abolished DDT in 2020, so this is now largely historical — but the mechanic recurs anywhere the act of paying out is itself taxed, and the professor works it as Example 2 in the text.

Do that, and the roads always meet. This is the discipline that separates a defensible valuation from a plausible-looking one.
