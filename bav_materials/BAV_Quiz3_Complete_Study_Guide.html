0.1 What "valuation" actually is

Strip away the jargon. A company is a machine that produces cash. You are being asked one question: what is that machine worth today?

AnalogyImagine a mango tree. It produces ₹10,000 of mangoes every year, forever. How much would you pay for the tree? Not ₹10,000 — that's one year. Not infinity — because money arriving in year 40 is nearly worthless to you today. The answer depends on how much you'd need to be paid to wait. If you demand 10% a year, you'd pay ₹10,000 ÷ 0.10 = ₹1,00,000. If you're nervous about the tree dying and demand 20%, you'd pay only ₹50,000.

That's the whole subject. The mangoes are cash flow. The 10% or 20% is the cost of capital. Everything else is detail about how to estimate those two numbers for a real company.

So there are exactly two hard problems in this course, and the two decks you were given map onto them one-to-one:

The problemThe numberYour material
How much cash will the machine throw off?Free Cash Flow (FCF)cashflow.pdf + Part 6 here
What return do the people who funded the machine demand?WACCWACC-2026.pdf + Parts 2–5 here
Why it worksWhy do those two numbers have to be the answer? Because of a no-arbitrage argument. If a machine produced ₹70 a year forever and you could get 17% elsewhere at the same risk, nobody would pay more than ₹70/0.17 = ₹411 for it — at ₹500 they'd just take the alternative. And nobody would sell it below ₹411 — they'd rather hold it. Competition drives the price to the point where the machine's return exactly equals the return available elsewhere at that risk. That indifference point is the value. The discount rate is not a made-up number; it is the return you are giving up by putting your money here instead of there — an opportunity cost.

0.3 Reading a P&L (and the ladder you must memorise)

The profit & loss statement is a ladder. You start with sales and subtract your way down. Learn the rungs in order — half the exam is knowing which rung you're standing on.

Revenue (Sales) − Operating expenses (raw material, wages, other, S&D, admin) = EBITDA Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortisation − Depreciation & Amortisation = EBIT = "Net Operating Income" = operating profit + Other income (interest received, dividends…) − Interest expense = PBT Profit Before Tax − Tax = PAT Profit After Tax = Net Income = "earnings"
Why the EBIT line is sacredEverything above EBIT belongs to the business. Everything below EBIT is about how the business was financed (interest = payment to lenders) and who taxes it. This is why every enterprise-level valuation starts at EBIT: EBIT is the profit the operations generate regardless of whether the company borrowed or not. Two identical factories, one debt-funded and one equity-funded, have the same EBIT and different PAT. Since we're valuing the factory, not the financing, we start at EBIT.
TrapEBIT is not "profit before interest and tax as printed". Indian companies often put "Other Income" — interest on their cash pile, dividends from investments — into the operating block. If you leave interest income inside EBIT and then also treat the cash as a separate non-operating asset, you have counted the cash twice. Section 3.10 of this guide is entirely about that mistake.

0.4 Time value of money and discounting

₹100 today ≠ ₹100 next year. Not because of inflation alone — even with zero inflation, ₹100 today can be invested and become more than ₹100. So:

Future value: FV = PV × (1 + r)^n Present value: PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)^n ← "discounting"

Perpetuity (a cash flow C, forever, starting next year, discount rate r):

PV = C ÷ r

Growing perpetuity (C next year, growing at g forever, requires g < r):

PV = C ÷ (r − g) ← "the Gordon growth formula"
Derive the perpetuity formula yourself (do this once, then you never memorise it again)

PV = C/(1+r) + C/(1+r)² + C/(1+r)³ + …

Multiply both sides by (1+r):

PV(1+r) = C + C/(1+r) + C/(1+r)² + … = C + PV

So PV + PV·r = C + PV → PV·r = C → PV = C/r.

For the growing version, replace C in year t with C(1+g)t−1 and the same trick gives PV = C/(r−g). Notice this blows up if g ≥ r — which is why "terminal growth must be below the discount rate" isn't a convention, it's arithmetic. It's also why terminal growth is usually capped near long-run GDP growth: no company grows faster than the economy forever, or it eventually becomes the economy.

0.5 Bonds: coupon, face value, market price, YTM

You cannot pass this quiz without this section. It is the source of the deck's very first "technical point."

A bond is a loan cut into tradeable pieces. Four numbers describe it:

Face value
What the borrower repays at the end. Say ₹100.
Coupon rate
The fixed % of face value paid as interest each year. A 10% coupon on ₹100 face = ₹10 cash every year. This is locked in on the day the bond is issued and never changes.
Market price
What the bond trades for today. This moves every day.
Yield to Maturity (YTM)
The discount rate that makes the PV of all the bond's future cash flows equal to its market price today. This is the return a buyer earns today — the real, current cost of that debt.
Analogy — this is the whole pointIn 2020 you took a home loan at 7%. Rates have since risen to 12%. Your EMI is still based on 7% — the coupon. But if you wanted a new loan today, you'd pay 12%. And if you wanted to sell your obligation to someone else, they'd only take it at a discount, because they can lend elsewhere at 12%.

7% is the coupon. 12% is the YTM. Only 12% tells you the real cost of debt capital today. The coupon is a historical accident of when you happened to borrow.
Why YTM and not coupon?The cost of capital is an opportunity cost — the return investors could get elsewhere for the same risk. Your lenders can currently get 12% on similar bonds. So 12% is what your debt costs the economy, even if your cheque book only shows 10%. The coupon is what you pay; the YTM is what the capital is worth. Cost of capital is always about worth, never about the cheque.
Worked micro-example
Micro-example — reading YTM off a price

A perpetual bond has ₹100 face value and a 10% coupon → it pays ₹10 every year forever. The market currently demands 12% on this risk class.

Market price = 10 ÷ 0.12 = ₹83.33. Face (book) value is still ₹100.

So: book value of debt = ₹100, market value of debt = ₹83.33, coupon = 10%, YTM (pre-tax cost of debt) = 12%.

You will see exactly these four numbers again in Part 2.1. They come from the deck.

TrapBond price and yield move in opposite directions. Rates up → old bonds (paying low fixed coupons) become less attractive → their price falls. This is why a company that borrowed cheaply years ago shows book debt above market debt, and vice versa.

0.6 Shares, market capitalisation, book value

Share
A slice of ownership. Owners get whatever is left after everyone else (suppliers, workers, lenders, taxman) is paid — the residual claim. That's why equity is riskier than debt, and therefore costs more.
Market capitalisation
Share price × number of shares. The market value of equity.
Book value of equity
Total assets − total liabilities, per the accountant. Historical cost, net of depreciation. Book value per share = book equity ÷ shares.
Slides — the Titan number you should rememberTitan's book value per share ≈ ₹230. Its shares trade around ₹4,629. That's a price-to-book of about 20×.

Why the gap? Because the accountant records what Titan spent — land at 1990s cost, stores at cost less depreciation. The market prices what Titan will earn — the Tanishq brand, the trust, the store network, decades of future jewellery margins. None of that is on the balance sheet. Book value looks backwards; market value looks forwards. Valuation is a forward-looking exercise, so we use market values. Always.

0.7 P/E, Enterprise Value, EV/EBITDA — the multiples you said you didn't know

Price-to-Earnings (P/E)
P/E = Market price per share ÷ Earnings per share (EPS) = Market capitalisation ÷ Profit After Tax

Read it as: "how many rupees am I paying for each rupee of annual profit?" A P/E of 25 means you're paying ₹25 for ₹1 of yearly earnings.

Why P/E is really just a DCF in disguiseTake a no-growth company that pays out all its earnings. Value of equity = PAT ÷ Ke. Divide both sides by PAT:
P/E = 1 ÷ Ke
So a P/E of 25 implies a cost of equity of 4%; a P/E of 10 implies 10%. Now add growth (payout ratio b, growth g): P/E = b ÷ (Ke − g). High P/E = low risk, or high growth, or both. A multiple is not an alternative to DCF — it's a DCF with the assumptions hidden. That's exactly why Mohanty makes you do the DCF: so you can see the assumptions someone else is hiding.
Enterprise Value (EV) — the most important definition in the course
EV = Market value of Equity + Market value of Debt − Non-operating cash Equity value = EV − Debt + Non-operating cash

Market cap is what the shareholders' slice is worth. EV is what the whole business is worth, regardless of how it's financed.

AnalogyYou buy a flat listed at ₹80 lakh. But it carries a ₹30 lakh mortgage you must take over, and there's ₹5 lakh cash lying in a drawer inside that comes with the flat.
· Market cap (what the seller pockets) = ₹80 lakh.
· Enterprise value (what the flat really costs you) = 80 + 30 − 5 = ₹105 lakh.

You add debt because you inherit it. You subtract cash because you immediately get it back. EV is the price of the machine; market cap is the price of the owner's stake in the machine.
Trap that costs marks every yearDiscounting FCF (a whole-business cash flow) gives you EV, not equity value. Students routinely stop at EV and call it the answer. You are not done. You must subtract debt (and add back non-operating assets) to get to equity — and the debt you subtract must be exactly the debt you used in the WACC weights. See Part 8.
EV/EBITDA and other multiples

EV/EBITDA compares a whole-business value to a whole-business profit. It's cleaner than P/E for comparing companies across different capital structures and tax regimes, because EBITDA sits above interest, tax and depreciation.

The consistency rule for multiplesNumerator and denominator must belong to the same claimants.
· EV ↔ EBITDA, EBIT, Sales, FCF — whole firm ✔
· Price (market cap) ↔ PAT, EPS, book equity, FCFE — equity only ✔
· EV/PAT and P/EBITDA are nonsense. Mixing a firm-level numerator with an equity-level denominator is the same category error as discounting FCF at Ke.
Book only — where multiples reappear in this chapterThe book uses the multiple method for one specific job: estimating market value of equity for an unlisted company, so you can compute its D/E and re-lever a beta. Example: Orissa Oil (unlisted), sales ₹6.5bn, sector median EV/Sales = 1.11 → imputed EV = ₹7.215bn; less debt ₹3.5bn → imputed equity ₹3.715bn. That imputed equity is then used in the Hamada equation. (Book pp. 134, and Appendix E step 4.) See §4.10.

0.8 Risk, return, and why only some risk gets paid for

Investors dislike risk. To hold something risky, they demand a higher expected return. So far, obvious. The non-obvious part — and it is the entire foundation of CAPM — is this:

Why the market only pays you for systematic riskSplit the risk of any single stock into two pieces:
  • Unsystematic (diversifiable / idiosyncratic) risk — the CEO resigns, a factory burns, a drug trial fails. Company-specific.
  • Systematic (market) risk — recession, interest rate shock, war, a demand collapse across the economy. Hits everything at once.
Now: hold 50 stocks. The idiosyncratic shocks partly cancel — one company's fire is offset by another's lucky patent. Hold 500 and they cancel almost entirely. So diversifiable risk can be eliminated for free, simply by holding a portfolio.

And here's the punchline: the market will not pay you to bear a risk you could have eliminated for free. If it did, everyone would diversify and bid the price up until the extra return vanished. So expected return depends only on systematic risk. That's what beta measures.
AnalogyAn insurance company writing 10,000 fire policies barely worries about any one house burning — that's diversified away. It worries about an earthquake, which burns 3,000 houses at once. The premium it charges reflects earthquake exposure, not individual-house exposure. Beta is a stock's earthquake exposure.

Beta (β) = how much a stock moves when the whole market moves.

βMeaningTypical of
1.0Moves exactly with the marketA broad index; a diversified large-cap
>1Amplifies the market — up more in booms, down more in bustsAutos, steel, airlines, young companies, luxury

1.1 One equation for everything

Slide 2 of the cash-flow deck is the whole course:

FCF₁ FCF₂ FCF₃ ∞ FCFₜ Value = ──────── + ──────── + ──────── + … = Σ ────────── (1+WACC) (1+WACC)² (1+WACC)³ t=1 (1+WACC)ᵗ

In words: the value of a company is the present value of every rupee of free cash flow it will ever produce, discounted at the rate its capital providers demand.

Everything else in both decks is answering one of two sub-questions:

  1. What is FCFₜ? → Part 6 (revenue → EBITDA → EBIT → NOPAT → gross cash flow → less gross investment → FCF)
  2. What is WACC? → Parts 2–5 (cost of debt, cost of equity, weights)
Why we can't just discount profitProfit is an accounting opinion; cash is a fact. Three specific reasons profit ≠ what you can take out of the business:
  • Depreciation is deducted from profit but no cash left the building this year — the cash left years ago when you bought the machine.
  • Capex is real cash going out but doesn't hit profit this year — it gets spread over future years as depreciation.
  • Working capital: a sale on credit is profit today but cash later. A growing company can be highly profitable and still run out of money.
FCF fixes all three: it undoes the depreciation deduction, subtracts the actual capex, and charges for the increase in working capital.

Why they must agreeBecause they're the same money, sliced differently. FCF is the whole pie. Lenders take a fixed slice (interest, net of its tax shield, plus principal). Shareholders get the rest, which is FCFE. Value the pie at the blended rate and subtract the lenders' slice, or value the shareholders' slice directly at the shareholders' rate — same house, two roads.

If they don't agree in your answer, you have made an inconsistency error. Nine times out of ten it's one of these three:
(a) you discounted FCF at Ke, or FCFE at WACC;
(b) you used book-value weights;
(c) you included a liability in the WACC weights but forgot to subtract it from EV (or vice versa).
The single most common error in this whole subjectDiscounting the wrong cash flow at the wrong rate. Slide 12 of the WACC deck says it in six words: "Equity cash flows discounted at Ke. Free cash flows discounted at WACC." Write it on your hand.

1.3 Explicit forecast period + terminal value

We can't forecast to infinity. So we split:

Value = PV of FCF for the explicit forecast years (say 5–10 yrs) + PV of the terminal value (everything after that) FCF_{n+1} Terminal Value_n = ─────────── (growing perpetuity, g = long-run growth) WACC − g
Trap — terminal value is 60–80% of the answerBecause most of a company's cash flows lie beyond year 5, TV usually dominates. Two consequences the exam loves:
1. Small changes in g move the value enormously. With WACC 13%, moving g from 4% to 5% raises TV by 12.5%.
2. Since TV dominates, the cash-flow pattern is back-loaded — which is why (§4.2) you must use the longest available spot rate as your risk-free rate, not the 1-year rate.
Class notes — the transition-year ruleThe final explicit year must be a genuine steady state: growth has settled at g, and the investment rate has adjusted to match (IR = g ÷ ROIC). If you drop growth to 5% but keep investing as if you were growing at 13%, your terminal FCF is far too small and you'll undervalue the company badly. §7.6 shows exactly this error costing 20% of the value.

Why the map is symmetric — and why that's the exam's favourite themeNotice the two vertical arrows meet. That meeting point is where consistency lives. Every single "gotcha" in this course is a violation of the symmetry between Track A and Track B:
  • Put a liability in Track B's weights? Then its interest must not be deducted in Track A, and it must be subtracted from EV.
  • Treat cash as non-operating? Then its interest income must not be in EBIT, and it must be added back at the end.
  • Use nominal cash flows in Track A? Then Track B must be nominal too.
  • Use rupee cash flows in Track A? Then Track B must be a rupee discount rate.
If you learn nothing else, learn to ask after every step: "is Track A still talking to the same set of claimants as Track B?"

Part 2 · The Four Technical Points

  1. Pre-tax cost of debt is not the coupon rate of the bond.
  2. Cost of equity is not the dividend yield.
  3. Weights must be market values of debt and equity, not book values.

Why the two errors cancel — see it algebraicallyEnterprise value = Equity + Debt. Equity is pinned at 315 by the P&L, which doesn't care what we call the cost of debt. So the only thing that changes is what we book as "debt" and what rate we attach to it — and WACC is defined so that FCF ÷ WACC reproduces EV. Change the debt value and the debt rate together in offsetting directions and you land on the same equity number. Consistency saves you even when the inputs are "wrong".

2.2 …but it does matter: the ₹500 million project that destroys ₹5.12m

This is the best example in the deck and the one most likely to be examined. It shows that "it doesn't matter" is only true for valuing a static company — the moment you make a new investment decision, using the coupon rate wrecks you.

Why exactly does the coupon method break here?Because the coupon rate is a sunk historical fact and the decision is forward-looking. When you borrow the extra ₹120.48m, the market charges you 12% — the YTM — not the 10% you happened to negotiate years ago. The coupon method assumes you can keep raising money at 10% forever. You can't. Cost of capital is always the cost of the next rupee, never the average of past rupees. The same logic is why we use market-value weights (Point 3) and marginal tax rates (§3.3): everything in cost of capital is marginal and forward-looking.

2.3 Point 2 — Cost of equity ≠ dividend yield

Dividend yield = Dividend per share ÷ Market price per share

A shareholder's total return has two components:

K_e = Dividend yield + Expected capital gain

A company that pays zero dividend does not have a zero cost of equity. Shareholders in a fast-growing company expect their entire return as price appreciation. Amazon paid no dividend for 25 years; its cost of equity was never 0%.

Book only (p. 109) — two further "nots"The book adds two more things cost of equity is not:
  • It is not the actual return the stock delivered over some past period. Realised ≠ required. If a stock crashed 40% last year, its cost of equity didn't become −40%.
  • It is the return required by the marginal investor — the one who trades frequently and is therefore likely to be the next buyer — and we assume that investor is well-diversified. This assumption is what licenses CAPM. The book flags that it breaks down for private companies (owner is undiversified, so total risk matters, not just beta) — dealt with in Chapter 9 of the book.

2.4 Point 3 — Weights must be market values

Slides — the two arguments he usesArgument 1 (Titan). Book value per share ₹230.19; market price ≈ ₹4,629. If you used book weights, equity would be ~5% of the capital structure instead of ~95%, and the WACC would be almost entirely the cost of debt. The answer would be garbage.

Argument 2 (the "X" question). X invested ₹100 in 2002. The book value of that investment is now ₹300; the market value is ₹1,000. Why should X bother about the market value?
Answer the "X" question properly before opening

Because ₹1,000 is what X can walk away with today. The ₹300 book figure is a bookkeeping record of past transactions; it isn't available to anyone.

That makes ₹1,000 the opportunity cost of staying invested: by not selling, X is choosing to keep ₹1,000 in this company rather than deploy ₹1,000 elsewhere. So the return X must demand has to be earned on ₹1,000, not on ₹300.

Now scale that up to every shareholder at once. The market value of equity is the amount investors currently have at stake in the firm. The cost of equity is the return demanded on that amount. So the weight of equity in WACC must be the market value.

The general principle: cost of capital is an opportunity cost of capital currently committed. Only market values measure capital currently committed. Book values measure capital historically contributed, which nobody can spend.

Corollary he likes to ask: if book weights were correct, then a company whose share price doubled would suddenly have a lower cost of capital with no change in its business — obviously absurd.

Book only — what if market value of debt isn't observable?Practically (Appendix E, step 4):
  • Equity: use market capitalisation. If unlisted, impute it via a sector multiple (§4.10).
  • Debt: if you know the exact redemption schedule, compute market value = PV of coupons + principal discounted at the current Kd. Otherwise, use book value of debt as a proxy — this is standard and accepted, because debt values move much less than equity values.
  • Operating leases (if you're treating them as capital): add the PV of future lease rentals to debt.
  • If cash is non-operating: use net debt.

2.6 Real vs nominal — the tomato example

WACC deck, slide 13

The tomato example — derive the Fisher equation without being told it

Real rate of interest = 3%. Inflation = 6%. What is the nominal rate?

Assume everyone in the economy buys only tomatoes. Tomatoes cost ₹100/kg today and are expected to cost ₹106/kg next year.

"Therefore I will deposit ₹100 in a bank today only if the bank gives me ____ next year."

Fill in the blank

Today, ₹100 buys me 1 kg of tomatoes.

A real return of 3% means: next year I must be able to buy 3% more tomatoes — i.e. 1.03 kg. That is what "real" means: purchasing power, measured in goods, not rupees.

Next year, 1.03 kg costs 1.03 × ₹106 = ₹109.18.

Nominal rate = 109.18/100 − 1 = 9.18% General: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation) (1 + 0.0918) = (1.03) × (1.06) ✔
TrapNominal ≠ real + inflation. 3% + 6% = 9%, but the answer is 9.18%. The missing 0.18% is the cross-term (0.03 × 0.06) — you need inflation compensation on the real return itself, not just on the principal. At low rates the approximation is harmless; in an exam with 6% inflation, the marker is looking for 9.18%.

2.6b Which to use — real or nominal? The ASL example

Worked example — WACC deck, slide 14 (ASL Limited)

ASL sells 1,000 units of JLTs each year. Unit selling price ₹100; unit cost of production (all direct costs) ₹60. Real discount rate 3%; inflation 4%. Ignore tax. What is the value of ASL?

Do it both ways — they must agree
Route A — real cash flows at the real rate

In today's purchasing power, ASL earns 1,000 × (100 − 60) = ₹40,000 per year, forever, and that never changes in real terms.

Value = 40,000 ÷ 0.03 = ₹13,33,333
Route B — nominal cash flows at the nominal rate

Nominal discount rate = 1.03 × 1.04 − 1 = 7.12%.

Nominal cash flow grows at inflation, 4% per year. Year-1 nominal cash flow = 40,000 × 1.04 = ₹41,600.

Value = 41,600 ÷ (0.0712 − 0.04) = 41,600 ÷ 0.0312 = ₹13,33,333 ✔ identical
"Assumptions made" — the part he actually asks for

Slide 14 ends with the words "Assumptions made". The equivalence holds only because:

  1. Selling price and all costs inflate at exactly the same rate (4%). If input costs rise 6% while you can only pass on 4%, real margins shrink and the real cash flow is not constant.
  2. Volume is constant — no real growth, so the only growth is inflation.
  3. There is no tax. This one matters a lot — see the trap below.
  4. Working capital and capex needs also scale with inflation.
Why we use NOMINAL in practice — the depreciation tax shieldReal and nominal give identical answers only in a frictionless, tax-free world. In reality, depreciation is charged on historical cost. Your machine cost ₹100 in 2020 and you depreciate ₹100 — not the ₹150 it would cost to replace it. So the depreciation tax shield is fixed in nominal rupees and its real value is eroded by inflation. There is no clean way to model that in a real-terms framework.

Same problem with debt: interest is contracted in nominal rupees. And your risk-free rate observed from G-Secs is a nominal rate, so building a real rate requires you to guess inflation anyway.

Answer to "In summary — use real or nominal?" (slide 17): use NOMINAL. Because market data (G-Sec yields, credit spreads, betas) is all nominal, and because tax shields on historical-cost depreciation are inherently nominal.

2.7 Rupee or dollar? Interest Rate Parity

Warm-up — WACC deck, slide 15

Exchange rate ₹80 per $. US interest rate 5% p.a. Indian interest rate 7% p.a. You have $100. Where will you invest?

The answer is "it makes no difference" — show why

Option 1 — stay in dollars: $100 × 1.05 = $105 after a year.

Option 2 — go to rupees: convert $100 → ₹8,000; invest at 7% → ₹8,560 after a year; convert back at the one-year forward rate.

Forward rate = Spot × (1 + r_INR)/(1 + r_USD) = 80 × 1.07/1.05 = ₹81.5238 / $ ₹8,560 ÷ 81.5238 = $105.00 ← identical

This is Interest Rate Parity (IRP): the currency with the higher interest rate must be expected to depreciate by exactly the interest differential.

Why IRP must holdIf it didn't, you'd have a money machine: borrow in the cheap currency, lend in the dear one, lock the exchange rate with a forward contract, and pocket a risk-free profit with zero of your own money. Arbitrageurs would do this at unlimited scale until the forward rate moved to kill the gap. IRP is enforced by arbitrage, which is why it is one of the tightest relationships in finance — much tighter than, say, purchasing power parity.

Deep point: the higher Indian rate is not free money. It is compensation for expected rupee depreciation. Which is exactly why (§3.8) a "cheap" 6% dollar loan is not cheap for an Indian company.
Worked example — WACC deck, slide 16 (ASL to an American investor)

An American investor wants to value ASL from §2.6b. US real discount rate 3%; US inflation 1%. Spot rate ₹80/$. What is ASL worth to him?

Try it, then open
The lazy (and correct) route

ASL is worth ₹13,33,333. At the spot rate of ₹80/$, that is $16,666.67. Done.

The route that proves it

US nominal rate = 1.03 × 1.01 − 1 = 4.03%. India nominal = 7.12%.

By IRP, the rupee must depreciate at 1.0712/1.0403 − 1 = 2.97% per year.

Rupee cash flows grow at 4%; the rupee loses 2.97% per year. So dollar cash flows grow at 1.04/1.0297 − 1 = 1.0% — exactly US inflation, which makes sense: the real cash flow is constant, so in dollar terms it should grow at dollar inflation.

Year-1 dollar cash flow = ₹41,600 ÷ (80 × 1.0297) = $504.99.

Value = 505 ÷ (0.0403 − 0.01) = 505 ÷ 0.0303 = $16,666.7 ✔
"Assumptions made in IRP"
  1. IRP holds continuously — free capital mobility, no capital controls, no transaction costs or taxes on cross-border flows.
  2. Expected future spot rate = today's forward rate (no risk premium on currency).
  3. The relevant risk (beta) of ASL is the same to both investors — i.e. markets are integrated.
The answer to slide 17: "Use $ or ₹ discount rate?"Whichever you like — as long as you are consistent. Rupee cash flows → rupee discount rate → rupee value → convert at spot. Dollar cash flows → dollar discount rate → dollar value. Both give the same answer if IRP holds. In practice, value in the currency the business actually earns in, then convert at spot — fewer forecasting steps, fewer places to err.

Answer — and the proof

Use 30% (the statutory/marginal rate).

Proof by the only test that matters — how much tax did the interest actually save?

If MJL had NO debt: taxable income = 20 + 10 = ₹30m → tax = 30 × 30% = ₹9m MJL actually pays: tax = ₹6m Tax saved by borrowing = ₹3m Interest paid = ₹10m Effective tax shield rate = 3 ÷ 10 = 30% ✔ = the statutory rate

Tax(unlevered) − Tax(levered) t* = ─────────────────────────────── Interest

For a profit-making company this always collapses to the marginal = statutory rate. The exception the book plants (Scenario 2)Under a progressive tax system (20% up to ₹25m taxable income, 30% above), MJL pays ₹4m (average 5%, marginal 20%), but as an unlevered firm it would have paid ~₹6.3m. · For historical financials → use the CASH tax rate (tax actually paid ÷ PBT).
· For projections → use the current STATUTORY tax rate.

3.4 What if there are several debt instruments at different rates?

Worked example — WACC deck slides 26–28 / Book Example 3.8 (XYZ Limited)

XYZ Limited has issued two types of debt: ₹100 million at 12% and ₹200 million at 14%. Projected EBIT = ₹100m. Tax rate 30%. Ke = 20%. g = 0. Book value of debt = market value of debt.

What is the value of XYZ? And which cost of debt do you use?

Solve, then compare all three legitimate methods
The anchor: value equity directly (FCFE method)
Interest = 12% × 100 + 14% × 200 = 12 + 28 = ₹40m PBT = 100 − 40 = ₹60m Tax @30% = ₹18m PAT = ₹42m Equity = 42 ÷ 0.20 = ₹210m ← this is the truth. All methods must hit it. Enterprise Value = 210 + 300 = ₹510m FCF = 100 × (1 − 0.3) = ₹70m
METHOD 3 — include BOTH debts in WACC
WACC = (210/510)×20% + (200/510)×14%×0.7 + (100/510)×12%×0.7 = 8.235% + 3.843% + 1.647% = 13.72% EV = 70 ÷ 0.1372 = ₹510m ✔ Equity = 510 − 300 = ₹210m ✔

Rule: if a company has issued several bonds at different pre-tax costs, use all of them — each as its own weighted term. Do not average the coupons naively.

METHOD 2 — deliberately IGNORE the ₹100m @12% debt

If a liability is not a source of capital, it must be treated as an operating item: its interest is subtracted before EBIT, and it is not subtracted from EV.

WACC = (210/410)×20% + (200/410)×14%×0.7 = 10.244% + 4.780% = 15.02% Adjusted EBIT = 100 − 12 (interest on the ignored debt) = ₹88m Adjusted FCF = 88 × 0.7 = ₹61.6m "EV" = 61.6 ÷ 0.1502 = ₹410m Equity = 410 − 200 (only the debt we counted) = ₹210m ✔
METHOD 1 — FCFE, ignore all debt

Equity = 42 ÷ 0.20 = ₹210m ✔

All three give ₹210m. Why?Because each is internally consistent about who the claimants are. Method 3 values the pie and hands out all three slices. Method 2 values a smaller pie (the 12% lender has been reclassified as a supplier, paid before EBIT) and hands out two slices. Method 1 values only the shareholder's slice. Different pies, same shareholder slice.

Which do you actually use? "Whichever is practically most convenient" — the book's own answer. Method 2 is convenient when the interest rate on a liability is not explicitly stated (see NIBL next).

Why this works — the one-sentence versionEvery liability must appear exactly once in your valuation — either as a financing item (in the discount rate, and deducted from EV at the end) or as an operating item (deducted before EBIT). AnalogyYou're valuing a restaurant. The rent can be handled two ways: (a) treat the landlord as a capital provider — capitalise the lease, add it to debt, put it in WACC, and subtract the lease liability from EV at the end; or (b) treat the landlord as a supplier — just deduct rent as an operating expense and forget about him. Both are correct. What is fatal is deducting the rent as an expense AND subtracting the lease liability at the end — you'd be paying the landlord twice.
Book only — the extra adjustment students always miss (p. 170)Under the gross-debt method (cash treated as an operating asset, no netting), interest income from cash is usually reported after EBIT in Indian statements. This is "an extra adjustment we need to make while using the gross-debt method" and it's the reason the book prefers the net-debt method. See §4.9.

3.6 Non-Interest-Bearing Liabilities (NIBL) — are they really free?

WACC deck, slides 24–25 He asks it as a question: "Are the non-interest-bearing liabilities really free?"

Slide 25 / Book pp. 102–103

A company buys raw materials on 1 year's credit and must pay ₹1,100 crores. Its cost of debt is 10%. Should we treat the trade payable as a source of funds while estimating the cost of debt (and capital)?

Answer

The credit is not free. If the company paid cash today, the supplier would happily accept about ₹1,000 crores. The ₹100 crores difference is the interest — it's simply buried inside the purchase price instead of being labelled "interest".

Implicit annual interest rate = 100 / 1000 = 10% — exactly the cost of debt.

Suppliers aren't charities. They fund the credit with their own borrowings and price it into the invoice.

Book only — the 3-month version with the annualisation (p. 102)Credit purchase of ₹10m for 3 months. The supplier would accept ₹9.7m in cash today. So the implicit interest is ₹0.3m on a loan of ₹9.7m for 3 months:
Implicit annualised cost = (1 + 0.3/9.7)⁴ − 1 = 12.96%
Note carefully: the base is 9.7, not 10 — you borrowed 9.7 and repay 10. And it's compounded 4 times because the cycle repeats quarterly. Getting the base or the power wrong is a classic slip.
So what do you actually DO with NIBL? Two permitted methods:
MethodConsequences you must honour
1Treat NIBL as a source of capitalShow debt as ₹9.7m (not ₹10m). Restate COGS to ₹9.7m (not ₹10m). Use 12.96% as that tranche's pre-tax cost of debt. Both EBIT and FCF are based on the revised COGS. And subtract ₹9.7m from EV at the end.
2Ignore NIBL in the cost of capital recommendedLeave COGS at ₹10m (the accountant already put the ₹0.3m implicit interest inside COGS as an operating expense — so the condition is automatically satisfied). Don't subtract NIBL from EV. Just net NIBL off in working capital.
Why Method 2 wins in practiceMethod 1 requires you to ring up every single supplier and ask what discount they'd give for immediate cash. That's not a real-world procedure. Method 2 requires nothing — because the accountant has already embedded the implicit interest in COGS, liability consistency holds automatically. This is why in Part 6 operating working capital is computed net of NIBL, and why NIBL never appears in the WACC weights.
Book only — deferred sales-tax loans (p. 103)Many Indian annual reports show a deferred sales-tax loan: the company collects sales tax from customers but pays the state government only after several years. Interest-free on paper. So what's the implicit cost?

The state grants this to attract industry. In exchange, the company builds its factory where the state wants, not where raw materials, labour and logistics are cheapest. The extra transport, higher wages, and cost of developing a township around the plant are the implicit interest. Since you can't measure that, adopt Method 2: ignore it in the cost of capital.

This is a lovely "explain the implicit cost" question — remember the mechanism, not just the label.

4.1 CAPM from scratch

E(rᵢ) = R_f + βᵢ × [ E(r_m) − R_f ] ↑ ↑ ↑ risk-free how much the price of rate market one unit of risk you market risk carry (the MRP)
Why the equation has this shape — build it in three moves Move 1. If you take no risk, you should earn the risk-free rate Rf. So Rf is the floor. Everything else is a premium on top.

Move 2. From §0.8, only systematic risk earns a premium. Suppose the market charges λ per unit of systematic risk. Then E(rᵢ) = Rf + λ × (units of systematic risk in stock i).

Move 3. Calibrate λ using the market portfolio itself, which by definition carries exactly one unit of market risk (β = 1). Plug it in: E(rm) = Rf + λ × 1, so λ = E(rm) − Rf. That's the market risk premium. Substitute back and you have CAPM.

So CAPM says exactly one thing: your required return = the risk-free floor + (your dose of market risk) × (the market price of market risk). Nothing more mysterious than that. And notice what's absent: the stock's own total volatility never appears, because the diversifiable part isn't paid for.

4.2 Estimating the risk-free rate

What makes an asset risk-free? No default risk and no reinvestment risk. In India: Government of India securities (G-Secs).

Problem 1 — which maturity? "CAPM is a one-period model"

Book only (p. 111) — the theoretical framingCAPM is a one-period model. The risk-free rate should therefore match the investor's investment horizon: a 1-year horizon → 1-year rate; a 5-year horizon → 5-year rate. But we project cash flows to infinity, and each cash flow should strictly be discounted at its own spot rate. So what horizon do we use? That's the practical problem the next two examples resolve.
Worked example — WACC deck slide 40 / Book Example 3.12

A risk-free asset pays 100, 100, 200 in years 1, 2, 3. Today's spot rates are 5%, 6%, 7%. Find (a) the value and (b) the single "yield" that reproduces it.

Solve
Value = 100/1.05 + 100/1.06² + 200/1.07³ = 95.24 + 89.00 + 163.26 = 347.50 Now solve for the single rate r: 347.50 = 100/(1+r) + 100/(1+r)² + 200/(1+r)³ ⇒ r = 6.52%

So two equivalent routes when the yield curve isn't flat:

  1. Discount each year's cash flow at that year's spot rate, then add up.
  2. Discount every cash flow at a single yield (6.52%) and add up.
The problem with route 2 — and a book typo to know aboutThe yield depends on the pattern of cash flows, not just on the spot rates. Change the timing and the yield changes even though the spot curve is unchanged. That's why you can't just look up "the" yield.

Note: the book prints this present value as 374.50 on pp. 111–112 — a digit transposition. 347.50 is correct (and is what the slide shows), and it's the number consistent with r = 6.52%. Use 347.50.

Corollary the book adds (p. 112): strictly, the correct Rf for a high-growth company (cash flows even further out) differs from that for a mature company. In practice, if the curve is flat at the long end, one number serves.
Book only — the ZCYC and the numbersGet the Zero Coupon Yield Curve (ZCYC) from the NSE. On 2 May 2012 it was upward-sloping and almost flat beyond 20 years, with a 20-year spot rate of 9.12% versus a 1-year rate of about 7.81%. The book adopts 9.12%.

Sanity checks the book runs: valuing 100 years of cash flows growing at 5% implies a yield of 9.06%; 50 years implies 9.05% — so 9.12% is a good approximation. Note also that the 10-year G-Sec coupon yield was 8.69%, different from the 10-year spot rate of 8.81%, because coupon-bearing bonds have front-loaded cash flows. Spot ≠ yield — don't grab the headline G-Sec yield off a newspaper.

Problem 2 — real or nominal? Which currency?

G-Sec yields are nominal and rupee-denominated. So they discount nominal rupee cash flows. (Point 4 again.)

Problem 3 — are these rates really risk-free?

Book only (p. 114) + WACC deck slide 39 — the sovereign-risk adjustmentIndia's sovereign rating was BBB (the lowest investment grade) as of early 2012; on 25 April 2012 S&P lowered the outlook to negative, citing high fiscal deficit and high debt burden. A BBB sovereign is not default-free.

So the "true" risk-free rate should answer a counterfactual: what would the 20-year spot rate be if India were rated AAA? Estimate it from the Credit Default Swap (CDS) spread — around 100 bps for AAA-rated Indian PSUs at the time.
True R_f ≈ 9.12% − 1.00% = 8.12%
But — and this is the examinable subtlety — the book then says: "in this book, I use 9.12% as the risk-free rate. If one uses 8.12%, then one has to increase the market risk premium by around 1%. I do not do this adjustment."

Why the two cancel: MRP = E(rm) − Rf. Lower Rf by 1% and the MRP mechanically rises by 1%. Since Ke = Rf + β×MRP, for β ≈ 1 the answer is unchanged. It only matters for β far from 1. Consistency again beats precision.

The WACC deck keeps this alive with a slide on India's 5-year CDS (worldgovernmentbonds.com) and a slide showing the risk-free rate as at 17 July 2026.

4.3 Estimating the Market Risk Premium

MRP = E(r_m) − R_f ← note E( ). It is the EXPECTED premium, not the realised one.

4.3a What is the "market portfolio"? And what proxy is acceptable?

Book only (p. 115) — theory that gets asked as a short noteIn CAPM equilibrium, all investors hold the same risky portfolio. If everyone holds the same one, it must contain every capital asset in the world: shares, bonds, debentures, currency, gold, real estate, silver, paintings, human capital. And it must be value-weighted.

"To the best of my knowledge, nobody has ever attempted to create such a market portfolio." So we use proxies — and in practice proxies contain only stocks. This gap is the seed of Roll's critique (§4.12).
Four properties a market proxy must have (book pp. 116–117)
  1. Broad-based. A narrow index won't capture the mood of the whole capital market.
  2. Value-weighted by market capitalisation — this is what the CAPM equation assumes.
  3. Liquid stocks only. Illiquid stocks don't respond fully to macro news on the day, which distorts both the index and every beta computed against it.
  4. Returns must include dividends, not just capital gains. Most indices exclude dividends → add the average dividend yield back to the estimated MRP.
Why the dividend point matters — the extreme illustrationSuppose every stock in an index has a 1% dividend yield and they all go ex-dividend on the same day. The index falls by 1% that day even though shareholders lost nothing — they received the cash. So a price index systematically understates true returns by roughly the dividend yield. Ignore it and you understate the MRP by ~1% and hence understate every cost of equity you compute.
Book only — the Sensex / Nifty / BSE 500 evidence (Tables 3.4–3.6)The free-float problem. BSE and NSE weight most indices by free-float-adjusted market cap, applying a factor based on promoter holding (e.g. ITC 0.7, Reliance 0.55, HDFC Bank 1.0, ONGC 0.45). But CAPM's market portfolio is a simple market-cap-weighted index, not a free-float one. This is a genuine theoretical mismatch — a good "critique the practice" answer.

Liquidity (Table 3.5). An average Sensex stock is 8× larger and trades 7× more frequently than an average BSE 500 stock. Around 25% of BSE 500 stocks report fewer than 250 transactions a day.

Correlation (Table 3.6). Sensex, Nifty, BSE 100/200/500 monthly returns are correlated ≥ 0.97 with each other. So the narrow indices are excellent proxies for the broad ones anyway.

Conclusion: use Sensex or Nifty. Liquid, correlated with everything else, and Sensex has data back to 1979 — the longest series in India, which matters for statistical precision.

Footnote worth stealing: BSE 500 averaged 1.46% monthly vs Sensex 1.30% (2001–2012), because small caps carry Fama–French size risk. Using BSE 500 would give a higher MRP.

Issue 1 — what estimation period?

Tension between two goals: long enough to average out booms and busts and include several business cycles, vs short enough that the market's character hasn't changed. Issue 2 — survivorship bias

Why 25% is also a meaningful number — the binomial tree argument

Treat the stock as a binomial process: each year it either doubles (p = 0.5) or halves (p = 0.5). From ₹100 on 1.1.2013, two years out gives:

The rule that falls outIf you believe the future will look like the particular path the past took → use the GEOMETRIC mean (it is a backward-looking measure of realised average return).

If you believe the future can take any of the possible paths with their probabilities → use the ARITHMETIC mean (it is a forward-looking measure of expected return).

CAPM needs an expected return. Under a pure random walk, arithmetic is the theoretically correct unbiased estimator (Blume, 1974). "Most people prefer to use arithmetic average while predicting future returns."
So why does the book finally choose geometric (7.13%)?

Because of mean reversion. If stock prices overreact and then revert, the middle paths (up-then-down, down-then-up) are more likely than the extreme ones, and the arithmetic mean overstates expected return.

The book tests this (Figure 3.10, monthly Sensex 2002–2012): under a random walk, 2-year variance should be about 2× the 1-year variance. In fact the 2-year variance is only about half of that — clear evidence that Sensex returns mean-revert.

Verdict (p. 125): "Going by the evidence presented in Figure 3.10, I will have more trust on the geometric mean figure. So I shall use 7.13% as the market risk premium in the rest of this book. If you believe that stock prices will not mean revert in the future and that they will follow a random walk process, then by all means use the 10.55% figure."

Counter-view to mention for full marks: Prof. Ian Cooper (LBS, 1996) argues that even with mean reversion the geometric mean is a downward-biased estimator, so the true MRP is higher than the geometric mean suggests.

4.3c Method 2 — the implied risk premium (Damodaran's method)

Book only (pp. 123–125) — powerful and frequently asked

Logic: stop looking backwards. Assume the market has correctly priced the index today, and reverse-engineer the MRP that makes today's index level consistent with expected future cash flows. It's an IRR calculation on the index.

Procedure:

  1. Take the index level today. Sensex closed at 16,420 on 10 May 2012.
  2. Build a mimicking portfolio of ₹16,420 with each stock at its index weight. (Bajaj Auto weight 1.58% → ₹259.44 invested → at ₹1,677.90 per share that's 0.155 shares → dividend ₹6.17, FCFE ₹10.96.)
  3. Aggregate: total dividend ₹248.68; total FCFE ₹410.23. Use FCFE (it's a better proxy for distributable cash than dividends).
  4. Project FCFE growth. The book uses projected GDP growth adjusted for inflation: 6.9% (2011-12) rising to 7% and declining to ~5% by 2022; add inflation (7% falling to ~5%) → nominal growth 12%, 12%, 12%, 11.5%, 11.5%, 11%, 11%, 10.5%, 10.5%, 10%, 10%.

4.4 Estimating beta — computing the returns

Covariance(Rᵢ, R_m) σ_i,m β = ────────────────────── = ────── Variance(R_m) σ²_m Equivalently: β is the SLOPE of the regression of stock returns on index returns.
Slide 44–45 — "How to compute returns" is a whole slide, so expect a question a) Arithmetic or logarithmic returns?
Arithmetic: R = (P₁ − P₀)/P₀ Logarithmic: R = ln(P₁/P₀)
Log returns are additive over time and better behaved statistically (closer to normal), so they're preferred for daily data. Over short intervals the two are nearly identical; over long intervals they diverge and arithmetic is what an investor actually earns.

b) Closing price or adjusted price? → ADJUSTED price. Always.

c) Adjustments needed for: dividends, bonus issues, stock splits, and rights issues.
Why unadjusted prices destroy a beta estimateA 1:1 bonus issue halves the share price overnight. Raw data records a −50% return on the ex-date. That's not a return — the shareholder now owns twice as many shares and is exactly as rich as before. Feed that −50% into a regression against an index that moved 0% and you have injected an enormous, meaningless outlier. Same with a 5:1 split (an apparent −80%) and, less dramatically, with dividends (price drops by roughly the dividend on the ex-date).

Rule: reconstruct the total return to a shareholder, not the change in the quoted price.
Rights issue — WACC deck slide 46 (Reliance Industries, 2021)

RIL made a rights issue in the ratio 1:15 at ₹1,257. The stock price on the record date was ₹1,435.

What return should you record on the ex-rights date?

A rights issue is a discounted issue to existing shareholders, so the share price must fall mechanically — but shareholders aren't poorer, because they received a valuable right.

Theoretical ex-rights price (TERP) = (15 × 1,435 + 1 × 1,257) / 16 = (21,525 + 1,257) / 16 = 22,782 / 16 = ₹1,423.88 Value of the right per existing share = 1,435 − 1,423.88 = ₹11.13 Adjustment factor = 1,423.88 / 1,435 = 0.99225

So the "drop" from ₹1,435 to ₹1,423.88 is a 0% true return, not a −0.78% return. Multiply all pre-event prices by 0.99225 to make the series comparable — exactly the same logic as adjusting for bonuses and splits.

Daily, weekly, or monthly returns?
Book only (pp. 135–136) — with the numbers The trade-off: daily data gives ~1,300 observations over 5 years (statistical power) but suffers from two problems:

4.6 Beta and operating risk (business risk)

Why cyclicality and fixed costs drive beta — derived, not assertedThe book derives it (pp. 126–127) and it's elegant:
PV(Assets) = PV(Cash Flows) = PV(Revenue) − PV(Fixed costs) − PV(Variable costs)
A company is a long position in revenues and a short position in costs. Beta is additive across a portfolio, so:
β_Assets = β_Rev × PV(Rev)/PV(Assets) − β_Fixed × PV(Fixed)/PV(Assets) − β_Var × PV(Var)/PV(Assets)
Now two reasonable assumptions: β of fixed costs = 0 (they don't move with the economy — that's what makes them fixed), and if the firm can pass input-price rises through, βRevenue ≈ βVariable costs. Substituting and simplifying:
⎛ PV(Fixed Costs) ⎞ β_Assets = β_Revenue × ⎜ 1 + ─────────────── ⎟ … 3(9) ⎝ PV(Assets) ⎠
Read the result: the higher the proportion of fixed costs, the higher the asset beta. Operating leverage amplifies market risk exactly the way financial leverage does. Fixed costs are, in effect, internal debt.
Four types of company with high operating risk (book p. 128)
  1. Cyclical companies — revenue is highly volatile; β usually > 1. (Steel, autos, cement, airlines.)
  2. Young companies — fixed costs are a higher share of total costs than for established peers; they struggle in downturns (and outperform in booms).
  3. Companies selling discretionary products — customers can simply go without in a recession. (Luxury, travel, jewellery.)
  4. Companies with low brand loyalty — customers defect the moment budgets tighten.
The one-question test he gives you"How will this company perform during downtimes? During boom times?" If the answer is "not much affected by the state of the economy," operating risk (and beta) is low. Otherwise it's high.

Toothpaste vs automobiles: when GDP falls 6%, toothpaste revenue doesn't fall 6% — people keep brushing. Car revenue does, and worse, because purchases are postponable and inventories pile up. Toothpaste β low; auto β high.

4.7 Beta and financial leverage — the Hamada equation

Derive it — you will be asked toStart from MM with taxes (Chapter 2 of the book):
V_L = V_U + D × t … 3(10)
Also VL = D + EL and VU = EU. Substituting:
D + E_L = E_U + D×t ⇒ E_L = E_U − D×(1 − t)
Read that in English: equity in a levered company = a long position in the equity of the otherwise-identical unlevered company plus a short position in D×(1−t) of riskless-ish debt. Levered equity is a geared claim.

Betas are value-weighted, so β of EL = β of [EU − D(1−t)]:
β_L = β_U × (E_U/E_L) − β_D × D(1−t)/E_L = β_U × [1 + (D/E)(1−t)] − β_D × (D/E)(1−t) … 3(11) Hamada equation
Most analysts assume βD = 0 (debt is nearly riskless), giving the form you'll actually use:
β_L = β_U × [1 + (D/E) × (1 − t)] … 3(12)
And with growth / continuous rebalancing to a target debt ratio (Appendix to Ch. 2), the tax term drops out:
β_L = β_U × (1 + D/E) … 3(12a)
Book only — two subtleties that make good short answers 1. When does levering actually raise beta? Differentiate 3(11) w.r.t. D/E: the relationship is positive only if
β_U × (1 − t) − β_D × (1 − t) > 0 ⇒ β_U > β_D
which is always true (assets are riskier than the debt secured on them). So beta always rises with leverage. Good.

2. Why does the (1−t) term disappear under rebalancing? Equation 3(12) assumes the rupee value of debt stays constant forever, so the tax shield is a safe, debt-like stream discounted at Kd. If instead the company rebalances to keep D/E constant, the debt level grows with firm value, so the tax shield is as risky as the assets, discounted at KU — and the (1−t) drops out. Which formula to use is a statement about the company's financing policy.

3. With preference capital in the structure:
Only equity & preference: β_E = β_U + (β_U − β_P) × (P/E) … 3(14) Debt, equity & preference: β_L = β_U + (β_U−β_D)(D/E)(1−t) + (β_U−β_P)(P/E) … 3(16) Same, but rebalancing: β_L = β_U + (β_U−β_D)(D/E) + (β_U−β_P)(P/E) … 3(16a)
Note there's no (1−t) on the preference term in any version — preference dividends aren't tax-deductible, so there's no shield. Same reason as §3.9.

4.8 The unlever → average → relever procedure

This is the single most-performed calculation in valuation practice, and the book's Appendix E makes it step 3 of the whole process.

Why bother? Why not just regress the company's own beta?Three reasons, all in the book:
  1. Regression beta is noisy. One stock, one regression, wide standard error. Averaging across 8–10 peers cancels the noise. "There is less noise associated with the estimation of the sector beta and hence this is a much more reliable figure."
  2. The company's own history may be unusable — recent IPO, merger, demerger, or a capital-structure change (Tata Motors 1998).
  3. The company may not be listed at all. Peers are the only route.
The logic of the three steps: peers in the same industry share business risk but have different debt levels. Unlevering strips out each peer's financing risk to isolate the common business risk; you average that; then you add back your company's financing risk.
STEP 1 — UNLEVER each comparable β_L β_U = ─────────────────── 1 + (D/E)(1 − t) STEP 2 — AVERAGE (use the MEDIAN — it is robust to outliers) β_U(sector) = median of the peers' β_U STEP 3 — RELEVER at YOUR company's capital structure β_L = β_U(sector) × [1 + (D/E)_yours × (1 − t)]

4.9 Net debt vs gross debt — and why net wins

Book only (Appendix D, p. 170) — an "extra depth" section you specifically asked about

When unlevering, should D/E be gross debt or net debt (debt − cash)? Table 3.12 shows both.

The test the book runs. Take ASL Limited and compare it with KLM Limited — a company with no debt and no cash, i.e. pure business risk, so KLM's observed beta is the true unlevered beta.

  • Net-debt method: ASL's unlevered beta comes out at 0.50 — exactly KLM's beta. ✔ Theoretically consistent.
  • Gross-debt method: for a company with $50m of cash and no debt, you get 0.4762 from one route and 0.4888 from another. ✘ Inconsistent with itself.

Why net debt is right, intuitively: cash is negative debt. A company with ₹100 of debt and ₹100 of cash has no net borrowing and therefore no financial risk. Cash also has a beta near zero, so holding it dilutes the equity beta downward — netting removes that distortion and leaves pure business risk.

Practical extra cost of the gross-debt method: interest income is reported after EBIT, so you must add it back — "this is an extra adjustment we need to make while using the gross-debt method."

The sentence to write in the exam"The key point to remember is that we must maintain risk consistency while doing valuation. Once we ensure that the free cash flow is consistently computed, it really does not matter whether we use the net debt method or the gross debt method." The differences are small and may be ignored in most practical applications — but the net-debt method is theoretically cleaner and needs fewer adjustments.

4.11 Adjustments for illiquidity and country risk

Book only (pp. 137–138) — the country-risk answer is counter-intuitive and highly examinable
Illiquidity premium

Investors in illiquid stocks bear the risk of not being able to sell when they want. This cannot be diversified away, because a portfolio of illiquid stocks is itself illiquid. Custom is to add 1–3 percentage points to Ke for illiquid stocks.

But the book prefers a cleaner route: instead of an arbitrary add-on, use the Fama–French multi-factor approach — estimate a liquidity beta and multiply it by a liquidity risk premium, treating illiquidity as a fourth factor. Same idea, but disciplined by data instead of judgement.

Country risk premium — "never add one"

Example 3.15. An American investor wants to buy Greek stocks. CAPM says the required return on a particular Greek company is 11%. He's worried about Greece's economy. Should he add a country risk premium?

Answer — and this is the one students get wrong

No. Never add an arbitrary country risk premium. Here's the argument in three steps:

  1. Split country risk into two parts. Part of it is global/systematic — Greece's collapse in 2012 suppressed stock markets everywhere; even Indian investors turned cautious. That part is already priced into the market risk premium, because when you estimate the MRP from historical index returns, all such episodes are already in the data. Adding it again is double counting.
  2. The remaining part is Greece-specific — and a Greece-specific risk is, by construction, diversifiable for an internationally diversified investor. International investors hold stocks all over the world; they don't care about a country-specific shock any more than about a single-company shock.
  3. The marginal investor sets the price. Even if our American investor personally demands a premium, he will not get it: other investors, who are internationally diversified and are not bearing that risk at the margin, will happily buy at the lower required return and bid the price up. "Such risks will not be priced in a global competitive capital market."
Rule: "If the country risk is really non-diversifiable, it is already priced in the market anyway — the market risk premium includes it. So do not inflate this figure by adding arbitrary premiums."

For balance (the book flags it): Damodaran takes the opposite view and does add explicit country risk premiums — his India "Total Equity Risk Premium" of 7.08% on slide 42 is built that way. Mentioning both positions is a full-marks answer. Also note the implicit assumption in Mohanty's argument: fully integrated global capital markets. Where capital controls bind (footnote 44 notes the rupee is not fully convertible on capital account and Indian investors largely cannot invest abroad), the domestic marginal investor is not globally diversified, which weakens the case.

4.12 Can we take CAPM seriously? APT and multi-factor models

Book only (pp. 138–139) — the critique section, near-certain short-note material
Roll's critique (Roll 1977; Roll & Ross 1994)

If the true market portfolio is mean-variance efficient, the return–beta relationship is exact. If it is not efficient, the relationship is not exact. But the true market portfolio (all capital assets in the world, including human capital) is unobservable, so we substitute Sensex or Nifty.

The bite: if the proxy index is inefficient, then "no sense can be made of either the beta that we get from the regression or the expected return that we get by plugging the beta into the CAPM equation." Worse, if the index is ex-ante inefficient, any variable correlated with expected returns will appear to have explanatory power — so CAPM is arguably not testable at all.

Kandel & Stambaugh (1995) sharpen it: expected returns can show essentially no correlation with betas computed against an index that is arbitrarily close to efficient — and a nearly perfect linear relation with betas computed against a grossly inefficient index. The empirical result tells you about the index, not about CAPM.

Fama & French (1992)

Found the relation between realised stock returns and beta is flat in the USA — high-beta and low-beta stocks earned similar returns. Instead, size (small minus big) and value (high minus low book-to-market) explained returns. This launched the 3-factor model, now standard in industry.

The defence

Da, Guo & Jagannathan (2012) argue we can still use CAPM to estimate the cost of equity for projects, even given the cross-sectional evidence against it — the cross-sectional tests and the capital-budgeting application are different questions.

The two substitutes
  • Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT): return is driven by several unnamed systematic factors. Problem: you must estimate the factor premiums, and the theory doesn't tell you what the factors are.
  • Fama–French multi-factor models: named factors (market, size, value, sometimes momentum and liquidity). Popular in industry, data easily available — for India, from the IIM-Ahmedabad Fama-French-Momentum data library (slide 43).

"These two models are however rarely used in practice" for cost-of-capital work — analysts overwhelmingly still use CAPM. Know why it's wrong and use it anyway is the honest professional position, and saying exactly that scores well.

4.13 Cost of warrants and convertibles

Slide 53 — he sets it up as a two-part puzzle. Do the puzzle first. Warm-up (portfolio betas):
  1. A stock has β = 1.2. You invest 50% in the stock and 50% in risk-free assets. Portfolio beta?
  2. You borrow 50% and invest 150% in the stock. Portfolio beta?
Answers
1) β = 0.5 × 1.2 + 0.5 × 0 = 0.60 2) β = 1.5 × 1.2 − 0.5 × 0 = 1.80

Beta is a simple value-weighted average, and borrowing at the risk-free rate contributes a negative weight at β = 0. Leverage scales beta linearly.

Why that warm-up unlocks warrantsA warrant (or a call option) is the right, not the obligation, to buy a share at a fixed price. Black–Scholes shows that a call is economically identical to a leveraged position in the stock: hold N(d₁) shares, funded partly by borrowing. The delta N(d₁) tells you how many shares the option behaves like.

Since a warrant is a levered stock position, and levered positions scale beta (the warm-up), the warrant's beta must be the stock's beta scaled by its leverage:
S β_warrant = N(d₁) × ─────── × β_stock … 3(22) Warrant value Then: K_warrant = R_f + β_warrant × MRP
Because S is always much bigger than the warrant's price, βwarrant ≫ βstock — warrants are enormously risky and therefore have a very high cost of capital.

The three-step procedure to write down:
  1. Estimate the beta of the underlying stock.
  2. Convert to the beta of the call using N(d₁) × S/Call × βstock.
  3. Plug into CAPM to get the cost of the warrant.
Convertible debentures: split into a straight bond + a warrant, cost each piece separately, and weight them. (Partially convertible debentures: only part of the face value converts; the rest stays a bond.)

Part 5 · Assembling the WACC

5.1 The formula, carefully

E D P WACC = ─── × K_e + ─── × K_d × (1−t) + ─── × K_p … 3(1) V V V where V = E + D + P (ALL at MARKET values) E = market value of equity K_e from CAPM D = market value of debt K_d = pre-tax (YTM) P = market value of preference K_p — NO (1−t) With several debt tranches, each gets its own term: E D₁ D₂ WACC = ─── K_e + ─── K_d₁(1−t) + ─── K_d₂(1−t) + … V V V
Why (1−t) sits on debt but not on equity or preferenceInterest is a tax-deductible expense: the taxman refunds t × interest. So the company's real cost of ₹100 of 10% debt is ₹10 − ₹3 = ₹7 at a 30% tax rate → 7%.

Dividends — equity or preference — are paid out of post-tax profit. No deduction, no shield, no (1−t).

The second half of the reason (and the exam-relevant half): FCF is defined as EBIT × (1−t) — i.e. computed as if the company had no debt, so it never enjoys the interest tax shield. The shield has to enter the valuation somewhere, and this is where: through the (1−t) in the WACC. That's the whole architecture — capture the tax benefit of debt once, in the discount rate, and never in the cash flow. Deduct interest from FCF and use the after-tax Kd and you've counted the shield twice.

The seven-step routine

  1. List every liability and split interest-bearing vs non-interest-bearing. Decide which are sources of capital. Write the decision down — you must honour it at the end.
  2. Cost of debt — pecking order (§3.1). Convert any foreign-currency loan to a rupee-equivalent rate (§3.8).
  3. Tax rate — marginal/statutory for the shield (§3.3). Multiply: Kd(1−t).
  4. Cost of equity — CAPM. Rf = longest spot rate; MRP ≈ 7%; β from peers, unlevered → median → relevered at your D/E.
  5. Market values — market cap for E; book debt as a proxy for D (or PV of coupons); PV of lease rentals if capitalising leases; net debt if cash is non-operating.
  6. Weight and add.
  7. Sanity-check. WACC must lie between Kd(1−t) and Ke. If it doesn't, you have an arithmetic error. It should sit closer to Ke for a typical low-debt Indian company.
Five ways students lose marks on the WACC line itself
  1. Using book weights (Point 3).
  2. Using the coupon rate as Kd (Point 1).
  3. Applying (1−t) to Ke or to Kp. Never.
  4. Forgetting that E must be market cap, not book equity — for an unlisted firm, impute it (§4.10).
  5. Building WACC with net debt but then computing FCF with interest income inside EBIT (§3.10).

5.3 Appendix E — "Steps involved in computing cost of capital, in a nutshell"

Book only (pp. 170–171) — the author's own checklist. Use it as your exam answer skeleton. 1. Identify sources of capital. Equity is always there. Debt for most. Preference in a few. Split debt into interest-bearing and other; ignore the "other" while computing the cost of capital. Find out whether the company uses operating leases to finance assets — if the amount is insignificant, ignore it; otherwise treat it as a source of fund.

2. Find the cost of debt. Follow the pecking order; most likely you'll use the credit-rating method. Get the rating of the company's bonds; get the default spread for that rating from www.fimmda.org; get the risk-free rate from www.nseindia.com. If it's a foreign currency loan, find the rupee-equivalent cost (assuming your cash flows are in rupees). Compute the tax rate using Equation 3(4) and then the after-tax cost of debt.

3. Find the cost of equity. Identify comparable companies; compute their unlevered betas. (Beta values are on websites, but "it is advisable to compute it yourself so that you have more control over it.") Take the average/median unlevered beta for the sector, then lever it at the debt ratio of your company. Get the market risk premium (the most recent quarterly figure is on the author's website) and compute Ke. If the stock is not listed and no comparable company is listed in the exchange, find the beta of similar assets listed in other exchanges.

4. Find the market value of assets. Listed → use market capitalisation for equity. Not listed → impute the value using the multiplier method (e.g. sector average P/E × the private company's net income). For debt: use book value from the balance sheet; if you know the exact redemption schedule, compute the market value by discounting the coupons at the cost of debt. If treating operating lease as a source of fund, add the PV of operating lease rentals to book debt. If cash is treated as a non-operating asset, compute net debt.

5. Compute the WACC using Equation 3(1).

6.1 Four starting problems

Cash-flow deck, slide 3

(a) To calendarize or not?

Calendarizing = restating a company's financials onto a common year-end so peers can be compared. Indian companies mostly close 31 March; US companies vary (Walmart 31 Jan, Apple late September).

Why it mattersComparing an FY-March-2023 Indian company with an FY-January-2023 US peer means comparing different economic periods. If a shock hit in December, it's in one set of numbers and not the other. Calendarize when you're comparing across companies (comps, multiples, sector betas). It matters less when you're valuing a single company on its own terms — and calendarizing introduces estimation error of its own, so it isn't free.

Value P starting from standalone financials; then from consolidated financials. What are the differences, and which method is better?

Full answer
Route 1 — start from STANDALONE financials (sum of the parts)

P's standalone statements show only P's own operations, plus its investments in S and M carried as assets.

  1. Forecast FCF from P's own operations only and discount at P's own WACC → value of P's core business.
  2. Add the market value of the stakes, treating them as non-operating assets:
    80% × ₹300m (S) = ₹240m 20% × ₹400m (M) = ₹ 80m ───────── Investments ₹320m
  3. Add the ₹320m to the PV of P's own FCF, less P's own debt → equity value of P.
Route 2 — start from CONSOLIDATED financials
  1. Consolidated statements include 100% of S (assets ₹100m, income ₹30m) even though P owns only 80%. M is not consolidated — it's equity-accounted, so consolidated income includes only 20% × ₹40m = ₹8m as "share of profit of associate", and the balance sheet carries the investment.
  2. Value the whole consolidated entity → consolidated enterprise value.
  3. Subtract consolidated debt.
  4. Subtract the Non-Controlling Interest at MARKET value: 20% × ₹300m = ₹60m. (Book NCI would be 20% × ₹100m = ₹20m — wrong, same market-vs-book argument as Point 3.)
  5. Add the market value of M: 20% × ₹400m = ₹80m — because M's cash flows were never in the consolidated FCF, only an equity-accounted profit share which isn't cash.
Differences
  • Consolidated revenue and EBITDA are overstated relative to P's true economic claim, because 100% of S is included but P owns only 80%. Ratios and multiples computed on consolidated figures need the NCI deduction to be meaningful.
  • Consolidated statements blend risk profiles. If S is in a different industry, one blended WACC is wrong for both.
  • M's economics are almost invisible in the consolidated numbers (only ₹8m of non-cash profit share), so M must be valued separately in either route.
Which is better?
The judgementIf P, S and M are in the same business with similar risk and growth → consolidated is simpler and fine; one WACC serves.

If they are in different businesses → standalone + sum-of-the-parts is better, because each business needs its own cost of capital (different betas), its own growth rate, and its own margins. Blending a low-beta FMCG subsidiary with a high-beta steel parent into one WACC gives a number that is wrong for both.

The universal rule: whichever route you pick, the FCF you discount and the claims you subtract must describe the same entity. Consolidated FCF → subtract consolidated debt and NCI. Standalone FCF → add investments at market value. That's liability consistency wearing a different hat.

6.4 Revenue — the top line

Slide 13 — "Sales or Net Sales?" and the agency questionThe important question is whether the company is a principal (it owns the goods and bears the risk → book gross revenue) or an agent (it just facilitates → book only the commission).

Oyo Rooms used to charge 22% commission from hotel partners. Should Oyo's revenue be the whole room tariff, or just the 22%? If Oyo doesn't own the room, doesn't set the price, and doesn't bear inventory risk, it's an agent → revenue is the commission.

Why it matters enormously: gross-vs-net booking changes revenue by 5×, changes every margin percentage, and destroys any EV/Sales comparison. IndiGo (InterGlobe Aviation) is the other example on the slide — passenger revenue vs ancillary revenue vs pass-through statutory charges.

Also: line items differ in service companies — no raw material, no inventory; the big cost is employee benefits; "capex" may be intangible.
Slide 14 — you may need to RESTATE previous years"We need a base number to forecast future sales."

Walmart International revenue fell from $121.4bn (FY2021) to $101bn (FY2022). A 17% collapse? No — Walmart sold Asda (UK) and Seiyu (Japan) during the year.

If you naively fit a growth rate to that series you'd forecast a shrinking business. Restate the prior years to exclude divested operations so the base is comparable. Same for large acquisitions, which flatter growth. Always ask: is this growth organic?

Why "ignore" for non-recurring items — and why that word is exactDCF values a perpetual stream. A one-off gain contributes essentially nothing to a perpetuity but, if you leave it in the base year, it inflates every future year's forecast and therefore the terminal value. The four classification examples he gives — think before opening
1. Income from credit card operations by a retail firm

Recurring? Yes. Part of main business? This is the judgement call.

If it's a store card that drives footfall and loyalty and is inseparable from the retail proposition → operating. It's a customer-acquisition tool that happens to earn interest.

If the firm runs a full consumer-finance arm lending to non-customers, with its own balance sheet and its own funding, that's a different business with a different risk profile and a different cost of capital — value it separately (sum-of-the-parts) and treat it as non-operating for the retail valuation.

Say both and give the deciding test: would the retail business still work if you shut the card down? If yes, it's separable.

2. A drug retailer gets revenue from selling containers

Recurring? Yes. Part of main business? Yes — you can't dispense medicine without packaging it; the revenue arises from the very act of retailing drugs. → Operating income. Forecast it as a percentage of pharmacy revenue.

3. Investment income — trade vs non-trade

The distinction is the whole point of the example.

Trade investments — strategic stakes in suppliers, distributors, or joint ventures that support the core business (an auto maker's stake in a component supplier). Recurring, and connected to the main business → arguably operating, or at minimum valued as an integral strategic asset.

Non-trade investments — mutual funds, bonds, unrelated equities, i.e. the treasury parking surplus cash. → Non-operating. Strip the income out of EBIT and add the market value of the portfolio at the end.

This is the same question as "is cash operating?" from §3.10, applied to securities.

4. Gain on sale of assets

Recurring? No. → Ignore (both columns of the bottom row say ignore, so you don't even need the second question).

It's an accounting artefact of the difference between book value and sale price. It will not repeat, so it must not be capitalised.

The one exception worth naming: a company whose business model is buying and selling assets — a real-estate developer, an airline that runs sale-and-leaseback as a routine financing strategy. Then the gains are recurring and are the main business → operating. Which shows the 2×2 isn't a lookup table; it's two genuine questions.

Slide 30 asks it directly: "Why is depreciation alone added back?" The answer has two halves — give both.
  1. Depreciation is a non-cash expense. It was deducted to get to EBIT and NOPAT, but no cash left the company this year. The cash left years ago, when the asset was bought.
  2. And — crucially — because we are about to subtract GROSS capex, not net capex. Gross investment includes the full amount spent on fixed assets this year. If we didn't add depreciation back, we'd be charging the business twice for the same assets: once through the depreciation of old assets and once through the cash cost of new ones.
The equivalent route (say this to show you understand):
FCF = NOPAT + Dep − Gross Capex − ΔWC (gross route) FCF = NOPAT − Net Capex − ΔWC (net route, where Net Capex = Gross Capex − Dep)
Identical. The gross route is preferred because it makes the two real cash items — the depreciation add-back and the actual capex — visible separately, which matters when you're forecasting them off different drivers.

"Alone"? Amortisation of intangibles is added back too (it's non-cash). Deferred taxes are handled by using the cash tax rate on historicals, so no separate add-back. Non-cash provisions are usually small enough to leave in "other expenses".

6.10 Gross Investment

Gross Investment = Capex + Δ Operating Working Capital − Δ NIBL where Capex = Δ Net Fixed Assets + Depreciation
Derive the capex formula — do it once and you'll never misremember it
Closing NFA = Opening NFA + Capex − Depreciation ⇒ Capex = (Closing NFA − Opening NFA) + Depreciation = Δ Net Fixed Assets + Depreciation

It's just the fixed-asset roll-forward rearranged. If NFA is flat but depreciation was ₹300 cr, the company must have spent ₹300 cr just to stand still — maintenance capex. Anything above that is growth capex.

7.1 Operating Invested Capital (OIC)

OIC = Operating Working Capital + Net Operating Fixed Assets (+ other net operating assets)
Slide 36 — his phrasing"Essentially operating invested capital is used to generate the NOPAT for a company."

That sentence is also the consistency test: OIC must contain exactly the assets that produced the NOPAT — no more, no less. If you excluded excess cash from operating assets (§6.3), it must be out of OIC. If a stake in an associate isn't generating your EBIT, it's out of OIC.
Why not just use total assets, or total capital employed?Because ROIC must answer a specific question: "what return is the business earning on the money genuinely tied up in operations?" Total assets includes a treasury portfolio earning 7% in T-bills, which would drag the measured return down and tell you nothing about the operating business. And OIC is measured net of non-interest-bearing liabilities (they're inside operating working capital) because suppliers finance part of the operation for free — that isn't capital you had to raise.

The balance-sheet symmetry: OIC computed from the asset side must equal (interest-bearing debt + equity − non-operating assets) computed from the financing side. Same money, two views. If they don't tie, you've misclassified something.

The comparison that matters is ROIC vs WACC, not ROIC vs zero. A company earning 12% ROIC with a 15% WACC is destroying value while reporting healthy profits.

7.3 EVA — Economic Value Added

EVA = Operating Invested Capital × (ROIC − WACC) Equivalently: EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × OIC)
Show the two forms are the same
OIC × (ROIC − WACC) = OIC × ROIC − OIC × WACC = OIC × (NOPAT/OIC) − OIC × WACC = NOPAT − WACC × OIC ✔
Why EVA exists — the accountant's blind spotAn accountant charges you for the cost of debt (interest is an expense) but charges you nothing for the cost of equity. So a company can show ₹100 crore of "profit" while having ₹2,000 crore of shareholder money tied up demanding a 12% return — i.e. ₹240 crore of cost the P&L never mentions. That firm reports a profit and destroys ₹140 crore of value.

EVA fixes this by charging rent on ALL capital, equity included. EVA > 0 means the business earned more than the full cost of every rupee employed. That is the only definition of "profitable" that a shareholder should accept.
AnalogyYou run a shop out of a building you own. Your accountant says you made ₹5 lakh profit this year. But you could have rented that building out for ₹8 lakh. You didn't make ₹5 lakh — you lost ₹3 lakh by choosing to run a shop instead of being a landlord. EVA is the number that charges you the ₹8 lakh rent on your own capital.

7.4 The growth equation: g = ROIC × Investment Rate

Net Investment Investment Rate = ────────────────── NOPAT g = ROIC × Investment Rate
Derive it — it's two lines and it's the most useful identity in the courseNext year's NOPAT = ROIC × next year's OIC. And next year's OIC = this year's OIC + Net Investment. So:
ΔNOPAT = ROIC × Net Investment ΔNOPAT ROIC × Net Investment g = ─────────── = ───────────────────── = ROIC × Investment Rate NOPAT NOPAT
What it means, and why it kills half the bad valuations in the world: growth is not free. To grow you must invest, and the rate at which profits grow is exactly (how much you reinvest) × (how well you reinvest it). You cannot assume 15% growth with a 10% ROIC and a 25% investment rate — the arithmetic forbids it (that combination yields g = 2.5%).

In an exam, this is the consistency check the examiner is looking for. Any terminal-value assumption must satisfy IR = g ÷ ROIC. Assume g = 5% with ROIC = 20% and your terminal investment rate must be 25% — not whatever your last explicit year happened to be.

7.6 ★ Valuation using the EVA method — and the reconciliation trap

Value = OIC + PV(EVA)
Why this must equal the DCF answerThe capital is already in the business, so it's worth at least what's tied up in it (OIC). Anything above that must come from earning more than the cost of capital — which is exactly what EVA measures. So Value = capital in + PV of the excess returns. It's the DCF rearranged, not a different model.

The practical advantage: EVA tells you where value comes from year by year, which FCF doesn't. A company with negative FCF (Titan, FY2022) can have strongly positive EVA — the FCF is negative because of investment, and the EVA tells you that investment is worth making.

Model answer
Answer

The cost of capital is an opportunity cost on capital currently committed, not a record of capital historically contributed.

Prediction 3 5 marks High — Technical Point 1, and the deck's "it does not matter if g = 0" line invites it

"The pre-tax cost of debt is not the coupon rate of the bond. Yet it is often said that it does not matter which of the two you use. Reconcile these two statements."

Model answer
Answer

The coupon rate is fixed on the issue date and reflects interest rates at that time; the YTM is the return a lender can currently earn on this credit, and cost of capital is by definition an opportunity cost. So the correct pre-tax cost of debt is the YTM.

It nevertheless does not matter when valuing an existing zero-growth company, because the two errors offset: the coupon method pairs a low cost of debt with a high (book) debt value, and the YTM method pairs a high cost with a low (market) debt value. Both reproduce the same equity value, since the actual cash interest paid — and hence PAT — is unchanged.

It does matter the moment the company raises new capital, because new debt is raised at the market rate, not at the historical coupon. In the deck's example a project with an IRR of 17% clears the coupon-based hurdle of 16.87% but fails the true hurdle of 17.57%, and destroys ₹5.12m of shareholder value.

General principle: cost of capital is marginal and forward-looking — the cost of the next rupee, never the average of past rupees.

Guide reference §2.1–2.2.

Prediction 4 5 marks High — the exact structural twin of the Q2 he set

"Why is the cost of preference capital not multiplied by (1 − t) while computing the WACC, when the cost of debt is?"

Model answer
Answer

The (1 − t) factor exists solely to capture the interest tax shield: interest is a deductible expense, so ₹100 of interest costs the company only ₹(100 × (1 − t)) after the tax saving.

Preference dividends are paid out of post-tax profit and are not deductible, so no shield arises. The pre-tax and post-tax costs of preference capital are therefore identical.

The same logic explains why the preference term carries no (1 − t) in the extended Hamada equation: βL = βU + (βU − βD)(D/E)(1 − t) + (βU − βP)(P/E), and why VL = VU when only preference capital is added.

Note also that in India a dividend distribution tax historically applied to preference dividends (16.995% as of 2013), which raises the effective cost rather than lowering it.

Guide reference §3.9, §4.7 · Book pp. 108–109.
Watch for the mirror question: "Why is Ke not multiplied by (1 − t)?" — same answer, plus: FCF is defined as EBIT × (1 − t), i.e. as if the firm were unlevered, so the tax benefit of debt must enter the valuation exactly once, through the WACC.

Prediction 5 5 marks High — slide 41 is a table built to be asked about

"CAPM is a one-period model. Which maturity should be used for the risk-free rate while valuing a company, and why?"

Model answer
Answer

Strictly, each year's cash flow should be discounted at that year's spot rate. If a single rate is used instead, that rate is the yield — and the yield is a value-weighted average of the spot rates, so it is dominated by whichever cash flow contributes most present value.

With spot rates of 5%, 6% and 7%, cash flows of (100, 10, 10) imply a yield of 5.49% — close to the 1-year spot — whereas (100, 100, 10,000) implies 6.99%, essentially the 3-year spot.

In a company valuation the terminal value is 60–80% of the total, so the cash-flow pattern is heavily back-loaded and resembles the second case. We should therefore use the longest-maturity spot rate available on the zero-coupon yield curve (9.12% on the NSE ZCYC as at 2 May 2012). The one-year rate is appropriate only for a project with a one-year life.

Two riders: the ZCYC spot rate differs from the headline G-Sec coupon yield (8.81% vs 8.69% at 10 years) because coupon bonds are front-loaded; and strictly a high-growth company warrants a longer-horizon rate than a mature one, though a flat long end makes one number adequate.

Prediction 6 5 marks High — literal slide question, cash-flow deck slide 31

"While computing operating working capital, what about short-term debt? And what about cash? Justify your treatment."

Model answer
Answer

Both are excluded. Operating working capital = (Total current assets − cash & marketable securities) − (Total current liabilities − short-term borrowings).

Short-term borrowings are financing, not operating. They already appear in the WACC weights as debt and will be subtracted from enterprise value at the end. Leaving them inside working capital counts them twice, and would make an increase in borrowing look like a source of operating cash — double-counting the financing.

Cash is excluded for the mirror reason. Excess cash is a non-operating asset netted against debt, and is added back separately at the end; only a minimum operating float is genuinely tied up in the operating cycle.

This is liability consistency applied to the working-capital line: every claim must be counted exactly once, either as a financing item or as an operating item, never both. For Titan FY2023 this removes ₹6,503 cr of borrowings and ₹792 cr of CMS, giving operating working capital of ₹12,733 cr, or 33% of revenue.

Prediction 7 5 marks Medium-high — book-only, counter-intuitive, and he loves counter-intuitive

"An investor wants to add a country risk premium while valuing a company in an emerging market. Should he? Justify."

Model answer
Answer

No — an arbitrary country risk premium should not be added.

Country risk splits into two parts. The non-diversifiable part already affects markets everywhere (Greece's 2012 crisis suppressed stock markets worldwide), and is therefore already inside the market risk premium, because the MRP is estimated from historical index returns that include all such episodes. Adding it again is double counting.

The remaining, country-specific part is by construction diversifiable for an internationally diversified investor.

Finally, the marginal investor sets the price. Even if one investor demands a premium for Greek risk, globally diversified investors who are not bearing that risk at the margin will buy at the lower required return and bid the price up. Qualification: this assumes integrated capital markets.   1. Value the consolidated entity; less consolidated debt.

Cost of capital is marginal and forward-looking — it is the cost of the next rupee, not the average of past rupees. New debt is raised at the market rate (YTM), not at a coupon negotiated years ago. So: coupon-vs-YTM is harmless for valuing a static, no-growth firm, but fatal for capital budgeting. The same logic drives market-value weights (Technical Point 3) and the use of marginal tax rates.

Q2 · Liability consistency with multiple debts 12 marks

ABC Limited has issued two debt instruments: ₹150 million at 10% and ₹250 million at 13%. Projected EBIT is ₹180 million, tax rate 30%, cost of equity 19%, growth zero. Market value of debt equals book value.

(a) Value the equity using the FCFE method.
(b) Value it by including both debts in the WACC.
(c) Value it by including only the 13% debt in the WACC, showing every adjustment required.
(d) Explain why all three give the same answer, and state the rule involved.

Model answer
(a) FCFE method
Interest = 10% × 150 + 13% × 250 = 15 + 32.5 = ₹47.5m PBT = 180 − 47.5 = ₹132.5m Tax @30% = ₹39.75m ; PAT = ₹92.75m Equity = 92.75 ÷ 0.19 = ₹488.16m
(b) All debts in WACC
EV = 488.16 + 400 = ₹888.16m ; FCF = 180 × 0.7 = ₹126m WACC = (488.16/888.16)×19% + (250/888.16)×13%×0.7 + (150/888.16)×10%×0.7 = 10.4438% + 2.5615% + 1.1814% = 14.1867% EV = 126 ÷ 0.141867 = ₹888.16m ✔ Equity = 888.16 − 400 = ₹488.16m ✔
(c) Only the 13% debt in WACC

The ₹150m at 10% is now an operating liability, so:

  • its interest of ₹15m must be deducted before EBIT;
  • it must not be subtracted from enterprise value;
  • it must not appear in the WACC weights.
Adjusted EBIT = 180 − 15 = ₹165m Adjusted FCF = 165 × 0.7 = ₹115.5m "Capital" = Equity 488.16 + Debt 250 = ₹738.16m WACC = (488.16/738.16)×19% + (250/738.16)×13%×0.7 = 12.5657% + 3.0814% = 15.6471% "EV" = 115.5 ÷ 0.156471 = ₹738.16m Equity = 738.16 − 250 = ₹488.16m ✔
(d) Why they agree — the rule

Each method is internally consistent about who the claimants are. Method (b) values the whole pie and hands out all three slices. Method (c) values a smaller pie — the 10% lender has been reclassified as a supplier and paid before EBIT — and hands out two slices. Method (a) values only the shareholder's slice directly. Different pies, identical shareholder slice.

Liability Consistency If D is a source of capital: do NOT deduct its interest from FCF; DO subtract D from Enterprise Value. If D is NOT a source of capital: DO deduct its interest before EBIT; DO add increases in D to FCF; do NOT subtract D from Enterprise Value.

Every liability must be counted exactly once — either as a financing item (in the discount rate and deducted from EV) or as an operating item (deducted before EBIT). Which method you choose in practice is a matter of convenience; method (c) is useful when a liability's interest rate is not explicitly stated (e.g. trade payables).

Q3 · Cash, and the two treatments 12 marks

A zero-growth company reports EBIT of ₹150 million (excluding interest income). It has debt of ₹200 million at 9% and cash of ₹80 million earning 6%. Tax rate 30%; cost of equity 16%.

(a) Find the equity value.
(b) Compute FCF, enterprise value and WACC treating cash as non-operating.
(c) Repeat, treating cash as operating.
(d) Reconcile, and explain how you decide which treatment to use.

Model answer
(a)
Interest paid = 200 × 9% = 18 ; Interest earned = 80 × 6% = 4.8 PBT = 150 − 18 + 4.8 = ₹136.8m Net income = 136.8 × 0.7 = ₹95.76m Equity = 95.76 ÷ 0.16 = ₹598.50m
(b) Cash non-operating (net-debt method)
FCF = 150 × 0.7 = ₹105m Net debt = 200 − 80 = ₹120m Net interest = 18 − 4.8 = ₹13.2m ⇒ pre-tax cost of net debt = 13.2/120 = 11% After-tax = 11% × 0.7 = 7.7% EV = 598.50 + 200 − 80 = ₹718.50m WACC = (598.50/718.50)×16% + (120/718.50)×7.7% = 13.3277% + 1.2860% = 14.6137% Check: 105 ÷ 0.146137 = 718.50 ✔ Equity = 718.50 − 200 + 80 = ₹598.50m ✔
(c) Cash operating (gross-debt method)
FCF = (150 + 4.8) × 0.7 = ₹108.36m EV = 598.50 + 200 = ₹798.50m WACC = (598.50/798.50)×16% + (200/798.50)×9%×0.7 = 11.9925% + 1.5779% = 13.5704% Check: 108.36 ÷ 0.135704 = 798.50 ✔ Equity = 798.50 − 200 = ₹598.50m ✔
(d) Reconciliation and choice

Both give ₹598.50m. The methods differ in what counts as the business: the gross method includes ₹80m of low-return cash inside the operating entity, which produces a larger FCF (108.36 vs 105), a larger EV (798.50 vs 718.50) and — because low-risk cash dilutes the average — a lower WACC (13.57% vs 14.61%). The effects cancel exactly at the equity line.

Note the effective 11% cost of net debt against a stated 9% loan: borrowing at 9% to hold cash at 6% is a negative carry, and the net-debt method makes that visible.

Choosing: ask whether the business could run tomorrow if the cash vanished. A minimum operating float (roughly 1–2% of revenue) is operating; a large treasury pile is non-operating. Per slide 12, this "has nothing to do with how accountants divide assets… it depends on your valuation objective." The net-debt method is theoretically cleaner for beta work (Appendix D) and needs fewer adjustments, since interest income reported below EBIT must otherwise be added back.

Fatal combinations to avoid: discounting ₹105m at 13.57%, or ₹108.36m at 14.61%, or computing EV correctly and then forgetting to add the ₹80m cash back.

Q4 · Full cost of capital build 18 marks

You are valuing Deccan Cements Limited, listed, with 50 million shares trading at ₹340. Book value of debt ₹2,000 million, all long-term, rated AA with 10 years to maturity. The company also has an operating lease with future rentals whose present value you have computed at ₹600 million. The 20-year G-Sec spot rate is 7.0%; the market risk premium is 7.13%; the marginal tax rate is 25%. Comparable cement companies have a median unlevered beta of 0.85. From the FIMMDA table, the 10-year AA spread is 142 bps.

(a) Compute the cost of debt.
(b) Compute the levered beta and cost of equity, treating the operating lease as debt.
(c) Compute the WACC.
(d) State every adjustment you must now make to the cash flow, and what you must subtract from enterprise value.

Model answer
(a) Cost of debt
Pre-tax K_d = 7.00% + 1.42% = 8.42% After-tax K_d = 8.42% × (1 − 0.25) = 6.315%

Method used: Pecking Order 3 — the credit-rating route, appropriate because no traded bond yield is given. Note that the same rate applies to the capitalised operating lease, since lease obligations rank alongside debt in risk.

(b) Beta and cost of equity
Market value of equity = 50m × ₹340 = ₹17,000m Total debt (incl. capitalised lease) = 2,000 + 600 = ₹2,600m D/E = 2,600 ÷ 17,000 = 0.15294 β_L = 0.85 × [1 + 0.15294 × (1 − 0.25)] = 0.85 × 1.11471 = 0.9475 K_e = 7.00% + 0.9475 × 7.13% = 7.00% + 6.755% = 13.76%
(c) WACC
V = 17,000 + 2,600 = ₹19,600m w_E = 86.735% ; w_D = 13.265% WACC = 0.86735 × 13.76% + 0.13265 × 6.315% = 11.9347% + 0.8377% = 12.77%

Sanity check: 12.77% lies between 6.315% and 13.76%, and sits close to Ke because the company is ~87% equity-financed at market values. ✔

(d) Consistency requirements
  1. Restate EBIT for the lease. Reported EBIT already deducts the full lease rental as an operating expense. Since the lease is now a financing item, add back only its interest component: 600 × 8.42% = ₹50.52m. The implied depreciation (rental − 50.52) stays as an operating charge.
  2. Do not deduct any interest — on the loan or the lease — in the FCF. FCF = adjusted EBIT × (1 − t) + depreciation − capex − ΔOWC + ΔNIBL. The tax shield is already captured by the (1−t) on Kd inside the WACC; deducting interest again would double-count it.
  3. Exclude short-term borrowings from operating working capital, and exclude cash & marketable securities from operating current assets.
  4. At the end, subtract ₹2,600m — the loan and the capitalised lease — from enterprise value. Whatever went into the WACC weights must come out at the bridge. Then add the market value of any non-operating assets.
  5. If any cash was treated as non-operating, add it back at market value.

Alternative permitted treatment: ignore the lease entirely — leave the full rentals in operating expenses, exclude ₹600m from the WACC weights, and do not subtract it from EV. This gives the same equity value and is the convenient choice when the lease is a small share of total debt.

Survivorship bias in the historical market risk premium

The historical MRP is estimated as the average excess of index returns over the risk-free rate — for India, 10.55% arithmetic over 1991–2011. The estimate is biased upward because the index is not a fixed basket: the BSE has changed Sensex composition more than 50 times between 1992 and 2012, and stocks that fail or go bankrupt are removed and replaced by successful ones. On 12 January 2009, BSE replaced Satyam Computers with Sun Pharmaceuticals; post-2009 Sensex returns therefore reflect Sun Pharma's performance, not Satyam's collapse. Investors in 2008 could not have known this would happen. The measured 10.55% thus reflects the realised performance of survivors rather than the return investors could reasonably have expected ex ante — so it overstates the true expected premium, and should be treated as an upper bound.

Why growth creates value only when ROIC > WACC

Growth is not free: g = ROIC × Investment Rate, so to grow at g a company must reinvest IR = g ÷ ROIC of its NOPAT, which reduces free cash flow. Whether that trade is worth making depends on the return earned on the reinvested capital relative to its cost. Take three firms with OIC ₹100m, WACC 15%, g 5%, and ROIC of 10%, 15% and 20%. Their values are ₹50m, ₹100m and ₹150m — identical growth, triple the value. The EVA formulation makes the mechanism explicit: EVA = OIC × (ROIC − WACC), so value = OIC + PV(EVA). When ROIC = WACC, EVA is zero and growth is value-neutral at any rate. When ROIC < WACC, growth destroys value: the ROIC-10% firm would be worth ₹66.7m if it stopped growing, against ₹50m if it grows at 5%. It should shrink and return cash. This is also why market-to-book ratios are, at bottom, a statement about ROIC versus WACC.

The four-question audit — run this on every answer

Sixty seconds that saves papers
  1. Whose cash flow is this, and whose rate am I using? Firm ↔ WACC, equity ↔ Ke. No exceptions.
  2. Is every liability counted exactly once? Either in the discount rate and deducted from EV, or expensed before EBIT and not deducted. Never both. Never neither.
  3. Are the numerator and denominator in the same units? Nominal ↔ nominal. Rupees ↔ rupee rate.
  4. Does the terminal year satisfy g = ROIC × IR? If not, fix the investment rate before computing TV.
And the arithmetic check: WACC must lie between the after-tax cost of debt and the cost of equity. If it doesn't, stop and find the error.
