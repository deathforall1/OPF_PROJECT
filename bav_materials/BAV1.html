01Foundations

What a company is made of, and why accounting is the way it is

Before you can value a business, you need to know whose money is inside it and why the numbers on a financial statement look the way they do.

1.1 A business has two owners: shareholders and lenders

Every company is built the same way. Someone puts in equity capital(shareholders) and someone lends debt capital(a bank or bondholder). Both groups paid for a claim on the same pile of assets, but the claims are completely different in character.

From the slides · a 500-firm thought experiment

Imagine 500 firms. In each one, shareholders put in Rs.1, 000 million and expect a 15% return. A bank lends Rs.1, 000 million to each firm at 10%. One of the 500 firms goes bankrupt. What happens to each investor?

The bank lent to all 500 firms individually and has no way to average away the loss on the one that failed beyond its loan book. A shareholder who is diversified across many companies barely feels it: one bad holding in a portfolio of hundreds is noise. This is why banks care about a single firm's survival far more intensely than a diversified shareholder does.

This difference in exposure explains a lot of downstream behavior:

  • If the company does brilliantly, the bank still only ever earns its fixed interest rate. Nothing more. If the company fails, the bank can lose its principal.
  • Shareholders capture all the upside and, if diversified, absorb the downside comfortably.
  • Because banks bear asymmetric risk (capped upside, real downside), banks push companies to be conservative: stable cash flows over high growth, expenses treated conservatively (advertising and R& D expensed immediately, but fixed assets allowed to be capitalized).
§

Why this matters for valuation:accounting rules were shaped more by what lenders needed to see (a conservative, cash-focused picture) than by what shareholders actually care about. As a shareholder-valuer, you have to unlearn some of that conservatism. It is fine if a company burns cash for a while, and it is fine if a few portfolio companies eventually go bust, as long as the overall expected return clears your required rate.

1.2 Limited liability is the reason equity investing exists at all

Before 1811 (when New York introduced the Limited Liability Law, a template the rest of the world later copied), a shareholder could be personally liable for a failing company's debts beyond their investment. Under that regime, almost nobody would buy shares in a business they didn' t control. Limited liability capped a shareholder's downside at what they invested, which is precisely what makes diversification across many small equity stakes viable, and is a foundational assumption behind every equity valuation model you will learn.

1.3 Which financial statement do you read first?

From the slides · the 1990 fax

It's 1990. You are at a private equity firm, on vacation in Mauritius. A fax arrives with the balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow statement of a target company, XYZ Limited. Which do you read first?

The honest answer: it dependson which valuation method and which multiple you are about to use. If you are going to use a cash-flow based DCF, the cash flow statement matters most. If you plan to use a price-to-book multiple, you need the balance sheet. The choice of "which statement first" is really a preview of "which multiple is correct for this company," a theme that returns constantly in relative valuation (Module 5 onward).

1.4 Accounting depreciation lies about how value actually declines

Here is a genuinely counter-intuitive result that shows up on Prof. Mohanty's slides and is a favorite exam trap. Consider an asset costing Rs.1,000 that generates a level Rs.300 cash flow for 5 years, at an IRR of 15.24%.

Worked example · economic depreciation
Year 0 1 2 3 4 5
Cash flow -1000 300 300 300 300 300
Opening PV - 1000.00 852.38 682.27 486.24 260.33
Closing PV 1000.00 852.38 682.27 486.24 260.33 0.00
Economic depreciation - 147.62 170.11 196.03 225.91 260.33
Profit (return on capital) - 152.38 129.89 103.97 74.09 39.67

Notice that the Rs.300 cash flow is level every year, but economic depreciation risesfrom Rs.147.62 to Rs.260.33 while profit fallsfrom Rs.152.38 to Rs.39.67. Why? The Rs.300 splits into "return on the remaining capital"(15.24% of the opening balance) and "return of capital"(depreciation). As the opening balance shrinks each year, the return-on-capital slice shrinks with it, so a growing share of the constant Rs.300 must be depreciation. Depreciation is not a smooth straight line: it accelerates precisely because the asset's remaining economic value is shrinking on a smaller and smaller base.

Contrast this with what a plain straight-line or accounting ROCE calculation shows: a conventional method spreads Rs.200 depreciation evenly across 5 years and shows ROCE risingfrom 10% to 50% as the book value shrinks, purely as an artifact of the accounting method, not because the business is actually getting more profitable. This is the first lesson in a theme you will meet again and again: reported accounting numbers and economic reality can diverge, and a valuer has to know which one they are looking at.

02Two Philosophies

Absolute valuation vs relative valuation

Every valuation method you will ever use is one of these two things, or a mash-up of both.

2.1 The barber, the landlord, and the bank shareholder

The textbook (Chapter 1) makes this distinction with three everyday examples. Hold on to all three: you will recognize this pattern in every multiple you learn later.

Example 1: Rajiv's barber shop

Absolute route:Rajiv works out his average cost per haircut (Rs.10) and adds a 100% margin, pricing at Rs.20. He is building the price from his own costs and required return, without looking at anyone else's price.

Relative route:Rajiv simply checks what a nearby barber shop charges (Rs.18) and charges the same, perhaps with a small premium for a nicer shop. He is not questioning whether Rs.18 is "correct." He takes the market price as given and prices relative to it.

Example 2: Deepa's rental flat near Salt Lake

Absolute route:Deepa forecasts net rental income (Rs.3 lakh/year, growing 5% annually), decides she wants a 15% return, assumes a 40-year life, and discounts all the cash flows. That present value, about Rs.29.21 lakh, is the maximum she should pay. This is a discounted cash flow (DCF) calculation, i.e. absolute valuation.

Relative route:she simply finds that similar flats in the area sell at Rs.2, 500 per square foot, and for a 1, 400 sqft flat that is Rs.35 lakh. She takes the Rs.2, 500/sqft figure as given.

Example 3: Valuing Syndicate Bank shares

Absolute route:project all future dividends and capital gains an investor would receive, and discount them to a present value per share.

Relative route:Syndicate Bank's EPS was Rs.33.30 (TTM). Similar banks trade at 5.06 times earnings. Implied price = 33.30 × 5.06 = Rs.168.5 per share. You are not questioning whether "5.06 times" is the right multiple for banks in general; you take that as given from the market.

§

In relative valuation, you implicitly assume other similar assets have already been priced correctly by the market, and you simply transfer that pricing onto the asset you are valuing. In absolute valuation, you form your own opinion of value from first principles (cash flows, required return, growth) without leaning on anyone else's price.

2.2 Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): the "most scientific" method

DCF says a business is worth the present value of every future cash flow it will generate:

DCF value
Value=CF₁ (1+r)+CF₂ (1+r)² +... to infinity

Here ris a risk-adjusted discount rate. You can apply this at the level of the whole firm (discount cash flow available to everyone who financed the firm, at the weighted average cost of capital, then subtract net debt to get equity value) or directly to equity (discount cash flow or dividends left over for shareholders, at the cost of equity, to get equity value directly). The Dividend Discount Model you will meet in Module 3 is the equity-level version.

DCF is considered the most scientific approach because it explicitly forces you to state every value driver: the cash flow forecast, the growth rate, and the discount rate. Nothing is hidden.

2.3 The Multiplier (relative) approach

Here you value an asset by comparing it to similar assets, using a ratio of price to some financial metric:

Multiplier identity
PAI=P'AI' for any AI > 0

where AIis an "accounting indicator"(earnings, book value, sales, EBITDA), and the prime marks are the peer company. Two principles govern whether this is done correctly:

  1. AI must be a genuine value driverof the asset, and it must move consistently with the value (e.g. more earnings should mean more value, all else equal).
  2. Numerator-denominator consistency:is the accounting indicator determining the value of equity alone, or of the whole firm (equity + debt)? Is it money available to all fund providers, or to equity holders only? Get this wrong and you compare apples to oranges (this is exactly the point of the concept-check question in Module 5).
The book's sharpest point about the two methods

DCF argues that value is the cause and the multiplier is the effect: you build up the cash flow forecast, discount it to get a value, and only then can you divide by an accounting number to see what multiple that value implies. The multiplier method flips this: it treats the multiplier as the cause and value as the effect(you look up a multiple and multiply). Since the multiple was picked somewhat arbitrarily (from comparable companies, without necessarily interrogating whether those comparables were themselves priced correctly), the multiplier method is theoretically less rigorous, even though it is by far the more popular method in practice because it is fast.

2.4 Historical cost approach

The crudest method: value the stock at book value, or, in a refined version, at replacement cost of assets minus market value of liabilities, divided by shares outstanding. This is sometimes called Net Asset Value (NAV), and it puts a floor under the stock price: if the market price falls well below this, someone can theoretically buy the whole company and sell its assets piecemeal for a profit.

Net Asset Value per share
NAV=Replacement Cost of Assets - Market Value of LiabilitiesNumber of shares outstanding

2.5 The five consistencies

The book's central promise is: as long as you maintain five consistencies in a valuation model, it will be theoretically correct, no matter which specific DCF variant (FCF, FCFE, APV, EVA, dividend discount) you use. These are worth memorizing now, you will see them referenced constantly:

  1. Assumptions consistency
  2. Risk consistency
  3. Asset consistency
  4. Liability consistency
  5. Multiplier consistency

03DDM

The Dividend Discount Model and where value actually comes from

This module answers the single most important question in this course: does growth always make a stock more valuable?

3.1 The Gordon growth formula

If a company pays a level-growing dividend forever, its value is:

Gordon growth model
P₀ =D₁ r - g

where D₁ is next year's dividend, r is the shareholder' s required return, and g is the constant growth rate of dividends forever.

3.2 Where does the growth rate itself come from?

A company can only grow its dividend by reinvesting the earnings it does not pay out. That gives a clean formula:

Sustainable growth rate
g=ROE × (1 - Payout Ratio)=ROE × Retention Ratio
Warm-up numeric

Net worth=$100m, ROE=20%, payout ratio=60%. Retention=1 - 0.60=40%. g=20% × 40%=8%.

3.3 The single most important idea in this course

Growth is not automatically good for shareholders. Whether growth adds or destroys value depends entirely on the relationship between ROE and the required return, r:

§

If ROE > r, growth adds value.
If ROE < r, growth destroys value.
If ROE=r, growth has no effect on value at all.

This feels backwards to most beginners the first time they see it, so let's prove it with three parallel companies, each with Net Worth = $100m and 2 million shares, where we bump the growth rate from 0% to 5% and watch what happens to price per share.

Worked example · Y Company (ROE 20% > r 15%)
Growth rate Payout E₁ ($m) D₁ ($m) Firm value ($m) Price/share
g=0% 100% 20 20 20 / 0.15=133.33 $66.67
g=5% 75% 20 15 15 / (0.15-0.05)=150.00 $75.00

Price rises from $66.67 to $75.00. Since ROE(20%) > r(15%), every rupee retained and reinvested earns more than shareholders demand, so growth is value-accretive.

Worked example · X Company (ROE 15% < r 20%)
Growth rate Payout E₁ ($m) D₁ ($m) Firm value ($m) Price/share
g=0% 100% 15 15 15 / 0.20=75.00 $37.50
g=5% 66.7% 15 10 10 / (0.20-0.05)=66.67 $33.33

Price falls from $37.50 to $33.33. Since ROE(15%) < r(20%), reinvested rupees earn less than shareholders require, so retaining more (to fund growth) actively destroys value versus just paying it all out.

Worked example · X Company again (ROE 15%=r 15%)
Growth rate Payout E₁ ($m) D₁ ($m) Firm value ($m) Price/share
g=0% 100% 15 15 15 / 0.15=100.00 $50.00
g=5% 66.7% 15 10 10 / (0.15-0.05)=100.00 $50.00

Price is unchanged at $50.00. When ROE exactly equals r, reinvested capital earns exactly what shareholders require, no more, no less, so shifting money between dividends today and reinvestment for tomorrow is a wash.

3.4 NPVGO: separating "no-growth value" from "growth value"

Every stock's price can be split into two pieces: the value it would have with zero growth (just paying out 100% of earnings forever) and the extra value created by growth opportunities:

Price-earnings identity with NPVGO
P₀ E₁ =1r+NPVGOE₁

The first term, 1/r, is the "no-growth" P/E. The second term is the Net Present Value of Growth Opportunities, expressed per rupee of current earnings. NPVGO can be positive (Y Company above), negative (X Company, growth destroys value here, so the growth term must be negative), or zero.

Computing NPVGO for Y Company

E₁ =$20m, r=15%, so no-growth value=20/0.15=$133.33m. Actual value with 5% growth=$150.00m. NPVGO=150.00 - 133.33=$16.67m(or $8.33 per share). All of the extra $8.33/share created by that growth announcement is captured in this one number.

04Capital Structure

Modigliani-Miller: does borrowing money change what a company is worth?

This module is a detour from multiples, but it explains why enterprise value and equity value are not the same thing, which you will lean on constantly from Module 5 onward.

4.1 Separate the investment decision from the financing decision

  • The investment decision(what projects to take, what assets to hold) sits on the asset side of the balance sheet and drives NOPAT.
  • The financing decision(how much debt vs equity) sits on the liability side and drives the cost of capital.

Modigliani and Miller (MM) asked a narrow, precise question: holding the investment decision fixed, does changing the debt-to-equity mix change firm value?

4.2 MM Proposition I (no taxes): capital structure is irrelevant

§

In a world with no taxes, the value of a firm is independent of its capital structure. Its weighted average cost of capital (WACC) does not change no matter how much debt it takes on.

Worked example · recapitalization, no tax

Debt=Rs.100m at Kd=6%. Equity=Rs.200m at Ke=12%. WACC=(100× 6% + 200× 12%) / 300=(6+24)/300=10%.

Now the company issues Rs.100m of new shares and fully retires its debt. Total capital is still Rs.300m, all equity. Under MM I, firm value and WACC are unchanged, so the new all-equity Ke must itself equal 10%(down from a blended 12% cost, because the "financing risk" that leverage added to equity has been removed entirely; only pure business risk remains).

Worked example · borrow and buyback, no tax

An unlevered company has NOI=Rs.10m, opportunity cost of capital=10%, 5m shares. Unlevered value=10/0.10=Rs.100m, so price=Rs.100m/5m=Rs.20/share.

It borrows Rs.40m and uses it entirely to buy back shares. Under MM (no tax, no new project), firm value is still Rs.100m. Shares bought back=40/20=2m, leaving 3m shares. Remaining equity value=100 - 40=Rs.60m. New price=60/3=Rs.20/share, unchanged.The lesson: simply moving money between debt and equity holders, with no tax benefit and no new project, cannot create or destroy value.

4.3 Where financing risk lives: cost of equity and beta

Total risk borne by shareholders splits into two parts:

Beta decomposition
Total Risk=Business Risk+Financing Risk → β Leveredis built on top of β Unlevered

When a business changes its leverage, the underlying business risk is untouched; only the financing risk layer changes. This is why MM II (below) says the cost of equity rises mechanically with leverage, even while WACC (in the no-tax case) stays flat.

4.4 MM Proposition II: cost of equity rises with leverage

MM II, no tax
Ke=r+DE× (r - Kd)
MM II, with tax
Ke=r+DE× (r - Kd) × (1 - t)

Adding debt raises Ke, but if the increase in Ke exactly offsets the cheaper cost of the added debt, WACC stays constant (this is the no-tax case). With tax, debt gets an additional advantage: interest is tax deductible.

4.5 MM Proposition I, with tax: debt now creates real value

Value of a levered firm, with corporate tax
VL=VU+t × D

The term t × D is the present value of the interest tax shield: the government effectively subsidizes debt by letting the company deduct interest before paying tax. This is the one clean exception to "capital structure doesn't matter."

Full worked example · U company recapitalizes, with tax

Unlevered company U: EBIT=Rs.1, 000, opportunity cost of capital=10%, tax=30%, 100 shares outstanding.

NOPAT=1, 000 × (1-0.30)=Rs.700. VU=700/0.10=Rs.7, 000. Price/share=7, 000/100=Rs.70.

Company borrows Rs.5, 000 at 5% and uses it to buy back shares. Tax shield value=0.30 × 5, 000=Rs.1, 500.
VL=7, 000+1, 500=Rs.8, 500. New equity value=8, 500 - 5, 000=Rs.3, 500.

Price jumps immediately to VL/100 shares=Rs.85/share (this happens the moment the market learns of the plan, before the buyback is even executed). Shares repurchased=5, 000/85=58.8; shares remaining=41.2. Remaining equity value=Rs.3, 500; price=3, 500/41.2 ≈ Rs.85, unchanged after the buyback, exactly as MM predicts.

New cost of equity: Ke=10%+(5, 000/3, 500)× (10%-5%)× (1-0.30)=10%+1.4286× 5%× 0.70=10%+5.0%=15%.

A shortcut worth memorizing: the price-impact formula

When a company announces new debt that funds (a) a positive-NPV project and (b) a share buyback, the entire jump in price per share can be computed directly, without building a full balance sheet:

Δ Price per share=NPV of new project+(t × new permanent debt)original shares outstanding

Example: a zero-growth unlevered company has NOI=Rs.250m, Ke=16%, 20m shares, tax=30%. Unlevered value=(250× 0.70)/0.16=Rs.1, 093.75m, price=Rs.54.69/share. It then announces it will borrow Rs.800m: Rs.500m to fund a project with NPV=Rs.100m, and Rs.300m to buy back shares.

Δ Price/share=(100 + 0.30× 800) / 20m=(100+240)/20=Rs.17/share. New price=54.69+17=Rs.71.69/share, and this holds both immediately on announcement and after the buyback executes (you can check this by rebuilding the full balance sheet; the answer matches).

05Multiples 101

The value driver concept: what makes any multiple correct or wrong

Every multiple you'll meet from here (P/E, P/B, P/S, EV/EBITDA) is just a specific choice of value driver, X. This module is the master key.

5.1 The basic identity, again, and why it matters more here

Multiplier method
VX=Multiple, so V=Multiple × X

Two questions must be answered correctly before you trust any V/X ratio:

  1. Is X determining the value of equity, or the value of the whole firm?EBITDA belongs to everyone who financed the company (debt and equity), so it pairs with Enterprise Value (EV), not with market capitalization. Net profit belongs to equity holders only, so it pairs with market capitalization (or price per share), not EV.
  2. Is X available to all fund providers, or to equity shareholders alone?Same idea, phrased the other way. Get this backwards and your multiple is simply wrong, no matter how carefully you picked your comparable companies.
Concept check, straight from the slides

A company has market capitalization of Rs.5, 000 crore, debt of Rs.2, 000 crore, cash of Rs.500 crore, and EBITDA of Rs.1, 000 crore. Which multiple is correct: (a) Market cap / EBITDA, (b) Enterprise value / EBITDA, (c) Share price / EBITDA, (d) Debt / EBITDA?

Answer: (b).EBITDA is a firm-level number (available to both debt and equity holders, before either is paid), so it must be divided into a firm-level value. Enterprise Value=Market cap+Debt - Cash=5, 000+2, 000 - 500=Rs.6, 500 crore. EV/EBITDA=6, 500/1, 000=6.5x. Market cap/EBITDA would understate the true multiple by ignoring the fact that equity holders don't have first claim on that EBITDA; debt holders do.

5.2 The three determinants of every multiple

Whatever the multiple, it is driven by exactly three underlying forces:

§

Profitability, Growth, and Risk.The relationship is not linear (a common mistake is fitting a straight-line regression of multiple against growth alone; you saw this fail in the Nifty Bank regression example ahead in Module 6).

5.3 Multiples are dangerous but useful

Multiples are popular because they are fast, market-based, and easy to communicate. They become misleading the moment you ignore differences in growth, risk, profitability, accounting policy, or capital structure between the company you're valuing and its "comparable" peers.

5.4 Which multiple fits which industry?

Sector Typical multiple(s) Why
Technology EV/Sales, EV/Revenue Often negative or near-zero EBITDA in growth phase
Consumer / Retail P/E, EV/EBITDA (EV/Sales for early D2C) Stable, comparable earnings
Financial services P/B, P/E Balance sheet (book equity) is the core value driver
Healthcare / biotech EV/Sales (pre-revenue), EV/EBITDA (mature pharma) Depends on stage; pre-revenue firms have no earnings
Energy / utilities EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF, EV/Reserves Capital-intensive, asset-heavy, cash-flow driven

The underlying rule: choose X such that the industry actually treats it as a key performance indicator. If X is not something the industry itself watches, do not use V/X for that industry.

5.5 What you should be able to answer by the end of this course

  • Which multiple is appropriate for this company?
  • Who are the real peers?
  • What adjustments are needed before comparison?
  • Should we use trailing, current, or forward denominator?
  • Is the company cheap for a good reason?
  • What valuation range is defensible?

Keep this list in your pocket. It reappears, expanded to ten questions, as the final checklist at the end of Module 11.

06The Big One

The Price-Earnings Ratio, properly understood

Far and away the most-used multiple in equity research. Almost every mistake in relative valuation shows up first in a misused P/E.

6.1 Definition, and the EPS you must be careful about

Price-Earnings Ratio
P/E=Market Price per ShareEarnings per Share

The numerator is usually just the current market price. The denominator has several flavors, and mixing them up is the single most common analyst error:

  • Current EPS:the most recent completed financial year.
  • Trailing EPS (TTM):the most recent four quarters.
  • Forward EPS:analysts' estimate for the next fiscal year, or next four quarters.

There are three practical ways to use P/E:

  1. Multiply the current P/E by expected EPS to get an upside target.
  2. Divide current price by expected EPS to get a forward P/E, and compare it against the current (trailing) P/E.
  3. Apply an industry (or model) P/E to the current or expected EPS to test whether the stock is over- or under-valued.
Real example · Tech Mahindra (ICICI Direct)

FY24 EPS=Rs.26.7, FY24 P/E=52.7x. FY26E EPS=Rs.65.5. Two ways to reach a similar target price: 42.2 (FY25E EPS) × 33.3 (a normalized multiple) ≈ Rs.1, 405, or 65.5 (FY26E EPS) × 21.4 (a lower multiple as earnings normalize) ≈ Rs.1, 402. Both routes to a similar answer, because EPS and multiple move in opposite directions as a temporarily depressed margin recovers, a pattern you'll see formalized in the "normalizing EPS" section below.

6.2 Basic vs diluted EPS: don't get this wrong on an exam

Worked problem, exactly as posed on the slides

A company had 1, 000, 000 shares outstanding on 1 April. It issued 200, 000 more shares on 1 June. It also has 50, 000 convertible bonds (face value Rs.100, 10% coupon, convertible into 1 share each, outstanding since the start of the year), 500, 000 employee stock options exercisable at Rs.30/share, and the stock trades at Rs.100/share. Expected profit after tax for the year: Rs.2, 000, 000.

Basic EPSuses the weighted-average shares actually outstanding: 1, 000, 000 for 2 months (Apr-May) plus 1, 200, 000 for 10 months (Jun-Mar)=weighted average ≈ 1, 166, 667 shares. Basic EPS ≈ 2, 000, 000/1, 166, 667 ≈ Rs.1.71.

Diluted EPSmust additionally assume conversion of anything that could dilute existing shareholders: the 50, 000 convertible bonds become 50, 000 more shares (and if converted, the company saves the after-tax interest it would have paid, which is added back to earnings). The stock options are dilutive only for the "net" shares they'd add, using the treasury-stock method (proceeds from exercise at Rs.30 are assumed used to buy back shares at the Rs.100 market price, so only the net new shares count). Diluted EPS will always be equal to or lower than basic EPS, because it spreads the same earnings over more shares.

Concept check

Can we compute the P/E multiple simply by dividing total market capitalization by total net income (PAT)?

Only if the PAT figure is genuinely all attributable to the ordinary equity shareholders whose shares make up that market cap (i.e. no minority interest, no preference dividends carved out, and the share count used for market cap matches the share count implicit in that PAT figure). This is the same numerator-denominator consistency idea from Module 5, applied at the level of a single ratio.

6.3 Normalizing EPS: don't value a company on a freak year

Cyclical or one-off events can make the most recent four quarters of EPS a poor guide to a business's real, sustainable earnings power. Two fixes exist:

  1. Use the historical average EPS over a full business cycle.
  2. Use the average ROE over a cycle, applied to the currentbook value per share. This is the better method, because it reflects the business's current size rather than an average size from years ago.
Real example · PVR Cinemas

PVR's EPS collapsed from roughly Rs.36.97 (2019) to negative territory for several years (2020 to 2023, including a covid-hit year and merger-related losses). Rather than use the crashed current EPS, analysts normalized: Book Value per Share = Rs.748.31, Average ROE (4-year period ending 2019) = 8%. Projected/normalized earnings ≈ Rs.60/share. This is a far more usable number for a P/E valuation than the actual reported figure of that year.

Two further adjustments matter before you trust a reported EPS:

  • Non-recurring items:gains or losses from asset sales, asset write-downs, goodwill impairment, one-off provisions, or accounting-estimate changes should typically be stripped out.
  • Comparability across companies:one company using FIFO and a peer using weighted-average inventory costing will report different profits from identical underlying economics.
Concept check

A cement company reports unusually high earnings this year due to a temporary supply shortage that inflated prices. Should you use current EPS for a P/E valuation?

No.This is exactly the "unsustainable current earnings" trap. Normalize using average margins or ROE over a full cycle instead, otherwise you will apply a fair multiple to an inflated earnings base and overvalue the company.

6.4 The theoretical P/E formula, and why it matters

Justified (theoretical) P/E ratio
P/E=ROE - gROE × (r - g)
Verifying the formula against Module 3's Y Company

ROE=20%, r=15%, g=5%. P/E=(0.20-0.05) / (0.20× (0.15-0.05))=0.15/0.02=7.5x.

Cross-check directly: EPS₁ =$20m/2m shares=$10/share. Price/share was computed as $75 in Module 3. P/E=75/10=7.5x. Matches exactly.

This formula also tells you exactly what to interrogate when comparing two companies' P/E ratios. Two conditions must both hold before a P/E comparison is fair:

  1. Ke(r) must be comparable:similar risk-free rate, similar market risk premium, similar beta.
  2. NPVGO must be comparable:similar growth opportunities (same ROE-minus-r spread, roughly).
Real-market application · reverse-engineering the Sensex

Average ROE of Sensex-30 companies ≈ 19.69%. Assume long-run growth g=6%. 10-year G-Sec yield (a proxy for risk-free rate)=7.02%. Market risk premium (IIMA estimate)=4.93%, so r=7.02%+4.93%=11.95%.

Implied P/E=(19.69-6)/(19.69× (11.95-6))=13.69/(19.69× 5.95)=13.69/117.16 ≈ 11.7x. But the Sensex actually traded around 20.82xin 2025-26. The model-implied P/E is well below the market's actual P/E.

You can run the formula backwards instead: hold ROE and g fixed, and solve for what required return r the market must be implicitly using to justify a P/E of 20.82x. Working through the algebra gives r ≈ 9.3%, which implies a market risk premium of only about 2.3%, well below the 4.93% historical IIMA estimate. Either the market expects higher growth than 6%, or it is pricing risk far more generously than the historical average, or the market is running rich relative to this simple model. As the slide itself notes, this kind of reverse-engineering is illustrative and "highly subjective," not a precision instrument, but it is exactly the reasoning an analyst uses to sanity-check an index-level valuation.

6.5 Another lens on the same formula: P/E via NPVGO

Same idea, NPVGO form
P₀ E₁ =1Ke+NPVGOE₁

This is exactly the identity from Module 3, restated as a multiple. It reinforces the same comparability rule: Ke must be comparable, and NPVGO (growth opportunities) must be comparable.

6.6 The regression trap, and when non-linear models do better

A tempting shortcut is to regress observed P/E ratios against Beta, ROE, and Growth across a peer set, then flag anything trading above or below the regression's predicted P/E as over- or under-valued.

Real example · Nifty Bank Index regression

Fitted model: PE=40.62 - 13.34× Beta - 22.15× ROE - 0.32× Growth. Applying this to each bank's actual Beta/ROE/Growth gives a "fair" predicted P/E; comparing it against the bank' s actual traded P/E flags Bank of Baroda and SBI as undervalued (actual P/E below predicted), and A U Small Finance Bank as overvalued (actual well above predicted).

But the relationship between a multiple and its drivers is not linear(recall Module 5.2). A decision-tree (non-linear) model run on the same data reshuffled several of these calls entirely: Federal Bank flipped from overvalued to undervalued, IDFC First flipped from undervalued to overvalued. The lesson: a single linear regression on cross-sectional multiples can be badly misleading, and Beta typically dominates ROE and Growth in feature importance for banks specifically, not the other way round.

6.7 When you must NOT use P/E

§

1. Valuing high-growth companies, where high growth requires such heavy investment that current EPS is tiny or negative (Amazon around 2013 had CFI/CFO near 78% while reporting thin GAAP profits).
2. Valuing natural-resource companies, where earnings depend heavily on commodity price cycles rather than operating performance.
3. When current earnings are not sustainable, i.e. this year's revenue or unit growth tells you nothing about next year' s (Apple's iPhone/iPad unit growth rates cratered from 71%/61% in 2012 to 16%/3% in 2013).

07 Growth-Adjusted

PEG and PEGY: adjusting P/E for growth

Quick, popular, and quietly biased. Know the bias, not just the formula.

7.1 PEG Ratio

PEG Ratio
PEG=P/Eg (as a whole number, e.g. 5 for 5%)
Worked example

P/E=16, expected growth=5%. PEG=16/5=3.2.

Analysts commonly (and loosely) treat PEG ≈ 1 as "fairly valued," below 1 as cheap, and above 1 as expensive relative to growth. Treat this as a rule of thumb, not a law.

7.2 Why PEG is biased against low-growth firms

The relationship between value and growth is not a straight line (again, Module 5.2's non-linearity point), but PEG divides by growth as if it were. This systematically penalizes slow, stable compounders and flatters high-growth names, purely as an artifact of the formula' s shape, not necessarily because low-growth firms are actually cheaper or more expensive.

7.3 PEGY: folding in the dividend yield

PEGY was designed specifically to correct PEG's blind spot for mature, dividend-paying companies whose total shareholder return comes partly from yield, not just growth.

PEGY Ratio
PEGY=P/EExpected Growth Rate + Dividend Yield
Worked example

P/E=16, expected growth=5%, dividend yield=4.5%. PEG=16/5=3.2. PEGY=16/(5+4.5)=16/9.5=1.7. Notice PEGY paints a very different (much less alarming) picture than PEG for the same company, once the dividend yield is given credit.

08 Book Value Based

Price to Book Value (P/B)

Where the balance sheet, not the income statement, does the talking.

8.1 The formula, derived from the same DNA as P/E

Start from the Gordon growth model, substitute E₁ =ROE × BV₀ (this year's book value determines next year' s earnings), and divide both sides by book value per share:

Price-to-Book, stable growth firm
P/B=ROE - gr - g
Verifying against Y Company (Module 3)

ROE=20%, g=5%, r=15%. P/B=(0.20-0.05)/(0.15-0.05)=0.15/0.10=1.5x. Cross-check: Book value/share=$100m/2m=$50. Price/share=$75. P/B=75/50=1.5x. Matches.

Notice P/B's drivers are the same three forces (ROE, growth, risk) as P/E, just combined differently: P/E divides by ROE×(r-g), while P/B divides only by (r-g). This is why P/E and P/B rank companies differently even when both are "correct."

8.2 Computing book value per share correctly

  • Usually exclude intangible assets (goodwill, brand value) since these are often not independently realizable or reliably measured.
  • Exclude revaluation reserves (asset write-ups that never passed through the income statement).
  • Be deliberate about how you treat deferred tax liabilities; there's no single universal rule, but be consistent across peers.

8.3 Why P/B matters enormously for banks specifically

Banks are legally required to hold minimum regulatory capital as a percentage of risk-weighted assets: Common Equity Tier-1 around 5.5%, total Tier-1 around 7%, total capital around 9%, plus a 2.5% capital conservation buffer. Since book equity is literally the regulatory constraint on how much a bank can lend, P/B (compared against ROE, since a bank's ability to generate returns on that regulatory capital is what matters) is the natural multiple for financial services, far more than P/E.

8.4 When NOT to use P/B

§

Avoid P/B for FMCG and pharmaceutical companies. These businesses are typically asset-light (brand equity, R& D, and distribution networks drive value, not physical book assets), so book value tells you very little about what the business is actually worth. Recall Module 1.3's question: "which statement do you pick up first?" For FMCG and pharma, the answer is the income statement, not the balance sheet, so P/E (or P/S) beats P/B here.

09 Revenue Based

Price to Sales Ratio (P/S)

Useful when a company has no earnings at all, but easy to game with leverage and cash.

9.1 Definition and formula

Price/Sales, decomposed
P/S=(P/E) × (E/S)=P/E × Net Profit Margin

So P/S inherits P/E's drivers (ROE, growth, r) and adds one more: net margin. Two companies with the same P/E but different margins will trade at very different P/S ratios, and vice versa.

9.2 The trap: leverage distorts P/S badly

Same assets, same sales, different leverage
Levered Company Unlevered Company
Total assets $1, 000m $1, 000m
Debt $600m $0m
Equity $400m $1, 000m
Sales $2, 000m $2, 000m
P/S 0.2x 0.5x

The levered company looks far "cheaper" on P/S, purely because its equity base is smaller (debt has funded most of the assets), not because it is actually a better business. P/S completely ignores capital structure, which is exactly the kind of comparability failure Module 5's second principle warns about.

Cash holdings distort P/S too
High-cash Unlevered Low-cash Unlevered
Operating assets $1, 000m $1, 000m
Cash $1, 000m $100m
Equity $2, 000m $1, 100m
Sales $1, 000m $1, 000m
P/S 2.0x 1.1x

Now the company with less cash looks cheaper, even though both companies have identical operating businesses. A pile of idle cash inflates the equity base and drags the P/S ratio up, making an otherwise identical business look artificially expensive.

10 Cash Flow Proxy

EV/EBITDA

A "crude proxy for operating cash flow" that Charlie Munger dismissed as unreliable, and that the LBO industry loves.

10.1 What EBITDA is trying to approximate, and why it falls short

EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortization) strips out financing costs, tax, and non-cash accounting charges to approximate the cash a business generates from operations. It became especially popular during the leveraged-buyout wave, where the key question is simply "how many years of this cash flow will it take to repay the acquisition debt," giving rise to the widely used Debt/EBITDA ratio.

But EBITDA ignores three things that are very real cash costs: capital expenditure, working capital investment, and interest and taxes actually paid. Buffett and Munger have both been openly dismissive of the metric for exactly this reason: interest and tax obligations are genuine cash outflows, and a business with heavy ongoing capex needs is not nearly as cash-generative as its EBITDA alone suggests.

Concept check

Company A and Company B report identical EBITDA. Company A requires heavy annual capex to sustain its business; Company B needs almost none. Should they trade at the same EV/EBITDA multiple?

No. Company B converts far more of its EBITDA into distributable free cash flow, so it deserves a higher multiple. This is precisely why some analysts prefer EV/EBITDA-minus-capex or EV/FCF for capital-intensive sectors like energy and utilities.

10.2 Using EV/EBITDA to estimate terminal value

Beyond a DCF's explicit forecast period, a company is assumed to settle into a steady state (low growth, high payout). Analysts commonly apply a peer-derived terminal EV/EBITDA multiple to the forecast' s terminal-year EBITDA to estimate the value of everything beyond the explicit forecast horizon, rather than relying purely on a Gordon-growth terminal value formula.

11 Peer Selection

Finding real comparable companies

A multiple is only as good as the peer set behind it. This is where most relative valuations quietly go wrong.

11.1 Three ways industries get classified

  • Products and services supplied ("sector" groups related industries, e.g. Healthcare covers pharma, biotech, medical devices, and hospitals).
  • Business-cycle sensitivity: cyclical (auto, housing, basic materials, industrials) vs non-cyclical (food and beverage, personal care, healthcare, utilities).
  • Statistical similarity: cluster analysis on financial characteristics rather than official industry labels (a real dendrogram of Sensex companies groups Tata Steel closer to Adani Ports than to other steel-adjacent names, purely on the statistics).

11.2 The commercial classification systems you'll actually see

System Built by Hierarchy
GICS S& P and MSCI Barra Company > sub-industry > industry > industry group > sector
Russell Global Sectors Russell / FTSE Company > industry > sub-sector > sector
ICB Dow Jones and FTSE Company > sub-sector > sector > super sector > industry

11.3 Ten broad sectors worth knowing by name

Basic Materials & Processing, Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Energy, Financial Services, Health Care, Industrial/Producer Durables, Technology, Telecommunications, and Utilities.

11.4 Building a peer list, step by step

  1. Check commercial classification systems (GICS, Russell, ICB).
  2. Read the target company's own annual report; management often names its competitors.
  3. Read competitors' annual reports for the same reason.
  4. Confirm each "comparable" actually derives a significant share of revenue and operating income from a business genuinely similar to the target's primary business.
Concept check

Two companies are in the same industry classification. One sells premium products in urban markets; the other sells low-margin products in rural markets. Are they automatically comparable?

No. Same industry label does not guarantee similar growth, risk, and profitability, which are the actual three determinants of any multiple (Module 5.2). Same-industry peers still need a real profitability/growth/risk check before you trust a multiple transferred between them.

11.5 The ten-question final checklist

§

1. Is the numerator consistent with the denominator?
2. Is the denominator positive and meaningful?
3. Is the denominator normalized?
4. Are peers truly comparable?
5. Are accounting policies comparable?
6. Are growth, risk, and profitability similar?
7. Are capital structures different?
8. Are there one-off events?
9. Are we using mean, median, or a specifically selected multiple?
10. Can the final number be explained economically?

12 Exam Prep

PYQ Bank and a mixed mock test

Real questions pulled directly from the slide decks and the textbook chapter, in the order they actually appeared, followed by a mixed 15-question mock test covering everything above.

12.1 PYQ Bank: concept-check and discussion questions from the source material

These are open-ended by design (the way Prof. Mohanty actually poses them). Try to answer out loud or on paper before revealing the model approach.

Day 3 slides · multiplier basics

A company has market capitalization of Rs.5, 000 crore, debt of Rs.2, 000 crore, cash of Rs.500 crore, and EBITDA of Rs.1, 000 crore. Which multiple is correct?

Show approach
Enterprise Value/EBITDA. EV=Mcap + Debt - Cash=5, 000+2, 000-500=Rs.6, 500cr. EV/EBITDA=6.5x. EBITDA is a firm-level number, so it must be divided into a firm-level value, not market cap alone.
Day 3 slides · EV/EBITDA

Company A and Company B have the same EBITDA. Company A needs heavy annual capex; Company B needs very little. Should they trade at the same EV/EBITDA?

Show approach
No. Company B converts more of its EBITDA to free cash flow, so it deserves a higher multiple. EV/EBITDA ignores capex intensity entirely, which is its biggest blind spot.
Day 3 slides · comparables

Two companies are in the same industry. One sells premium products in urban markets, the other low-margin products in rural markets. Are they automatically comparable?

Show approach
No. Same industry classification does not guarantee similar growth, risk, and profitability, the three actual determinants of a multiple. Always verify these three before transferring a multiple between "peers."
Day 3 slides · EPS normalization

A cement company reports unusually high earnings this year because of a temporary supply shortage. Should you use current EPS for P/E valuation?

Show approach
No, normalize. Use either the historical average EPS over a full cycle, or (better) the average ROE over a cycle applied to the current book value per share, since that reflects the business's present size.
Day 3 slides · basic vs diluted EPS

Can we compute the P/E multiple by dividing market capitalization directly by total net income (PAT) of a company?

Show approach
Only if PAT is fully attributable to the ordinary equity shareholders whose shares make up that market cap, with no minority interest or preference dividend distortions, and the implied share count is consistent on both sides of the ratio.
Day 1-2 slides · growth and value

Net worth=$100m, ROE=15%, payout ratio=80%, shares=3m, required return=16%. What happens to the stock price if the growth rate increases to 5%?

Show approach
ROE (15%) is below r (16%), so any growth here destroys value, the price falls. Compute: base case g=15%× 20%=3%, E1=15, D1=12, P0=12/(0.16-0.03)=Rs.92.31m, or Rs.30.77/share. At g=5% (retention raised to 33.3%, so D1=15× 0.667=10), P0=10/(0.16-0.05)=Rs.90.9m, or Rs.30.3/share. Price falls slightly, consistent with ROE < r.
Day 1-2 slides · growth and value

Net worth=$100m, ROE=20%, payout ratio=60%, shares=2m, required return=18%. What will be the stock price if it wants to grow at 10%?

Show approach
Retention needed for 10% growth=10/20=50%, so payout must fall to 50%. E1=$20m, D1=$10m. P0=10/(0.18-0.10)=$125m. Price/share=125/2=$62.50.
Day 5-6 slides · source of value

Net Worth=$100m, ROE=15%, required return=15%, shares=2m, growth=0. Why does the stock price NOT change when expected growth increases to 5%?

Show approach
Because ROE exactly equals r. Reinvested capital earns precisely what shareholders require, no more and no less, so shifting earnings between "paid out now" and "reinvested for growth" is value-neutral. Numerically, both g=0% and g=5% give P0=$100m, price=$50/share.
Day 5-6 slides · HUL, multi-stage DDM

HUL's TTM EPS is Rs.43.83, DPS was Rs.41. Analysts expect 5-year EPS growth of 10%. Required return = 9.5%. Terminal growth = 5% after year 5. ROE = 24.9%. Find intrinsic value per share today.

Show approach (multi-stage DDM)

This needs a two-stage DDM: grow the dividend at 10% for 5 explicit years, then apply Gordon growth (at 5%) to get a terminal value at the end of year 5, then discount everything back at 9.5%.

D1 through D5 (growing at 10% from D0=41): 45.10, 49.61, 54.57, 60.03, 66.03.
Terminal value at end of year 5=D5× 1.05 / (0.095-0.05)=69.33/0.045 ≈ Rs.1, 540.7.
Discount factors at 9.5% for years 1-5: 0.913, 0.834, 0.762, 0.695, 0.635.
PV of dividends ≈ 41.2+41.4+41.6+41.8+41.9 ≈ Rs.207.8.
PV of terminal value ≈ 1, 540.7× 0.635 ≈ Rs.978.6.
Intrinsic value ≈ Rs.207.8 + Rs.978.6 ≈ Rs.1, 186 per share. Recompute this one yourself on paper or in a spreadsheet; multi-stage problems like this are exactly where small rounding differences creep in, and your professor will want to see the full method, not just a final number.

Day 4 slides · MM with tax

A zero-growth unlevered company has NOI=Rs.250m, Ke=16%, 20m shares, tax=30%. It borrows Rs.800m: Rs.500m to fund a project with NPV=Rs.100m, and Rs.300m to buy back shares. Find the price of the stock after the announcement.

Show approach
Base price=(250× 0.70)/0.16/20m=Rs.54.69/share. Value created=NPV of project + tax shield on new debt=100 + 0.30× 800=Rs.340m, spread over the original 20m shares=+Rs.17/share. New price ≈ Rs.71.69/share, both immediately on announcement and after the buyback executes.
Textbook Chapter 1 · Questions for Discussion

Jolly wants to open a pizza outlet in the IIT Mumbai campus. How can she price the pizzas using the absolute valuation method? How can she use the relative valuation method? Which makes more sense here?

Show approach
Absolute: build up her per-pizza cost (ingredients, labor, rent allocation) and add a target margin. Relative: check what nearby campus pizza outlets charge and price close to that. In a competitive, price-transparent market like a campus food court, relative pricing usually makes more practical sense, since customers already have a strong reference price in mind (exactly like Rajiv's barber shop in Module 2).
Textbook Chapter 1 · Questions for Discussion

A dot-com company, idly-vada.com, does door delivery of South Indian snacks in Bangalore. In its first year it generated net sales of Rs.3, 500 and a net loss of Rs.28, 000. You want to sell the company after one year. Which valuation method would you use?

Show approach
P/E is unusable (negative earnings). Relative valuation on sales (EV/Sales or P/S) is the natural fallback for an early-stage, pre-profit company, exactly the "Technology sector" guidance from Module 5.4, provided you can find any comparable early-stage peers. A pure DCF is very hard here too, given how little historical data exists to project future cash flows confidently.
Textbook Chapter 1 · Questions for Discussion (WhatsApp)

Each WhatsApp user pays a yearly rental of $1 (first year free). There were 450 million MAUs when Facebook acquired it. Cost of capital=8%. Users reach 1 billion by end of 2016, grow 50% p.a. till 2020, then 25% till 2025, then 10% till 2035, with a 5% terminal growth after that. What is a fair value of WhatsApp?

Show approach
This is a pure multi-stage DCF on a per-user revenue proxy: build a year-by-year user count forecast through each growth phase (50%, then 25%, then 10%, then a 5% terminal growth Gordon-growth value), multiply users by the $1 rental (net of the first free year) to get a cash flow stream, and discount everything at 8%. The exercise is really testing whether you can chain together three explicit growth phases plus a terminal value, the same skill as the HUL problem above, just applied to a "revenue per user" driver instead of EPS. Facebook's actual $19-22bn price implied roughly 63x trailing sales, which the textbook flags as very difficult to justify with any standard method, a useful reminder that real M&A prices sometimes reflect strategic value (data, user base, competitive removal) that no standard multiple captures cleanly.
