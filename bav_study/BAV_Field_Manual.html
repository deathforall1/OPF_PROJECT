The spine of the whole course

If you remember nothing else, remember this page. Every question in every quiz this term has been a variation on one of the four ideas below.

The one equation
Value = Σ (Cash flow to a claim) ÷ (1 + rate that matches that claim's risk)t

Everything else — FCF, FCFE, CCF, APV, DDM, EVA, the multiples, the VC method — is this equation with a different definition of “cash flow to a claim” and a matching definition of “rate”. They are not competing methods. They are the same method wearing different clothes, and they must all give the same answer.

1 · Consistency beats accuracy

He wrote it himself: “No one can prove that assumptions are correct… the focus should be to justify the assumptions rather than trying to prove that they are correct.” A model with debatable inputs but no internal contradiction outranks a model with a “right” answer and a hidden contradiction.

2 · Growth is never free

If a company grows, it must reinvest. g = ROIC × Investment Rate. Any projection that shows growth with zero net investment is wrong before you check a single number — and he has now tested exactly this in Quiz 3.

3 · Count every asset and claim once

If an asset's income is inside your cash flow, do not add the asset back at the end. If a liability is inside your cost of capital, you must subtract it from enterprise value. Never both. Never neither.

4 · The multiple is an output

You compute value first, then look at what multiple your own value implies, then compare with peers. You never start from “the market says 20×”. In his framing, the multiplier is the result of valuation, not its cause.

Part I

Ground zero

Written for someone who has genuinely never done this. If you already know what a balance sheet is, skim to Part II — but read §1.3 and §1.4 anyway, because every later formula is built on them.

1.1 What a company is made of, and why anyone bothers

A company is a machine that takes money in and, if it works, spits out more money later. Two groups hand over the money to build the machine:

  • Lenders give money and are promised a fixed return (interest) and their money back on a date. They get paid first. Their upside is capped: however well the company does, they get their interest and nothing more.
  • Shareholders (equity) give money and are promised nothing at all. They get whatever is left after everybody else has been paid. That leftover is unlimited on the upside and zero on the downside — never negative, because of limited liability.

Limited liability is the whole reason equity investing exists. If a shareholder could be chased for a company's debts, nobody would buy shares in anything risky. Because the worst case is “my investment goes to zero”, ordinary people can own a slice of a steel plant.

That asymmetry — capped return for lenders, residual and unlimited for shareholders — drives every result in this course. It is why the cost of equity is higher than the cost of debt. It is why adding debt makes equity riskier. It is why equity can be seen as a call option on the firm's assets.

Value of the firm = Value of what the lenders own + Value of what the shareholders own
Enterprise Value = Debt (market value) + Equity (market value)

Read that line backwards and you have the single most useful rearrangement in the course:

Equity value = Enterprise value − Debt (+ non-operating assets, − other claims — the full bridge comes in §4.7)

1.2 Reading the three statements in sixty seconds each

The balance sheet — a photograph

Taken on one date. Left side: what the company owns (assets). Right side: who has a claim on it (liabilities and equity). The two sides are equal by construction, which is the only reason it is called a “balance” sheet.

Assets — what the money boughtClaims — where the money came from
Non-current: factories, machines, land, buildings (“net fixed assets” = cost minus accumulated depreciation); investments in other companies; goodwillEquity: share capital + reserves & surplus (all the profit ever earned and not paid out)
Current: inventory, trade receivables (customers who owe you), cash and bank balancesNon-current liabilities: long-term borrowings, deferred tax, long-term provisions
Current liabilities: trade payables (suppliers you owe), short-term borrowings, provisions

The single distinction that matters most for us is not current-versus-non-current. It is interest-bearing versus non-interest-bearing. Borrowings carry an explicit interest cost; trade payables usually do not. That distinction decides what goes into your cost of capital and what gets subtracted from enterprise value. We return to it in §4.3, where it is worth about a third of a Quiz 3 paper.

The profit & loss — a film

Covers a period, usually a year. Read it as a ladder, and memorise the ladder, because half the exam vocabulary lives on it:

Revenue (net sales)
− Cost of materials, employee costs, other operating expenses
= EBITDA Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation
− Depreciation & amortisation
= EBIT also called Net Operating Income, or “operating profit”
− Interest
= PBT Profit Before Tax
− Tax
= PAT Profit After Tax, a.k.a. net income, a.k.a. “earnings” — the E in P/E

Notice where interest sits. Everything above interest belongs to all providers of capital (lenders and shareholders together). Everything below interest belongs to shareholders alone. That single fact is why FCF is discounted at WACC and FCFE at Ke.

Depreciation, and the lie it tells

A machine costs ₹100 crore and lasts 10 years. The accountant spreads that ₹100 crore across ten years at ₹10 crore a year — straight-line depreciation. It is a bookkeeping convention, not a measurement. The machine does not actually lose exactly ₹10 crore of usefulness each year; typically it loses much more in year 1 and much less in year 8.

Two consequences you will be asked about:

  • Depreciation is an expense that involves no cash going out. The cash went out when the machine was bought. So when we build cash flow, we add depreciation back.
  • Because net fixed assets shrink mechanically every year while profits do not, accounting ROCE drifts upward over an asset's life for reasons that have nothing to do with performance. Do not over-read a rising ROCE trend.

The cash flow statement — the referee

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. The cash flow statement reconciles the two, in three buckets: operating, investing, financing. In this course you will usually build cash flow yourself from the P&L and balance sheet rather than lifting it from the statement, because you need it defined your way (see §4.8).

1.3 Time value, and the two shortcuts you will use in every problem

₹100 today is worth more than ₹100 next year, for two reasons that have nothing to do with inflation: you could have invested it, and the future is uncertain. So future money is discounted.

PV = CFt ÷ (1 + r)t

Two special cases collapse an infinite series into a single division. You will use them more than any other formulas in the course.

Shortcut 1 — the level perpetuity

A fixed amount C, every year, forever, starting one year from now:

PV = C ÷ r

₹80 a year forever at 17.391% is worth 80 ÷ 0.17391 = ₹460. That is the OLP Limited example you will meet three times in this manual.

Shortcut 2 — the growing perpetuity (Gordon)

An amount that starts at C1 next year and grows at a constant rate g forever:

PV = C1 ÷ (r − g)   requires r > g, always

Two traps live inside this formula and both have been examined:

  • The numerator is next year's cash flow. If you are given this year's, you must grow it: C1 = C0(1+g).
  • If g ≥ r the formula returns a negative or infinite number. That is not a “high valuation”; it means the assumption set is impossible. No company grows faster than its discount rate forever.
Sir's trap · the (1+g) that goes missing

Terminal value at the end of year 5 is FCF5 × (1+g) ÷ (WACC − g), not FCF5 ÷ (WACC − g). Then that value sits at the end of year 5, so it is discounted back by five periods, not six. Getting one of these two right and the other wrong is the most common arithmetic slip in the class, and it is worth a full mark every time.

1.4 Risk, return, and why only some risk gets paid for

Investors dislike uncertainty, so riskier assets must offer higher expected returns or nobody buys them. But — and this is the idea people find genuinely counter-intuitive — not all risk earns a return.

Split the risk in any one stock into two pieces:

  • Systematic (market) risk: things that move all companies together — interest rates, GDP, war, the monsoon. You cannot escape this by owning more stocks.
  • Unsystematic (specific) risk: the CEO resigns, a factory burns, a drug trial fails. Own forty stocks and these average out to roughly nothing.

Because a diversified investor can eliminate specific risk for free, the market will not pay them for bearing it. Only systematic risk is compensated. That is the entire content of CAPM:

Ke = Rf + β × (E[Rm] − Rf)
required return = risk-free rate + (how much market risk you carry) × (the price of one unit of market risk)

β = 1 means the stock moves with the market. β = 1.7 means a 10% market move tends to produce a 17% move in the stock. β = 0.5 means a defensive stock.

Consistency check · hold on to this one

CAPM prices only systematic risk because it assumes the investor is diversified. Remove that assumption — a promoter with 90% of her wealth in one private textile business — and CAPM understates the return she needs. That single crack is the entire foundation of Part V's private-company block, so notice it now.

Check yourself · Part I

A company's EBIT is ₹200m, interest ₹20m, tax rate 30%. What is PAT, and what is NOPAT?

PAT = (200 − 20) × 0.7 = ₹126m. NOPAT = 200 × 0.7 = ₹140m.

The ₹14m gap is the interest tax shield (20 × 30%). NOPAT deliberately ignores the financing decision; PAT does not. That gap is what APV in Part III values separately.

Why can a diversified investor demand a lower return than an undiversified one on the same company?

Because the diversified investor's specific risk has been averaged away in a portfolio, so the only risk left to compensate is systematic. The undiversified investor bears total risk — systematic and specific — and will price it accordingly. Same asset, different required return, different value. Part V makes this worth roughly six times the equity value in the Kavita Textiles case.

Terminal-year FCF is ₹100m, WACC 12%, g 4%. What is the terminal value?

100 × 1.04 ÷ (0.12 − 0.04) = 104 ÷ 0.08 = ₹1,300m. If you wrote 100 ÷ 0.08 = 1,250 you dropped the (1+g).

Part II · Quiz 1 territory

Relative valuation and the multiples

The first block of the course. It looks like the easy part and it is not: he examines multiples as algebra, never as lookups. Every question here reduces to “what drives this ratio, and what happens to it when one driver moves?”

2.1 Two honest ways to price anything

Suppose you want to price a flat in Jamshedpur. You have exactly two defensible approaches.

Absolute — compute it yourself

Estimate the rent the flat will earn every year for the rest of its life, decide what return you need, discount, add up. You have now produced a number that does not depend on anyone else's opinion. This is DCF, and he calls it “the only scientific method of valuing the company” in the absence of real options.

Relative — borrow someone's opinion

Find three similar flats sold last month at ₹9,000 per square foot. Multiply. Fast, intuitive, and completely dependent on the market having priced those flats correctly. This is the multiplier approach.

There is a third, which he mentions and dismisses: the historical cost approach — what did it cost to build? Useful for insurance, nearly useless for valuation, because cost bears no reliable relation to earning power.

Relative valuation is not wrong. It is a relative statement: it tells you what this asset is worth if the comparables are fairly priced. When the whole sector is mispriced, every multiple in it is mispriced together, and the method cannot see it.

2.3 Gordon, growth, and where growth actually comes from

Start with the simplest absolute valuation there is: a share is worth the dividends it will pay.

P0 = D1 ÷ (Ke − g) the Dividend Discount Model in Gordon form

Now the question that separates the top of the class from the middle: where does g come from? It is not a wish. A company can only grow by putting profit back into the business.

Retention ratio b = 1 − payout ratio
g = b × ROE growth in profit for an equity-financed firm

and the firm-level version you will use constantly:
g = Investment Rate × ROIC  where IR = Net Investment ÷ NOPAT

Retain nothing and grow at 0%. Retain everything and grow at your ROE. There is no third option, and no company escapes this relationship in steady state.

Sir's trap · g is an output, not a dial

In his own words from the Quiz 3 solution: changing g directly is incorrect, “as it is an input to the model”. If your terminal year has ROIC = 12% and an investment rate of 11.15%, then g is forced to 1.34%. You cannot separately assert g = 3%. When a question hands you g, ROIC and net investment that do not satisfy g = ROIC × IR, the answer is always: one of them is wrong, and you must back out the consistent set.

NPVGO — splitting value into “as is” and “growth”

A neat decomposition he likes, because it forces you to see that growth only adds value when returns exceed the cost of capital.

Value = (EPS1 ÷ Ke) + NPVGO
= value if the company paid out everything and never grew, plus the NPV of all future growth opportunities

If ROE = Ke, then NPVGO = 0: the company can grow all it likes and create nothing, because it earns exactly what its shareholders demand. Growth is only valuable when ROE > Ke (or at firm level, ROIC > WACC). Growth with ROIC < WACC actively destroys value — the faster it grows, the poorer you get.

Worked example · where to invest?

His slide, three companies, each with operating invested capital of ₹100m, cost of capital 15%, projected growth 5%.

CompanyROICSpread (ROIC − WACC)Verdict

B15%0%Growth is value-neutral. Worth exactly ₹100m.

Invest in C. And notice: the answer does not depend on the growth rate being high — it depends on the spread. A company growing at 2% with a 10-point spread beats a company growing at 20% with a negative one.

2.4 Modigliani–Miller: does the financing mix change the value?

MM's insight is best understood as a question about a pizza. If you slice a pizza into six pieces instead of four, do you get more pizza? No. Debt and equity are slices of the same cash flows. In a world with no taxes and no bankruptcy costs, how you slice cannot change the total.

MM Proposition I (no taxes): VL = VU capital structure is irrelevant
MM Proposition II: Ke = ρ + (ρ − Kd) × D/E cost of equity rises with leverage

ρ (rho) is the unlevered cost of equity — the return shareholders would demand if the company had no debt at all. It reflects pure business risk. Proposition II says: as you add debt, the equity that remains is riskier, so Ke rises exactly enough to offset the cheapness of debt, and WACC does not move.

Now add corporate tax, and the pizza actually does get bigger, because interest is tax-deductible and the government's slice shrinks:

MM Proposition I (with tax): VL = VU + t·D for permanent, fixed debt
MM Proposition II (with tax): Ke = ρ + (ρ − Kd)(1 − t) × D/E
Worked example · Quiz 1, Question 4 (his actual paper)

Beta Limited is unlevered, perpetual EBIT ₹200m, cost of equity 12%, tax 20%. It borrows ₹50m and invests it in a project with NPV ₹10m. What is the new enterprise value?

Unlevered value = EBIT(1−t) ÷ ρ = 200 × 0.8 ÷ 0.12  =  1,333.33 ρ = Ke = 12%, since the firm is unlevered
+ Tax shield = D × t = 50 × 0.20  =  10.00
+ Cash raised and still in the firm =  50.00
+ NPV of the new project =  10.00
= New enterprise value 1,403.33

His mark scheme is the interesting part: 3 marks for 1,403.33; 2 marks for 1,353.33 (forgot to add the ₹50m of cash the firm now holds); 1.5 marks for 1,343.33 (forgot the cash and the NPV). He has pre-mapped the two ways students go wrong. Which means: show the build-up line by line — a wrong total with a visible correct structure still earns most of the marks.

Sir's trap · the cash does not vanish

When a firm borrows ₹50m and invests it, the ₹50m is still inside the firm — as a new asset. Students routinely add the tax shield and the NPV but forget the principal itself. Ask: “after this transaction, what does the firm own that it didn't own before?” Answer: the project (worth 50 + 10) and a tax shield (10), funded by 50 of debt.

2.5 The value-driver formula: every multiple in one line

This is the most economical piece of algebra in the course and it generates every multiple result he can ask for. Start from Gordon and substitute in the growth identity.

P0 = D1 ÷ (Ke − g), and D1 = E1 × payout = E1(1 − g/ROE)

P0 = E1 × (1 − g/ROE) ÷ (Ke − g)

Divide both sides by E1  →  P/E = (1 − g/ROE) ÷ (Ke − g)
Divide both sides by BV  →  P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g)

Stare at those last two lines. They contain the answers to at least three of his exam questions:

  • Every multiple has exactly three determinants: growth (g), return quality (ROE or ROIC), and risk (Ke or WACC). Nothing else. If two companies trade at different multiples, one of those three differs — or the market is wrong about one of them.
  • Set g = 0 in the P/E formula and it collapses to P/E = 1 ÷ Ke. The ROE term vanishes completely. So at zero growth, P/E tells you nothing at all about ROE — it only tells you the required return.
  • Set g = 0 in the P/B formula and you get P/B = ROE ÷ Ke. So P/B > 1 ⟺ ROE > Ke. This is the direct answer to Quiz 1 Question 1.
Worked example · Quiz 1, Question 1 (his actual paper)

“Firm A is expected to grow at 0% by the market. Between the P/E ratio and the P/B ratio, which ratio will tell you if its ROE is greater than the required rate of return? Why?”

Answer: the P/B ratio. When g = 0, P/E = 1/Ke regardless of ROE, so P/E carries no information about profitability. P/B, however, equals ROE/Ke, so P/B above 1 tells you directly that ROE exceeds the required return. (1 mark for saying P/B, 1 mark for the justification — his own mark scheme. The bare answer caps at half.)

Worked example · Quiz 1, Question 2

“Is it possible for a stock to trade at a lower price-earnings and a higher price-to-book ratio?”

Yes. Take a company with low growth and high ROE. Low g pushes P/E down (little growth to pay for). High ROE pushes P/B up (each rupee of book equity is generating a lot of profit). A cash-generative, mature, high-return business — a mature FMCG brand with no reinvestment runway — sits exactly there. The two ratios have different sensitivities to the same three drivers, which is precisely why you look at both. (3 marks.)

2.6 P/E, properly

Basic vs diluted

Basic EPS uses shares outstanding today. Diluted EPS assumes every convertible instrument, warrant and ESOP that is currently in-the-money converts into shares. Always use diluted — those claims on future profit are real, and ignoring them flatters the company.

Trailing vs forward

Trailing P/E uses the last twelve months' actual EPS; forward P/E uses next year's estimate. Valuation is about the future, so forward is more relevant — but it now depends on whose estimate you use, which imports someone else's opinion into your “independent” analysis.

Normalising EPS

The E in P/E must be sustainable earnings. Strip out one-offs before you compute anything: asset sale gains, insurance receipts, restructuring charges, litigation settlements, and — his 2026 example — unrealised gains on investment stakes.

Worked example · Alphabet, Q2 2026 (his slide)

Reported diluted GAAP EPS of $9.11 included roughly $99bn of unrealised gains on its SpaceX and Anthropic stakes. Adjusted EPS was $2.85 — which actually missed the $2.89 consensus. A P/E computed on $9.11 is meaningless: the numerator is a share price for an operating business, the denominator has been inflated by a mark-to-market on holdings the company did not sell. Asset consistency, in a headline.

Why P/E is not comparable across capital structures

Price is an equity number; earnings are an after-interest number. Both are affected by leverage, but not proportionately. Two identical businesses with different debt loads will show different P/Es for reasons that have nothing to do with operating quality. EV/EBITDA sidesteps this, which is why it dominates deal-making.

Sir's trap · the regression trap

Damodaran-style, you can regress P/E across a sector on growth, payout and beta, then plug in your company's values to get a “predicted” P/E. It looks rigorous. Two problems he will press on: (i) with 8–12 comparables you are fitting three coefficients to almost no data, so the standard errors are enormous and no coefficient is significant — see Part VI; (ii) the regression assumes the sector as a whole is correctly priced, which is exactly what the exercise was supposed to test. It is circular. Report it as a cross-check, never as the answer.

2.7 PEG and PEGY

PEG = (P/E) ÷ g  where g is expressed as a number, so 20% → 20, not 0.20

The intuition: a P/E of 40 is expensive for a company growing at 5% and cheap for one growing at 50%. PEG normalises for growth. A PEG near 1 is the folk rule for “fairly priced”.

Why he is sceptical of it: PEG assumes P/E is linear in g. It is not. Look again at the value-driver formula — g appears in the numerator (through the payout term) and in the denominator (through Ke − g). The relationship is strongly convex. So PEG systematically flatters high-growth companies and punishes low-growth ones. It also ignores risk entirely: two companies with identical P/E and identical g but very different betas have identical PEG, and are not equally attractive.

PEGY = (P/E) ÷ (g + dividend yield)

PEGY patches the most obvious hole: a mature company returning cash rather than growing is not worse, just different. Adding yield to growth gives a fairer denominator for total shareholder return.

Worked example · DMart's 4.4× PEG (from the guest lecture)

DMart traded at 75× FY22 P/E with 26% earnings CAGR — a PEG of about 4.4×, which sounds indefensible. The analyst's justification: Walmart, Costco and Target carried PEGs of 5.3×, 4.9× and 7.3× respectively, so DMart was actually the cheapest of the set on that measure, while growing faster and carrying net cash against a global average net-debt/equity of 85%. Whether you accept it or not, notice the structure of the argument — a rich multiple is defended by finding the right comparison set, not by asserting quality. That is exactly the move he wants you to be able to critique.

2.8 P/B and the ROE link

P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g)   →   at g = 0: P/B = ROE ÷ Ke

Three readings that are examinable:

  • P/B = 1 means the market thinks the company earns exactly its cost of equity. It creates no value and destroys none.
  • P/B < 1 means the market expects ROE below Ke — every rupee retained is expected to destroy value. Common in public-sector banks and struggling capital-intensive firms.
  • P/B > 1 means ROE > Ke. The gap between P/B and 1 is a direct read on the size of the spread.

When P/B is the right multiple: balance-sheet-driven businesses, where assets are the business — banks, NBFCs, insurers, oil & gas, real estate. For a bank, book value is a meaningful economic quantity and earnings are volatile, so P/B paired with ROE beats P/E. For an asset-light IT services firm, book value is nearly meaningless and P/B is noise.

2.9 Price-to-sales — and the two reasons he bans it

P/S looks appealing: revenue is harder to manipulate than profit, and it exists even when profit is negative. He asked for two reasons not to use it and they are both consistency failures.

Worked example · Quiz 1, Question 3 (his actual paper)

“Give 2 reasons why one should not use the price-to-sales ratio while valuing a stock.”

  1. Leverage. The numerator (price) is an equity claim; the denominator (sales) belongs to the whole firm. Two companies with identical sales and identical operations but different debt will show different P/S for purely financing reasons. You are comparing a part to a whole. (1 mark)
  2. Cash balances. Same defect from the other side. A company sitting on a large cash pile has a higher market cap for a given business, so a higher P/S, without a rupee more of revenue. (1 mark)

The general principle, which is worth more than the specific answer: a multiple must have a numerator and a denominator that belong to the same claimants. Equity numerator ⟶ equity denominator (price with EPS, price with book value). Firm numerator ⟶ firm denominator (EV with EBITDA, EV with sales, EV with invested capital). Mixing them is the multiplier-consistency violation, and EV/Sales is the fixed version of P/S.

2.10 EV/EBITDA

EV/EBITDA = (Market cap + Debt − Cash) ÷ EBITDA

The industry workhorse, and the multiple most M&A deals are actually struck on. Its virtues: it is capital-structure neutral (both numerator and denominator are pre-financing), it is unaffected by different depreciation policies, and EBITDA is a rough cash proxy.

Its defects, which he will want you to state:

  • EBITDA ignores capital intensity entirely. A steel plant and a software firm with the same EBITDA are not equally valuable, because one must spend most of that EBITDA on maintenance capex just to stand still. Buffett's line — that treating EBITDA as cash flow means believing the tooth fairy pays for capex — is the whole objection.
  • It ignores working capital, which for a jeweller like Titan is where the cash actually goes.
  • Ind AS 116 broke comparability. Since operating leases moved onto the balance sheet, rent no longer sits in operating expenses — it splits into depreciation and interest, both of which are below EBITDA. So EBITDA jumps for any lease-heavy business (retail, aviation, cinemas) with zero change in economics, and multiples computed across the transition are not comparable. This appeared explicitly in the guest lecture and is very examinable.
Sir's trap · EBITTDA, 2026 edition

Quiz 3 asked whether analysts should use EBITTDA — Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Tokens, Depreciation and Amortisation — i.e. adding back AI token spend. His own answer key:

  • For: in the early AI-adoption phase, token spend is exploratory — companies are buying API calls to discover what AI can do for them. That is economically closer to R&D than to cost of goods sold, and R&D-like spending is conventionally treated as investment rather than operating cost.
  • Against: once AI is embedded in the business process, the revenue is already higher because of it. Excluding the corresponding cost breaks the matching concept in accounting. Second, measuring performance on EBITTDA encourages profligate token usage — you have removed the manager's incentive to be efficient.

Marking: one argument each way earns 2 of 3; matching the reasoning above earns the third. The lesson generalises — when he asks for “one argument for and one against”, the structure is worth most of the marks, so always give exactly one of each, clearly labelled.

2.11 Finding comparables, and adjusting them

A multiple is only as good as the peer set. His criteria, and the guest lecturer's, agree on what actually matters — and it is not the industry code:

  • Same business model and drivers, not the same SIC/GICS label. AI-based screening is now used precisely because static industry codes group companies that share nothing economically.
  • Similar growth, margin and risk profile. The value-driver formula says these are the only three things that set a multiple, so peers must match on them.
  • Similar size and liquidity. Cost of equity is a negative function of size (Fama–French, 1993), so a ₹500 crore company does not deserve a ₹50,000 crore company's multiple.

Then adjust the peer multiple for the specific differences you could not screen away. The guest lecture's list, which is a good checklist: illiquidity, size, growth, client concentration, group-company dependency, revenue model and mix, economies of scale, margin profile, and control.

Exam angle · what drives a premium or discount

Premium: superior scalability (5-year revenue and EBITDA CAGR), pricing power, intangibles/brand, high ROCE, efficient fixed-asset turnover, short working-capital cycle, high cash conversion. Discount: margin contraction, lagging growth, weak future potential, client concentration, promoter dependency, a sunset industry. Learn this as two lists — a “what drives the multiple” question is a near-certainty.

Check yourself · Part II

A company has ROE of 25%, Ke of 12%, and retains 40% of earnings. What is g, and what is its P/B?

g = b × ROE = 0.40 × 0.25 = 10%.

P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g) = (0.25 − 0.10) ÷ (0.12 − 0.10) = 0.15 ÷ 0.02 = 7.5×.

Note how violently sensitive that is: nudge Ke to 13% and P/B falls to 5.0×. When (Ke − g) is small, everything is fragile — which is the honest reason high-growth valuations swing so hard.

Two cement companies, identical operations. One has ₹1,000cr of debt, the other none. Which multiple would you compare them on, and why?

EV/EBITDA. Both numerator and denominator are pre-financing, so the comparison is unaffected by the capital-structure difference. P/E would be distorted: the levered firm has lower earnings (after interest) and a lower market cap, but not in the same proportion. P/S would be distorted for the same reason as P/E.

Why is a terminal growth rate of 8% incompatible with a terminal EV/EBITDA of 5×?

Multiplier consistency. A company growing at 8% forever is a company with a long runway and, presumably, returns above its cost of capital — the market prices such businesses at high multiples. An EV/EBITDA of 5× is what the market pays for a no-growth or declining business. The two statements describe different companies. Compute the multiple your Gordon terminal value implies and check it against real steady-state peers; if it looks absurd, your g or your WACC is wrong.

Now we compute the number ourselves

Discounted cash flow. The core claim of this part is unusual and worth stating plainly: FCF, FCFE, CCF and APV are not four methods. They are one method, and if your four answers differ, three of them — possibly all four — are wrong.

3.1 From accrual profit to actual cash

Profit is not cash. Four adjustments turn one into the other, and each has a reason.

Start:  EBIT × (1 − t)  =  NOPAT tax on operating profit, as if there were no debt
+ Depreciation & amortisation
= Gross Cash Flow
− Capital expenditure
− Increase in operating working capital
(+ increase in other non-interest-bearing liabilities)
= Free Cash Flow (to the firm)

Why we tax EBIT and not PBT

FCF is supposed to be the cash the business generates, before deciding how to finance it. If we taxed PBT, the interest deduction would sneak the financing decision into the cash flow — and then we would double-count it, because WACC already contains the (1 − t) on debt. Tax EBIT, keep financing out of the numerator, and put it entirely in the discount rate. That is risk consistency in one sentence.

Sir's trap · why is depreciation alone added back?

This was Quiz 3, Question 2, worth 5 marks, and the answer is not “because it is a non-cash expense” — that is only half of it. His own key:

Capex is measured as:  Change in Net Fixed Assets + Depreciation
(because NFA fell by depreciation during the year, so gross spend = the increase you can see + the fall you cannot)

So depreciation has already been added into the capex figure that you are about to subtract. Adding depreciation back to NOPAT cancels that effect. The two moves are a matched pair. If you added back depreciation but computed capex as “change in NFA” only, you would be double-counting; if you computed capex correctly but did not add depreciation back, you would be under-counting. The right answer explains the cancellation, not the non-cash-ness.

Why capex is subtracted and working capital matters

Capex is real money leaving the building — for machines you will use for a decade. It never touches the P&L in the year it is spent (only its depreciation does, over ten years), so cash flow must catch it directly.

Working capital is subtler. Sell ₹100 on 60-day credit and profit rises by ₹100 while cash rises by nothing. Buy inventory for ₹50 and cash falls with no effect on profit. So growth in receivables and inventory consumes cash; growth in payables releases it.

Operating working capital = Operating current assets − Operating current liabilities
Operating current assets = inventory + receivables + other receivables (excluding surplus cash)
Operating current liabilities = payables + other current liabilities (excluding short-term borrowings)
Sir's trap · borrowings are not working capital

Short-term borrowings sit in current liabilities on the balance sheet, but they are interest-bearing — they are debt, a source of capital, and they belong in the cost of capital and in the EV-to-equity bridge, not inside working capital. Leave them in working capital and you have double-counted the financing. Titan's 2023 current liabilities of ₹11,521 crore fall to ₹5,018 crore of operating current liabilities once ₹6,503 crore of borrowings comes out.

The logic of CCF is elegant and he tests it. The tax shield is a real cash flow that the firm keeps. You can either (a) leave it out of the numerator and account for it by reducing the discount rate — that is FCF at WACC, where the (1−t) on Kd does the work; or (b) put it in the numerator explicitly and then not reduce the discount rate — that is CCF at pre-tax WACC. If your FCF-based equity value and your FCFE-based equity value differ, run this identity year by year and the mismatch will show itself. Nine times out of ten the cause is the same: you assumed a constant WACC (which requires a constant D/E) while holding debt fixed in rupees. A constant D/E with growth g means debt must grow at g, so net new borrowing is g × D every year. If that line is missing from your FCFE, the two methods cannot agree.

Worked example · Quiz 2, Question 2 — 5 marks, three sentences

“When a firm invests in a positive NPV project, it immediately changes the cost of capital of the firm. Why?”

The equity value rises by the NPV of the project the moment the market learns of it. Debt is unchanged. So the debt-to-equity ratio falls. A lower D/E means less financial risk borne by equity, so the cost of equity falls (MM Proposition II), and the weights shift toward the now-cheaper equity — so the weighted average cost of capital falls too. That is the whole answer. Three sentences, five marks.

3.4 WACC, derived rather than memorised

WACC is not a formula handed down from above; it is a weighted average of what each provider of money demands.

WACC = Σj Kj × (Aj / V) his book's Equation 3(1) — each source at its own cost, weighted by its share of total market value

Why market-value weights, not book

His own example: Titan's book value per share is ₹230.19; the shares trade around ₹4,629. Titan's cost of equity is about 12%. Now ask — 12% of what? An investor who can sell today at ₹4,629 expects a 12% return on ₹4,629, not on ₹230. The book number is a historical accounting record of what was contributed decades ago; nobody's required return is calculated on it.

He puts the same point as a question: X invested ₹100 in 2002; book value of that stake is now ₹300 but market value is ₹1,000. Why should X care about the market value? Because ₹1,000 is what X gives up by not selling. The opportunity cost is the market value, so the return must be earned on the market value.

Why the debt term carries (1 − t)

Interest is deductible. A company paying 10% on debt with a 30% tax rate really only bears 7%, because the tax bill falls by 3%. Equity dividends are not deductible, so no such adjustment applies to Ke.

3.5 Adding growth: the clause hidden inside "constant discount rate"

Growth costs money. In the cash flow it appears as net investment:

Net investment = Capex + ΔWorking capital − Depreciation   = Gross investment − Depreciation
FCF = NOPAT − Net investment

Now the clause. When you write “WACC = 12%, constant, forever”, you have quietly asserted that the D/E ratio is constant forever. And if equity is growing at g while D/E is fixed, debt must also grow at g. Which means every year the firm borrows an extra g × D — and that borrowing is cash in to the shareholders, so it must appear in FCFE.

Sir's trap · the most common error in his course

Growing FCFE, discounted at a constant Ke, with debt held constant in rupee terms. It cannot be right: if debt is fixed while the firm grows, leverage falls every year, so Ke falls every year, so a constant Ke is wrong. Either (a) let debt grow at g and keep the rates constant, or (b) hold debt fixed and use a schedule of changing Ke and WACC — which is exactly when you reach for APV instead.

3.6 Terminal value

You cannot forecast line items for eternity, so you forecast explicitly for N years and then collapse everything after that into one number sitting at the end of year N.

TVN = FCFN × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g)  then  PV = TVN ÷ (1 + WACC)N

Choosing g

Terminal g is the growth rate of a company that has reached steady state. It is bounded above by the nominal growth rate of the economy it operates in — a firm growing faster than its economy forever eventually becomes the economy. Build it multiplicatively, which is a detail he specifically flags:

g = (1 + real GDP growth) × (1 + inflation) − 1
5% real and 5% inflation is 10.25%, not 10%

Choosing the length of the explicit period

Forecast explicitly until the company plausibly reaches steady state. If ROIC greatly exceeds WACC, or growth is far above steady state, five years is too short — you would be assuming the company drops instantly from 25% growth to 4%. The choice is examinable: he re-values Tata Steel under a different explicit period in an appendix precisely to show that it matters.

The exit-multiple cross-check

Compute the EV/EBITDA that your Gordon terminal value implies, and compare it with what real steady-state companies trade at. Then run it in reverse: what growth rate does a peer's exit multiple imply? If the two views bracket each other, you have multiplier consistency. If your Gordon TV implies 30× EBITDA for a cement plant, something is broken.

3.7 The circularity problem

Here is the snake eating its tail. To compute WACC you need market-value weights. The market value of equity is what you are trying to compute. You need the answer to get the answer.

Four responses, in ascending order of respectability:

  1. Use book-value weights. Wrong, and he will mark it wrong. Never do this silently.
  2. Use the current market capitalisation. Defensible for a listed company, and it is what most practitioners do — but note the tension: you are using the market price as an input to a calculation whose purpose is to test whether the market price is right. Disclose it.
  3. Use a target D/E. Management's stated target, or the industry norm. You only need the ratio, never the rupee values, so the circularity dissolves. Best answer when the firm is migrating to a target structure.
  4. Iterate. His own published method (SSRN 413240). Seed with any equity figure, compute WACC, compute value, take the implied equity, feed it back, repeat until it converges. In Excel: enable iterative calculation, or use Goal Seek to drive (implied equity − assumed equity) to zero.
Consistency check · the elegant escape

Value the equity directly with FCFE at Ke. No weights are needed, so there is no circularity at all. That is precisely why his Quiz 2 solution computed equity first (1,416.25) and only then used it to build the WACC weights. When a question gives you multiple debt instruments at different rates and asks for enterprise value, the FCFE road is nearly always the fast one.

3.8 Adjusted Present Value

APV takes the opposite philosophy to WACC. Instead of blending the financing benefit into the discount rate, it values the business as if it had no debt at all, and then adds the value of the financing separately.

APV = Value of the unlevered firm + PV(interest tax shields) − PV(costs of financial distress)

Step 1 — find ρ, the unlevered cost of equity. Unlever the observed Ke or the observed beta:

Ke = ρ + (ρ − Kd)(1 − t)(D/E)  →  solve for ρ
or via beta:   βU = βL ÷ [1 + (1 − t)(D/E)]  Hamada, debt beta ≈ 0

Step 2 — value the unlevered firm: discount FCF at ρ.

Step 3 — value the tax shield. This is where the marks are, because the correct discount rate depends on the debt policy:

Straight from your class notes on the APV lecture: “Mistake: using the MM equation, based on the assumption that g = 0. When you bring growth into the picture, the riskiness of the tax shield changes.”

With growth:  βL = βU[1 + (1 − t)·D/E] − βD·(D/E)(1 − t)
When g = 0: the (1−t) stays. When debt grows with the firm, drop the (1−t) — the shield is no longer riskless.

Terminal unlevered value = FCFN(1 + g) ÷ (ρ − g)

Practically: when D/E is held constant and the firm grows, the (1−t) comes out of the relevering formula and the shield is discounted at ρ, not Kd.

When APV beats WACC: whenever leverage is changing. An LBO that pays down debt from 70% to 20% over five years has a different WACC every year; using one constant WACC is simply wrong. APV handles it naturally, because the debt schedule enters the shield calculation directly.

3.9 Preference capital and the tax nuance

Preference shares are leverage without the tax break. The dividend is fixed like interest, so it creates the same financial risk for equity — but it is paid out of post-tax profit, so no shield.

  • Preference capital appears in WACC as its own term at its own cost, with no (1 − t).
  • It must be subtracted in the bridge from enterprise value to equity value — liability consistency.
  • In unlevering/relevering, preference behaves like debt for risk purposes but like equity for tax purposes.
WACC = (E/V)Ke + (D/V)Kd(1−t) + (P/V)Kp

3.10 Financing policy in practice — Sampa Video

The Sampa Video case (HBS 201-094) is the applied version of §3.8. Sampa is a Boston video-rental chain considering a home-delivery business: $1.5m up-front, incremental cash flows projected 2002–2006, then 5% growth forever. The case's real question is not “what is the NPV” — it is “how does the financing decision change the answer, and which method should you use?”

Management is choosing between two policies, and the case is constructed so that each demands a different technique:

Policy A — a fixed amount of debt

Raise a set sum, keep it or pay it down on a schedule. The tax shields are then known amounts on known dates, roughly as safe as the debt itself. Use APV, discount the shields at Kd, and if the debt is permanent the shield is simply D × t.

Policy B — a constant debt-to-value ratio

Borrow more as the project succeeds, less if it stumbles. Debt now tracks firm value, so the shields are as risky as the business. Use WACC (or APV with shields at ρ). This is the standard textbook assumption and the one a constant WACC silently requires.

Case inputs worth remembering as a template: Rf 5.0%, project cost of debt 6.8%, market risk premium 7.2%, marginal tax 40%, debt beta 0.25, asset beta 1.50. Note that the asset beta comes from internet-based comparables (Kramer.com, Cityretrieve.com) rather than from Sampa itself — a bottom-up beta, because the new business is not the old business.

Exam angle

“A company plans to pay down its debt from 60% of capital to 20% over five years. Which valuation method would you use and why?” — Answer: APV. A single constant WACC assumes a constant D/V, which is precisely what this company is not doing. APV values the unlevered business at ρ and then values the actual, declining schedule of tax shields year by year at the rate matching the debt policy. Add one line on distress costs if leverage starts high.

Cost of capital, cash flow, and the growth engine

4.1 The four technical points about the cost of capital

These four open his lecture and open the chapter in his book. Learn them as a list; “state the four technical points” is a free five marks.

  1. The pre-tax cost of debt is not the coupon rate. The coupon is a historical promise fixed on issue day. The cost of debt is what the company would pay to borrow today — the yield to maturity.
  2. The cost of equity is not the dividend yield. Shareholders' total return is dividend yield plus capital gains. A company paying no dividend does not have a zero cost of equity.
  3. Weights must be market values, not book values. §3.4 covers why.
  4. The discount rate must match the riskiness and the type of the cash flow. Equity flows at Ke, firm flows at WACC. Nominal flows at nominal rates. Rupee flows at rupee rates.

The shareholders put in ₹379.52m and ended up with ₹689.40m of a company that was worth ₹315m — they are ₹5.12m poorer. The 17% project destroyed value. That proves 17.57% was the true cost of capital and 16.87% was an artefact of using the coupon rate. This is his single most-loved demonstration; the number −5.12 is worth remembering.

Real versus nominal — the tomato

Real rate 3%, inflation 6%. The nominal rate is not 9%. Tomatoes cost ₹100 today and ₹106 next year. To be 3% better off in tomatoes, you need enough money to buy 1.03 tomatoes' worth at next year's price: 1.03 × 106 = ₹109.18.

(1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation)  →  1.03 × 1.06 = 1.0918, so 9.18%

The rule that follows: nominal cash flows at nominal rates, real cash flows at real rates. Both give the same answer if done consistently. Mixing them — forecasting revenue with no price increases and then discounting at a nominal WACC — systematically understates value, and is one of the most common real-world modelling errors.

Rupees or dollars — interest rate parity

Spot ₹80/$, US rate 5%, India rate 7%. Where do you invest $100? Nowhere in particular — the forward rate adjusts so both give the same return:

Forward = Spot × (1 + r₹) ÷ (1 + r$) = 80 × 1.07/1.05 = ₹81.52/$

Hence: value the company in rupees with rupee flows and a rupee discount rate, then convert at the spot rate. Or convert flows to dollars at forward rates and discount at a dollar rate. Both are right; both give the same number. What is wrong is converting rupee flows at the spot rate and discounting at a dollar rate.

4.2 Cost of debt: the pecking order

  1. Find the YTM of the company's traded bonds. Best, and rarely available in India — only 83 of the BSE 500 had borrowed directly from the public as of FY23.
  2. Ask someone who knows. Bloomberg, or the treasury team.
  3. Use the credit rating plus a spread. The practical route. FIMMDA publishes annualised spreads by rating and tenor; add the spread to the matching-tenor G-Sec.
  4. Rate the bonds yourself. Last resort: build a synthetic rating from interest coverage and leverage, then use the spread table.

The FIMMDA spread grid runs from AAA (a handful of basis points at short tenors, ~117bp at 15 years) down to BBB− (450–560bp). The shape matters: spreads widen with tenor and explode below investment grade.

Which tax rate in Kd(1 − t)?

The marginal statutory rate, because the question is “what does the next rupee of interest save us in tax?” Use the cash/effective rate only as a sanity check on history. Under §115BAA the effective rate for most large Indian companies is 25.168% (22% + 10% surcharge + 4% cess); the old regime runs to about 34.94%; §115BAB for eligible new manufacturing is about 17.16%. Verify from the tax note in the annual report — do not assume.

Foreign currency loans

A dollar loan at 6% is not 6% debt for an Indian company. Convert the whole schedule to rupees at expected forward rates and compute the implied rupee IRR. His worked case: US$10,000 at 6%, spot ₹64/$, rupee rate 7%, dollar rate 1% — the implied rupee cost of debt comes out at 12.30%, not 6%. The extra six points are expected depreciation of the rupee.

4.3 ★ Liability consistency — the highest-yield idea in the paper

This is the single rule that generates the most exam questions, and it is beautifully simple. Whatever you treat as a source of capital must be treated as a source of capital everywhere.

If a liability D₁ is a source of capitalIf a liability D₀ is not a source of capital
In WACCInclude it, at its own costExclude it
In the cash flowDo not subtract its interest from FCFDo subtract its interest from FCF, and add any increase in it
In the bridge to equitySubtract it from enterprise valueDo not subtract it

Both columns are correct. What is never correct is mixing them — putting a liability in WACC and then not subtracting it, or subtracting a liability you never charged for.

Worked example · XYZ with two debts (his slide)

XYZ has ₹100m at 12% and ₹200m at 14%. EBIT ₹100m, tax 30%, Ke 20%, g = 0. Three legitimate routes to the same value:

  1. Value equity directly by FCFE. Interest = 12 + 28 = 40. PAT = (100 − 40) × 0.7 = 42. Equity = 42 ÷ 0.20 = ₹210m. No weights, no circularity.
  2. Treat only one debt as a source of capital. Then the other's interest is deducted inside FCF and its principal is not subtracted at the bridge. Different WACC, different enterprise value — same equity value of ₹210m.
  3. Treat both as sources of capital. WACC uses both at their own rates; EV = 210 + 300 = ₹510m; subtract ₹300m at the bridge. Same ₹210m.

Are non-interest-bearing liabilities really free?

His question: a company buys raw material on one year's credit and must pay ₹1,100 crore; its cost of debt is 10%. Is that credit free?

No. The supplier has priced the credit into the invoice. Had the company paid cash it would have paid roughly ₹1,000 crore. The ₹100 crore difference is interest in disguise. So the *economically* right treatment would charge for it — but conventional practice keeps trade payables inside working capital (reducing the investment) rather than in WACC, and the price is that the implicit finance cost is buried in cost of goods sold. Say this explicitly if asked; the marks are in recognising that “non-interest-bearing” is a label, not a fact.

Debt-like items you must not forget

From the practitioner's list — these routinely appear as “what else do you subtract?”: borrowings; unfunded gratuity and leave encashment; stretch creditors (a 60-day credit period being run at 180 days is 120 days of disguised borrowing); non-convertible preference shares; capitalised lease liabilities; and a probability-weighted share of contingent liabilities (estimated financial impact × probability of occurrence).

4.4 What if cash is present? The two treatments

His worked case, and it is examinable in either direction. Zero-growth company: EBIT 100 (excluding interest income), debt 100 at 10%, cash 50 earning 7%, tax 30%, Ke 20%.

First, the anchor — value the equity directly, because that answer cannot depend on the treatment you choose:

PBT = 100 + (50 × 7%) − (100 × 10%) = 100 + 3.5 − 10 = 93.5
Net income = 93.5 × 0.7 = 65.45  →  Equity = 65.45 ÷ 0.20 = 327.25

Treat cash as operating

Interest income stays in the flow. FCF = (100 + 3.5) × 0.7 = 72.45.
EV = 327.25 + 100 = 427.25.
WACC = 16.957%.
Check: 72.45 ÷ 16.957% = 427.25 ✓

Treat cash as non-operating

Interest income excluded. FCF = 100 × 0.7 = 70.
EV = 327.25 + 100 − 50 = 377.25.
WACC = 18.55% (effective Kd on net debt is 13%).
Check: 70 ÷ 18.55% = 377.25 ✓

Consistency check

Two different enterprise values, two different WACCs, one equity value: 327.25. That is asset consistency working. The enterprise value is not a fact about the world — it is a fact about your definition of “the enterprise”. Equity value is the invariant. When an exam answer differs between two treatments, you have broken something.

How to decide: operating cash is the working balance the business genuinely needs to run (a retailer's till float). Surplus cash and marketable securities are non-operating by default, especially when large. Whatever you choose, the same choice must appear in the cash flow, in the beta, and in the bridge.

4.5 CAPM and its three inputs

E(ri) = rf + βi × [E(rm) − rf]

He teaches CAPM while being open about its problems: Fama and French (1992) found beta did not explain stock returns; Martingale Asset Management recommends buying low-risk stocks precisely because the low-beta anomaly persists. The alternatives are APT (you must estimate factor premia) and Fama–French multi-factor models (popular in industry, data readily available — including an Indian dataset published by IIM Ahmedabad).

Input 1 — the risk-free rate

Use the 10-year G-Sec yield. Three complications he raises:

  • Which maturity? CAPM is a one-period model, so strictly the horizon should match the cash flow. In practice we value perpetuities, so a long bond is the least-bad proxy. If the yield curve is not flat, the honest answer is to find the single rate that reproduces the same present value as the full spot curve.
  • Are they really risk-free? India has a sovereign CDS spread — the market charges for the possibility of default. A government can always print rupees, so rupee debt is nominally default-free, but not free of inflation risk.
  • Real or nominal, and which currency? Match the cash flow.
Worked example · the yield curve is not flat

Cash flows 100, 100, 200 in years 1–3; spot rates 5%, 6%, 7%.

Value = 100/1.05 + 100/1.06² + 200/1.07³ = 347.50
Solve 347.50 = 100/(1+r) + 100/(1+r)² + 200/(1+r)³  →  r = 6.52%

The single equivalent yield depends on the shape of the cash flows. Front-loaded flows (100, 10, 10) give 5.49%; back-loaded flows (100, 100, 10000) give 6.99%, approaching the 3-year spot rate. Practical lesson: for a perpetuity-heavy DCF, the long end of the curve is the right anchor.

Input 2 — the market risk premium

Three routes, and no government publishes it:

  • Historical: average realised index return minus the risk-free rate over a long window. Sensitive to the window and to whether you use arithmetic or geometric means.
  • Implied (Damodaran's method): take today's index level and consensus cash flows, solve for the discount rate that makes them equal, subtract rf. Forward-looking and internally consistent, but depends on the consensus forecast. Damodaran publishes an India total equity risk premium of about 7.08%.
  • Convention: the practitioner community works with 6–7% for India. The guest lecturer confirmed the market uses 6% or 7% almost universally.

Two technical points about the market portfolio, straight from his book. In theory it contains all capital assets — shares, bonds, gold, real estate, human capital — and nobody has ever built it. So we use a proxy, which must be (a) broad and (b) value-weighted. And because most indices are price indices, not total-return indices, they exclude dividends: add the dividend yield back when computing a historical premium from a price index, or you understate it by the whole yield.

Input 3 — beta. See §4.6, which is a section on its own.

4.6 Beta, start to finish

Step 1 — compute returns

Arithmetic or logarithmic. Use adjusted prices, not closing prices, and adjust for dividends, bonus issues, splits and rights issues. Get this wrong and the beta is measuring corporate actions, not risk.

Worked example · adjusting for a rights issue

In 2021 RIL made a 1:15 rights issue at ₹1,257 when the stock was at ₹1,435 on the record date. The theoretical ex-rights price is (15 × 1435 + 1 × 1257) ÷ 16 = ₹1,423.87. The stock “falling” from 1,435 to 1,424 overnight is not an 0.8% loss — it is an accounting artefact of issuing cheap shares. Unadjusted, that day enters your regression as a spurious negative return.

Step 2 — choose the estimation period and frequency

Two principles in tension:

  • A longer period is statistically better — more observations, tighter standard errors.
  • But the operating and financial risk of the company must not have changed over the period. A company that sold half its business three years ago is not the same company.

On frequency: daily data gives ~1,300 points over five years but suffers from infrequent trading (if the stock does not trade Tuesday, Tuesday's "return" is fictitious) and non-synchronous trading (the stock trades less often than the index constituents, creating positive serial correlation and biasing beta downward). For actively traded stocks with five years of data, daily versus monthly is a matter of convenience. For thinly traded stocks, use weekly or monthly.

His own table: Tata Steel's beta rises from 1.348 to 1.717 simply by switching to 60 monthly returns. Across ten energy companies, daily 1-year, daily 5-year and monthly 5-year betas differ substantially for every single name. The number is not a fact about the company; it is a fact about the estimation choice, and you must disclose the choice.

Step 3 — unlever, average, relever

Observed betas are contaminated by each company's own leverage. To get a clean read on business risk, strip the leverage out, average across peers, then add back your company's leverage.

βU = βL ÷ [1 + (1 − t)(D/E)]  Hamada, assuming βdebt ≈ 0
βL = βU × [1 + (1 − t)(D/E)]  relever at the subject's target D/E
  1. Take 4–6 listed peers; get each one's levered beta and D/E.
  2. Unlever each with its own D/E and tax rate.
  3. Take the median (more robust than the mean with small samples and outliers).
  4. Relever at the subject company's target D/E.

Net debt or gross debt? Net usually wins. Cash is a negative-beta-ish asset; a company with ₹1,000 crore of debt and ₹900 crore of cash does not carry the financial risk of a company with ₹1,000 crore of debt and no cash. Note that net D/E can be negative for a cash-rich company — Spice Islands Apparels in the Kavita case shows net D/E of −0.64, producing an unlevered beta above its levered beta.

Beta and operating risk

Beta reflects two things layered on top of each other: operating risk (how cyclical the business is, and how high its fixed costs are — operating leverage) and financial risk (leverage). Unlevering removes the second and leaves the first. Two firms in the same industry should have similar unlevered betas; if they do not, one of them has a different cost structure or a different business mix, and you should find out which before averaging them.

Cost of warrants and convertibles

A warrant is a call option on the company's own shares. Its cost is not the coupon on the instrument it came attached to. His method:

  1. Note that a levered position in a stock has a levered beta: put 50% in a stock with β = 1.2 and 50% in T-bills and the portfolio beta is 0.6; borrow 50% and invest 150% and it is 1.8.
  2. A call option is exactly such a levered position — it has an option delta and an implicit leverage — so βoption = βstock × (Δ × S ÷ option value), which is much larger than βstock.
  3. Feed that beta into CAPM to get the warrant's cost of capital.

Practical upshot: a 1% coupon convertible bond is not 1% debt. Split it into a straight bond valued at the company's real Kd plus a warrant valued as an option, and cost each piece properly.

4.7 Assembling the WACC, and the bridge to equity

The seven-step routine
  1. List every claim on the balance sheet and mark each as interest-bearing or not, and as a source of capital or not. Write the decision down — you will need it twice more.
  2. Cost of debt: YTM, or rating + FIMMDA spread. Take it after tax at the marginal statutory rate.
  3. Cost of equity: CAPM. Rf from the 10-year G-Sec; ERP as a stated assumption; beta regressed and cross-checked bottom-up.
  4. Any other claims — preference, leases, foreign loans — at their own costs.
  5. Weights at market value. Handle circularity by target D/E or by iteration, and say which.
  6. Blend.
  7. Sanity check: is WACC between Kd(1−t) and Ke? Is it plausible for the sector? Is it above the terminal growth rate by a comfortable margin?

The bridge from enterprise value to equity value

Equity value
  = PV(explicit-period FCF) + PV(terminal value)  = enterprise value of operations
  + market value of non-operating assets  surplus cash, financial investments, vacant land, assets held for sale, non-operational advances to related parties
  − debt and debt-like items  borrowings, unfunded gratuity/leave encashment, stretch creditors, preference shares, lease liabilities
  − contingent liabilities  estimated impact × probability
  − minority interest  if consolidated

He gave an FMCG company valued by DCF and asked which of seven adjustments are needed to get equity value. Six of the seven are “not needed”. His key:

Standalone or consolidated?

His P/S/M example: P Limited owns 80% of S and 20% of M. Consolidation rules mean S is fully consolidated with a minority interest line, while M is equity-accounted (P records 20% of M's profit and nothing of its assets).

  • Consolidated is right when subsidiaries are in the same business and geography — the group operates as one economic entity.
  • Standalone plus separately valued investments is right when subsidiaries are in different lines of business or different operating environments. This is his Tata Steel logic: European operations face a different world from Indian ones, so blending them into one set of margins and one WACC is meaningless.

Either way, be careful: value each subsidiary stake at market value if quoted, at a justified estimate if not, and disclose it if the investments are a large share of value. For Tata Steel they exceeded 35% of the DCF value — at that point the "assumption" is most of the answer.

4.9 ROIC, EVA and the growth equation

Operating Invested Capital (OIC) = Net fixed assets + Operating working capital
the capital actually tied up in generating NOPAT

ROIC = NOPATt ÷ OICt−1  note the lag: this year's profit on last year's capital

EVA = OICt−1 × (ROICt − WACCt) = NOPAT − (WACC × OICt−1)

Enterprise Value = OIC + PV(all future EVA)

EVA is just the DCF rearranged. Instead of “what cash comes out”, it asks “how much did we earn above the charge for the capital we used”. Both give the same value, and he tests exactly that.

g = ROIC × Investment Rate    where   IR = Net Investment ÷ NOPAT

Read it as a sentence: growth equals how good your investments are, times how much of your profit you reinvest. A company with 20% ROIC that reinvests a quarter of its profit grows at 5%. There is no way to grow faster without either better projects or more reinvestment.

Worked example · EVA and FCF must agree (his slide)

OIC ₹100m at year 0, ROIC 20%, investment rate 25%, WACC 13%, terminal growth 5%.

NOPAT = 20% × opening OIC20.022.625.027.0

The investment rate has been restated from 25% to 25%… of a growth rate of 5%, not 5% of anything else. If instead you leave net investment at ₹10m in the terminal year while claiming g = 5%, EVA gives ₹198.05m and FCF gives ₹161.34m — they diverge, and the model is broken. That divergence is the alarm bell.

Sir's trap · the EVA method needs one extra year

From your class notes: “EVA method estimates for t+1 years” and “restate year 4 numbers”. Because EVA in year t depends on OIC at t−1, an EVA model with an N-year explicit period needs the year N+1 forecast to compute the terminal EVA correctly. Forgetting this is why students' EVA and FCF values fail to reconcile even when everything else is right.

4.10 ★ The terminal-year consistency engine

This is Quiz 3, Question 1, and it is the most generalisable technique in the whole course. Learn it as a machine you can run on any set of terminal-year inputs.

Worked example · Quiz 3, Question 1 (his actual paper)

Maura Lartin projects for the terminal year: NOPAT $100m; net investment $0; terminal EBITDA multiple 9×; WACC 10%; EBITDA $130m; ROIC 12%. What mistake did she make? Find the correct terminal value.

The mistake: net investment cannot be zero when the terminal growth rate is positive. g = ROIC × IR, so IR = 0 forces g = 0 — but her own terminal value implies a positive g. (2 marks for identifying this.)

Her FCF = NOPAT − NI = 100 − 0 = 100  1 mark
Her TV = 9 × 130 = 1,170  1 mark

— now back out the growth her own numbers imply —
From TV = FCF(1+g)/(WACC−g), rearranged:  g = (TV × WACC − FCF) ÷ (TV + FCF)
g = (1170 × 0.10 − 100) ÷ (1170 + 100) = 17 ÷ 1270 = 1.3386%  1 mark

Implied investment rate = g ÷ ROIC = 1.3386% ÷ 12% = 11.155%  1 mark
Correct net investment = NOPAT × IR = 100 × 11.155% = 11.155  2 marks
Correct FCF = 100 − 11.155 = 88.845  1 mark
Correct TV = 88.845 × 1.013386 ÷ (0.10 − 0.013386) = 1,039.49  1 mark

The naive answer overstates terminal value by ₹130m, about 12.5% — because it credits the company with growth it never paid for.

The generalised engine — run this on any terminal year
  1. Write down every terminal-year quantity you are given: NOPAT, NI, FCF, EBITDA, multiple, WACC, ROIC, g.
  2. Check the identity g = ROIC × (NI ÷ NOPAT). If it fails, one input is wrong.
  3. Decide which input is the most reliable anchor — usually the exit multiple or the WACC, because those come from the market.
  4. Back out the implied g from the anchor: g = (TV × WACC − FCF) ÷ (TV + FCF).
  5. Force the other quantities to comply: IR = g/ROIC, NI = NOPAT × IR, FCF = NOPAT − NI.
  6. Recompute the terminal value with the Gordon formula and the corrected FCF.

Your class notes show the same engine applied to a second set of numbers — 713.4 × 1.044 ÷ (9.3% − 9%), then discounted ten years, giving 54.29 − 11.05 = 43.24 flagged as “first mistake”, and separately g = 3%, NI = 0 → does not make sense. He clearly runs this drill more than once, which is a strong signal it returns in the interim.

Check yourself · Part IV

Terminal NOPAT is ₹500m, ROIC 15%, terminal g 4.5%. What must net investment be?

IR = g ÷ ROIC = 4.5% ÷ 15% = 30%. Net investment = 500 × 30% = ₹150m. Terminal FCF = 500 − 150 = ₹350m. Any other net investment figure contradicts the stated growth.

Your model treats cash as non-operating and subtracts it in the bridge. Where else must that decision appear?

Two other places. (1) Interest income on that cash must be excluded from net operating income and therefore from NOPAT and FCF. (2) The beta and cost of capital must reflect the operating business only — in practice that means net-debt weights, and the effective cost of debt changes (in his example, from 10% gross to 13% on net debt). Miss either and your two treatments will not reconcile to the same equity value.

A company's shares fell 0.8% on the record date of a 1:10 rights issue priced 20% below market. Is that a real return for beta estimation?

No. Compute the theoretical ex-rights price — (10 × P + 1 × 0.8P) ÷ 11 — and treat the move relative to that, not to the previous close. Otherwise you inject a mechanical negative return into your regression on a day that may have nothing to do with the market's movement, biasing both beta and R².

Why should short-term borrowings not sit inside working capital?

Because they are interest-bearing — a source of capital, not an operating item. They belong in the cost of capital (where you charge for them) and in the bridge (where you subtract them). Leaving them in working capital both understates the investment the business requires and double-counts the financing. It is a liability-consistency violation.

5.1 Public versus private: the four differences

Everything in §5.4–5.6 follows from four gaps between a listed company and a private one. Learn them as a list — they make a clean short-answer question.

  1. Financial data. A listed company files audited quarterly results in a standard format. A private company files an annual return, often with different accounting standards (Ind AS applies only above ₹250 crore; below that there is far more flexibility on fair-value treatment and leases), and the accounts are prepared to minimise tax, not to inform investors.
  2. Market price. There is none. So there is no market capitalisation, no return series, and therefore no beta — which knocks out the standard route to the cost of equity and the market-value weights for WACC.
  3. Ownership structure. Concentrated. The owner is typically undiversified, so CAPM's central assumption fails.
  4. Liquidity. You cannot sell on Tuesday afternoon. That illiquidity is real and priced.

A fifth, which he raised in class: corporate governance in private companies tells you about problems that are going to come. Weak governance is a leading indicator, not a footnote.

5.2 The venture capital method

A VC does not build a ten-year DCF for a company with no revenue. The VC method inverts the problem: if I need a 50% annual return and I think the company will be worth $X in five years, what share must I own today?

Step 1 — Terminal value at exit = (exit-year earnings) × (comparable multiple)
Step 2 — Future value of my investment = Investment × (1 + r)n
Step 3 — Required ownership % = FV of investment ÷ Terminal value
Step 4 — Post-money valuation = Investment ÷ Required ownership %
Step 5 — Pre-money valuation = Post-money − Investment
Step 6 — New shares = Old shares × [ownership% ÷ (1 − ownership%)]  →  Price = Investment ÷ New shares
Worked example · NewVenture, Question 1 Part 1(a) and (b)

John Thompson wants to raise $5m. He projects net income of $5m in year 5. Comparables trade at 20× earnings. The VC requires 50% per annum. The company has 1,000,000 shares outstanding.

Terminal value in year 5 = 20 × 5 = $100m
FV of the VC's money = 5 × 1.55 = 5 × 7.59375 = $37.97m
Required share = 37.97 ÷ 100 = 37.97%  (a)

Post-money = 5 ÷ 0.3797 = $13.17m    Pre-money = 13.17 − 5 = $8.17m
New shares = 1,000,000 × (0.3797 ÷ 0.6203) = 612,100 shares

Notice the elegant cross-check: the founder's 1,000,000 shares are worth the pre-money valuation of $8.17m, so the price per share is $8.17. Pre-money value divided by pre-money shares is the price per share, always. Use it to catch arithmetic errors.

Worked example · raising more money — Part 1(c)

Suppose John needs $12m in this round rather than $5m.

FV = 12 × 1.55 = $91.13m  →  Required share = 91.13 ÷ 100 = 91.13%
Founder keeps 8.87%; his 1m shares imply total shares = 1 ÷ 0.0887 = 11.27m
VC buys 10.27m shares for $12m  →  price = $1.17 per share

The founder's price per share collapses from $8.17 to $1.17 while the company's projected exit value has not changed at all. That is the whole lesson of part (d)'s graph: required ownership rises linearly with the amount raised and exponentially with the discount rate and the holding period. Raising more than you need is extraordinarily expensive.

Worked example · the option pool — Part 1(e)

Samantha Jones argues that to recruit a senior team, management must own 15% by the end of year 5. What share must she insist on today?

The trap: she still needs 37.97% at exit. But the option pool will dilute her between now and then. So she must ask for a stake that survives the dilution:

Required today = 37.97% ÷ (1 − 15%) = 44.68%

Founder's 1m shares are then (1 − 44.68%) = 55.32%  →  total shares = 1 ÷ 0.5532 = 1.81m
VC shares = 0.81m  →  price = $5m ÷ 0.81m = $6.19 per share

Same money, same exit, but the price per share falls from $8.17 to $6.19 — the option pool is being funded entirely out of the founder's pocket, which is exactly why "pre-money with the pool included" is the most fought-over line in a term sheet.

The method in one sentence: compute everybody's required share of the exit pie first, then walk backwards through each dilution event, grossing up by ÷ (1 − dilution%) at each step. Never work forwards.

5.3 Dilution protection, participating preferred, and warrants

Why preferred stock at all?

VCs invest in convertible preferred stock, not common. Standard convertible preferred gives the holder the maximum of the liquidation value and the as-converted common value — downside protection with full upside participation. In the problem set you are told to assume no dividends and a 1:1 conversion ratio, which makes it behave like common for the arithmetic while retaining the protection.

Participating preferred goes further: the holder gets the principal back and converts. It is “double dipping”. Because it takes more of the exit value, the VC needs a smaller headline percentage for the same return — so on paper the founder looks less diluted while actually being worse off in most exit scenarios. The change in perceived risk/reward is the point of part 2(c): participating preferred shifts value to the investor in low and middling exits, and matters little in a very large one.

Anti-dilution: the three regimes

A “down round” — new shares issued below the price prior investors paid — triggers protection clauses. Question 2 of the problem set is the cleanest possible illustration.

Worked example · Question 2, all three scenarios

Setup: Series A bought 10m convertible preferred at $1.00. Founders and management hold 15m common. The company now raises $5m in Series B at a $15m pre-money valuation.

Scenario 1 — no protection. Pre-money shares = 25m, so price = $15m ÷ 25m = $0.60. Series B buys 8.333m shares. Total 33.333m.

Scenario 2 — weighted-average protection. Series A's conversion price is reset using

NCP = [(OB × OCP) + New$] ÷ OA
OB = shares outstanding before; OCP = old conversion price; New$ = amount raised; OA = shares outstanding after

This is circular (the new share count depends on the new price, which depends on the new share count), so solve simultaneously: NCP = $0.8889, Series B price = $0.5714, Series B gets 8.75m shares, Series A converts into 11.25m.

Scenario 3 — full ratchet. Series A's conversion price resets all the way to the new price, as if they had invested today. Solving the same circularity: price = $0.3333, Series A converts into 30m shares, Series B gets 15m.

Anti-dilution protection does not take value from the new investor; it transfers value from the founders to the earlier investor. Under a full ratchet the founders fall from 45% to 25% while Series A rises from 30% to 50%. That is why founders resist ratchets and accept weighted average.

Worked example · Question 3 — pricing a warrant into a deal

Entrepreneur says the company is worth $5m (5m fully diluted shares → $1.00/share). The VC believes it is worth $4m ($0.80/share) and wants to invest $4m. To break the impasse the VC agrees to pay $1.00 per share, but asks for a 5-year option to invest a further $3m at a 20% premium ($1.20/share).

The VC is paying $4m for: 4m shares (worth 4 × $0.80 = $3.2m in her own view) + the warrants.
So the warrants must be worth $4.0m − $3.2m = $0.8m to her.

Valuation the VC is really assigning: $4m pre-money, i.e. $0.80 per share.
Warrant package (option on 2.5m shares at $1.20) valued at $0.8m ≈ $0.32 per underlying share.

The general principle: when a deal contains a security you cannot price directly, price everything you can and treat the residual as the value of the unknown piece. Then ask whether that residual is plausible — is $0.32 a sensible price for a five-year call struck 50% above the VC's own estimate of fair value? Here, probably yes, given start-up volatility. That is the check he wants you to run.

5.6 DLOM, control premium, and key-person value

Discount for lack of marketability (DLOM)

Private shares cannot be sold quickly. That illiquidity is not diversifiable, so it deserves a price. Three empirical methods:

  • Restricted stock studies: compare the price of privately placed, transfer-restricted shares of otherwise-listed companies with their market price. Typical discounts 20–35%.
  • Pre-IPO studies: compare a private placement price with the subsequent IPO price. Historically 30–50%, though narrower in recent samples.
  • Option-pricing (protective put) models: value the put option that would hedge the price over the expected holding period until a sale or IPO. The most theoretically satisfying, because it makes the discount a function of volatility and expected holding period rather than a convention.

The case applies a flat 25%: ₹79.15 cr × 0.75 = ₹59.36 crore. He is explicit that this is “within the empirical range, but a judgment call rather than a model output”, and he asks which method you would defend to a client. The sensitivity is material: 15% DLOM gives ₹67.28 cr, 30% gives ₹55.41 cr — a ₹12 crore swing from one assumption.

Contrast: reported net worth is ₹15.6 crore. Even after every haircut, the business is worth about four times its book value.

Control premium and minority discount

  • Control premium: 20–30% for the ability to direct operations and strategy — set dividend policy, hire and fire, sell the company, change the capital structure.
  • Minority discount: 15–25% for the absence of that ability.

They are two sides of one coin, and the empirical evidence comes from control-premium studies (e.g. FactSet Mergerstat). The exam-relevant point: a DCF built on management's own forecasts is, by default, a control value — it assumes you can implement the plan. Valuing a minority stake on those cash flows without a discount overstates it.

Key-person value

The rule, stated exactly as he does

“The value of the business is inversely proportional to the stature of the key person in the company. The buyer buys the company minus the key person. If the company can survive without the key person, the value of the business will be high.”

The counter-intuitive part is worth stating carefully in an answer: a celebrated, indispensable founder makes the business less valuable to a buyer, because what the buyer acquires is the business without that person. Mitigations that recover some of the value: a non-compete agreement; key-man insurance to fund a buyout or transition; building management depth so operations do not depend on one person; and structuring the deal with an earn-out or escrow tied to the key person's retention.

In Kavita's case it is largely moot — both Kavita and her husband will continue in the business — which is itself a point worth making: identify the issue, then say why it does not bind here.

Exam angle · IPO proceeds — three treatments

How you handle money raised in an IPO depends entirely on what happens to it:

  • Taken out by existing owners (an offer for sale) → ignore it in the valuation; it never enters the firm.
  • Used to pay down debt → change the debt ratio, which changes the cost of capital and therefore the value of the firm.
  • Held as cash for future reinvestment → add the proceeds to the DCF value of the company.

Why she reaches for real options anyway

Cable operators were upgrading to 750 MHz of bandwidth. Of that, 550 MHz went to analog video and 98 MHz to digital services, network control, telephony and internet. The remaining 102 MHz — seventeen 6 MHz channels — sat dark. Martin called it the “stealth tier”.

Sir's trap · what to criticise in a real-options answer
  • The underlying is not traded. Black–Scholes assumes you can construct a replicating portfolio by trading the underlying. You cannot trade "one dark channel per home passed".
  • Volatility of the wrong thing. She uses implied volatility from a one-month option on Cox equity to price a ten-year option on a channel's cash flows. Equity volatility is levered; channel-level volatility is not. The horizon mismatch is severe.
  • The strike is a judgement, not a price. Choosing between "technological cost ≈ 0" and "opportunity cost of forgone analog revenue" changes the answer enormously, and the true cost includes the customer-satisfaction cost of switching a channel off — which she says is "difficult to estimate".
  • Double counting. If the DCF's terminal value already embeds growth from new digital services, adding the stealth-tier option on top counts the same future twice. Asset consistency, again.
  • Additivity. Seventeen options on correlated payoffs are not worth 17 × one option — exercising one changes the value of the rest.
Exam angle · when is a real option genuinely worth valuing?

Three conditions must all hold: (1) there is genuine flexibility — a right without an obligation; (2) there is meaningful uncertainty — with no uncertainty, an option is worth only its intrinsic value; and (3) the flexibility is exclusive — if any competitor can do the same thing, the option has no scarcity value. Cable's dark fibre passes all three. A generic "option to expand the factory" usually fails the third.

5.8 AI in business valuation

Lecture 18. His framing is the important part, and it is exactly the kind of statement that becomes a one-line exam question:

What changes and what does not

Valuation theory: unchanged. DCF, multiples, cost of capital, terminal value all work exactly as before. What changes is how the inputs get built and checked.

From prompts to agents

2023-era AI was single-turn: a human asks, the model answers, and every next step needs a fresh instruction. Agentic AI plans and acts — it breaks a goal into steps, calls tools and data sources, checks its own output and continues without a prompt for each step. For valuation that means multi-step work (sourcing peer data, building comps, updating models) can run with far less manual back-and-forth. The workflow he showed: define objective → source data → screen peers → normalise metrics → develop scenarios → build and validate → generate output, with the agent looping back to source more data after scenarios are built.

Where it actually helps

  • Peer screening. NLP compares product mix, geography and customers, going beyond static SIC/GICS codes; clustering groups companies by growth, margin and risk profile, flagging economically similar peers across different industry labels; the peer set refreshes as business mix shifts.
  • Data extraction. Indian filings are lodged in XBRL, a structured tagged format — convert to Excel with any XBRL-to-XLSX tool, or drag the file into Excel and read it as an XML table.
  • Scenario construction. Not labels like “optimistic”, but a specific set of driver values, one per scenario.
Sir's trap · the mistake he flags, and the rule he sets

The mistake: letting the model produce a number in prose. His fix, verbatim: “Force the model to write the Python code (or code in some other language) and show the computations for FCF.” Code is auditable; a paragraph is not.

His disclosure rule for the project, also verbatim: “Should you use Chat-GPT or Claude, Gemini, or Command R+? Use them, if you want. But write the prompts used, the response obtained, and how the same has been used in the report.” The same applies to equity research reports — “use them, give references, and never go by the recommendations made in such reports as they are mostly biased. They contain useful information about the industry.”

Driver-based revenue forecasting

The naive method — last year's revenue × (1 + g) — hides what is driving the number. Build the driver instead: break revenue into real components and project each on its own driver.

Anthropic (his worked example)

2026 revenue $42,560m: API & cloud 72.8%, Claude Code 11.3%, enterprise subscriptions 9.3%, consumer 4.2%, other 2.3%. The driver: billed token volume × realised blended price. Scenarios then become physical: bear 2,600 trillion tokens at $8.75/MTok; base 3,100 at $10.00; bull 3,600 at $10.50.

The CSRP — a term you must know

In Indian practice the CSRP is capped by convention at about 4%, and the market risk premium sits at 6–7%. Note this is the practitioner's version of the same problem §5.5 solved with total beta — two routes to pricing non-diversifiable-in-practice risk.

On beta sourcing: comparable companies (can be computed any time, refined peer set, but subjective and data-hungry) versus Damodaran's published India dataset (country- and industry-specific, 15+ years of history, widely accepted — but published only once a year, on 5 January). On the debt-to-capital ratio the four candidates are, in order of preference: market values, target ratio, industry ratio, book values.

Terminal value — a third option

Alongside Gordon growth and exit multiples, practitioners use the H-model, in which growth fades linearly from the horizon rate to the perpetual rate before settling. Use it when a business is doing heavy capex and it is not fair to snap from 20% growth to 4% in a single year. Your class notes flag exactly this: “business doing a lot of capex → not fair to use the Gordon model → H-model, multi-period cash flow”.

Practical problems in the market approach

  • Databases lie about deals. A transaction negotiated on net asset value gets recorded as a multiple. A deal where 100% of equity was bought but only 60% of the price was disclosed shows a nonsense multiple. Period adjustments to revenue and margins, management replacement cost, and actuarial gratuity provisioning all sit inside a real "adjusted EBITDA" and none of them are in the database.
  • Different databases give different multiples for the same company, because they compute the numerator and denominator differently.
  • Ind AS 116 broke EBITDA comparability across the lease-accounting transition (see §2.10).
  • Adjusted EBITDA is the most-used term in deal-making and the least standardised.

How M&A valuation differs

The typical deal construct, worth memorising as a sentence: “an EV/EBITDA multiple of x on sustainable EBITDA on a cash-free, debt-free basis, adjusted for normalised working capital and any other non-operating assets.”

Normalised working capital = the sustainable amount of operating working capital required to run the business normally, excluding temporary or abnormal fluctuations. It is a negotiated number and it moves the price directly.

Primary transaction

The company issues new shares; capital flows into the company; pre-money + investment = post-money; funds used for growth. Buyer is underwriting margin expansion and above-historical growth.

Secondary transaction

An existing shareholder sells; consideration flows to the seller; nothing enters the company. Driven by liquidity and exit. Typically at a 15–20% discount to the primary price — capital creation versus wealth transfer.

Five deal patterns worth carrying into an exam

  • Packaging — forward integration. No DCF possible (seller exiting, no earn-out), no transaction comps available, so offers came in at 5× EV/EBITDA. The advisor convinced the buyer to value on net asset value plus goodwill of two years' PAT — the seller landed at 8× EV/EBITDA. Lesson: when both standard methods fail, construct a method the counterparty finds fair.
  • Transformer — monopolising the market. Buyer valued on intangible benefits of the acquisition, not multiples or DCF, and framed downside risk as one year of its own profits. Seller wanted 850, deal closed at 650, with ₹200 of land and buildings excluded and taken on rent.
  • Die-casting — the sunset trap. ₹345 cr revenue, ₹70 cr adjusted EBITDA, #1 with 70%+ share, 30-day cash conversion — and still hard to sell. Structural decline (revenue ₹360→345 cr, EBITDA ₹80→70 cr) means low visibility, lower terminal value, lower multiples. ₹450 cr of surplus real estate on the books requires a demerger with tax consequences: fewer buyers, more complexity.
  • Filtration — the tax structure gap. ₹100 cr+ of liquid assets trapped inside the company. The buyer would take it as a dividend (~36% tax); the seller would receive it as capital gain (~15%). The differing tax treatment creates a real gap between the seller's expectation and the buyer's willingness to pay — and structuring, not arguing, is what closes it.
  • Staffing — the auction. Five bidders produced equity values of 150, 250, 250, 300 and 450 (later cut to 250) for the same company, with wildly different structures — 100% exit at closing, versus 12–36 month promoter lock-ins and earn-outs. Value is not a property of the company alone; it is a property of the company and the buyer together.

Synergy, done properly

If an acquisition question appears, the structure is fixed:

  1. Value the acquirer standalone and the target standalone.
  2. Value the combined entity using only incremental, timing-specific, risk-matched synergy cash flows. Treat revenue, cost, tax and financing synergies separately, and make implementation costs and timing explicit.
  3. Report the synergy value and then the acquirer's net gain after the premium paid. If the premium exceeds the synergy, the acquirer's shareholders lose — however good the strategic logic sounds.
  4. Never substitute EPS accretion for economic value creation. A high-P/E company buying a low-P/E company always shows accretion arithmetically, and that tells you nothing about value.

Check yourself · Part V

A VC needs a 40% annual return over 4 years on a $8m investment. Exit-year earnings are projected at $10m and comparables trade at 15×. What share does she need, and what is the pre-money valuation?

Terminal value = 15 × 10 = $150m. FV of investment = 8 × 1.4⁴ = 8 × 3.8416 = $30.73m. Required share = 30.73 ÷ 150 = 20.49%.

Post-money = 8 ÷ 0.2049 = $39.04m. Pre-money = 39.04 − 8 = $31.04m.

Same deal, but management must hold 20% at exit. What share must the VC take today?

20.49% ÷ (1 − 0.20) = 25.61%. She must own more today so that after the option pool dilutes her she still holds 20.49% at exit. Post-money falls to 8 ÷ 0.2561 = $31.24m, so pre-money drops to $23.24m — the pool is paid for by the founders.

Why does a full ratchet hurt founders so much more than weighted-average protection?

A full ratchet reprices the entire earlier investment as if it had been made at the new, lower price, regardless of how small the new round is. Weighted average reprices only in proportion to the size of the down round relative to the shares already outstanding. In the worked example the founders fall from 45% to 42.9% under weighted average and all the way to 25% under a full ratchet — and the new investor gets exactly 25% either way, so the entire difference comes out of the founders.

You value a private company at ₹200 crore using a 25% DLOM. A classmate argues the buyer is a listed strategic acquirer, so no DLOM should apply. Who is right?

Your classmate, mostly. DLOM compensates for the holder's inability to sell. A listed strategic acquirer is buying the business to operate it, and its own shareholders hold liquid shares — so illiquidity at the target level is not a cost they bear. In a private→public transaction the illiquidity discount shrinks or disappears, and the cost of equity should move toward plain CAPM because the buyer's investors are diversified. Both effects push value up. The counter-argument worth noting: if the acquirer takes a minority stake with no exit route, illiquidity is real again.

An analyst adds a real-option value for a factory's "expansion optionality" on top of a DCF whose terminal value already assumes 6% growth. What is your objection?

Double counting — a violation of asset consistency. The 6% terminal growth already assumes the company keeps expanding and reinvesting; the option to expand is the mechanism by which that growth happens, not a separate asset. Add the option only for flexibility the base DCF explicitly excludes — as Martin does, where her DCF counts the capex for the stealth tier but none of its revenue. Second objection: an option to expand a generic factory is not exclusive, so competitors erode its value.

6.1 Regression, read properly

A regression fits the best straight line through a cloud of points by minimising the sum of squared vertical distances. In this course it shows up in exactly three places, and they are the three places to expect a question.

WhereThe regressionWhat the slope means
Beta estimation (§4.6)Stock return on market returnβ — sensitivity to market moves
Laura Martin's ROIC analysis (§5.7)Adjusted EV ÷ average invested capital, on 1999E ROICHow much EV/IC the market pays per point of ROIC
The multiples regression (§2.6)Sector P/E on growth, payout and betaThe market's implicit price for each fundamental

The market model

ri,t = αi + βi · rm,t + εt

β = Cov(ri, rm) ÷ Var(rm)   =   ρi,m × (σi ÷ σm)
  • β (the slope) is what you want — systematic risk.
  • α (the intercept) is Jensen's alpha: the average return earned beyond what CAPM predicts. A positive, significant alpha means outperformance. In practice α is almost never statistically distinguishable from zero, which is itself an examinable observation.
  • ε is the residual — everything the market did not explain. Its variance is the specific risk.
Worked example · computing beta from summary statistics

A stock has σi = 32% p.a., the index has σm = 18% p.a., and the correlation between them is 0.45.

β = ρ × (σi ÷ σm) = 0.45 × (32 ÷ 18) = 0.80
R2 = ρ2 = 0.452 = 20.25%
Systematic risk = R2 × σi2 = 0.2025 × 322 = 207.4  →  σ = 14.4%
Non-systematic = (1 − R2) × σi2 = 0.7975 × 1024 = 816.6  →  σ = 28.6%

Sanity check: 14.4² + 28.6² = 207 + 818 = 1,025 ≈ 32². Variances add; standard deviations do not.

6.2 The t-test — is that coefficient real?

A regression will always produce a number. The t-statistic asks whether that number is distinguishable from zero given the noise in the data.

t = (estimated coefficient − hypothesised value) ÷ standard error of the coefficient

Usually the hypothesis is “the true value is zero”, so   t = coefficient ÷ SE
Degrees of freedom = n − k  n observations, k estimated parameters; for a simple regression k = 2
|t|Interpretation at the 5% level
< 1.0Nothing there. The coefficient is smaller than its own noise.
≈ 1.7Significant at 10% for moderate samples — “weak evidence”.
≈ 2.0The working threshold. For n > 30 the 5% two-tailed critical value is close to 1.96–2.05. Learn 2 and use it.
> 2.6Significant at 1%.
Worked example · is this beta significantly different from 1?

You regress 60 monthly returns of a stock on the Nifty and obtain β = 1.35 with a standard error of 0.22.

Test 1 — is beta different from zero?
t = 1.35 ÷ 0.22 = 6.14  →  far above 2. The stock clearly has market exposure.

Test 2 — is beta different from 1? (the question that actually matters)
t = (1.35 − 1.00) ÷ 0.22 = 0.35 ÷ 0.22 = 1.59  →  below 2.
You cannot reject the hypothesis that the true beta is 1.

95% confidence interval
1.35 ± 2 × 0.22 = [0.91, 1.79]  df = 58

Now feed both ends into CAPM with Rf = 6.8% and ERP = 7%: Ke ranges from 13.2% to 19.3%. That six-point spread on the discount rate is the honest confidence interval around your valuation, and saying so is exactly the humility he rewards. It is also the technical case for cross-checking with a bottom-up beta.

Sir's trap · the small-sample regression

Regressing sector P/E on growth, payout and beta with ten comparables means n = 10, k = 4, so df = 6. The critical t at 5% with 6 degrees of freedom is 2.45, not 2. With that little data, standard errors are large, almost nothing is significant, and the R² is inflated by overfitting. If asked to critique such a regression, say: too few observations relative to parameters; check the t-statistics before using any coefficient; report the adjusted R², not the raw one.

Adjusted R² penalises extra regressors: R̄² = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − k). Raw R² can only rise when you add a variable; adjusted R² can fall, which is what makes it the honest measure.

6.3 R², risk decomposition, and total beta

R2 = systematic variance ÷ total variance   =   ρ2
1 − R2 = the diversifiable share

For a typical individual stock R² sits between 0.10 and 0.35 — meaning 65–90% of the variation in a single stock's returns has nothing to do with the market. That is not a defect of the model; it is the reason diversification works.

Worked example · total beta, the bridge to Part V

Damodaran's device for undiversified owners. If an investor holds only this asset, she is exposed to the whole σi, not just the systematic part. Scale beta up by the reciprocal of the correlation:

Total beta = β ÷ ρ = β ÷ √R2

Example: β = 0.90, R2 = 0.25 → ρ = 0.5 → Total beta = 1.80
Ke diversified  = 6.8% + 0.90 × 7% = 13.1%
Ke undiversified = 6.8% + 1.80 × 7% = 19.4%

This is the rigorous version of the “double the beta” shortcut in §5.5 — and notice that with R² = 0.25 the two coincide exactly, because ρ = 0.5. The Kavita numbers give the same story from the other direction: non-systematic risk about five times systematic implies R² ≈ 1/6, so ρ ≈ 0.41, so total beta ≈ 2.45 × β. Applied to the case's β of 0.51 that is a total beta near 1.25 — and the implied Ke climbs toward the 71% figure once you account for the full variance scaling.

Worked example · reading Laura Martin's regression

Her Exhibit 5 plots adjusted EV ÷ average invested capital against 1999E ROIC for eight cable and entertainment companies, with R² = 70%.

  • What it says: 70% of the cross-sectional variation in how the market values invested capital is explained by ROIC alone. That is a genuinely strong relationship for a cross-section, and it justifies the method.
  • What it does not say: nothing about causation, and nothing about whether the level of the whole sector is right. If every cable stock is 30% overvalued, the regression fits beautifully and every target price is 30% too high.
  • Sample size: eight points. df = 6, so a coefficient needs |t| > 2.45. With R² = 70% and n = 8 the slope is probably significant — F ≈ R²(n−2)/(1−R²) = 0.70 × 6 / 0.30 = 14, comfortably above the 5% critical value of about 6 — but the prediction interval around any single fitted value is wide.
  • The mechanical step: Cox's ROIC improves 0.8% (80bp), she reads a target multiple of 1.594 off the fitted line, applies it to average invested capital of $12,136m to get an implied EV of $19,345m, then bridges to equity: + non-consolidated assets $12,292m + other assets $400m + cash and option proceeds $23m − debt $3,800m = $28,260m ÷ 565.2m shares = $50.00.

That bridge is worth studying on its own — it is exactly the EV-to-equity bridge of §4.7, in a real analyst's hands.

6.4 The numericals he can build from this

Given the material and the +4/−1 format, these are the shapes to be ready for. Each is a 60–90 second calculation.

  1. Compute beta from σ, σm and ρ — then decompose total risk into systematic and specific, and read off R².
  2. Test whether beta differs from 1 given a coefficient and standard error; build the 95% interval; convert both ends into a cost-of-equity range.
  3. Convert beta to total beta using R², then compute the undiversified cost of equity and the resulting equity value. This links Part VI to Part V and is the most likely "hard" numerical.
  4. Unlever and relever a set of peer betas with different D/E ratios, take the median, relever at a target — with at least one comparable carrying negative net debt to see if you handle it.
  5. Read a regression output table — coefficient, SE, t, R², n — and answer: which coefficients are usable, what is the predicted value, what is wrong with the specification.
  6. Compute the implied growth from an exit multiple and a WACC, then force net investment to comply (§4.10). This is a quant question dressed as a valuation question.
  7. Compute an implied volatility or an option value given Black–Scholes inputs, or — more likely — be given the option value and asked which input is the least reliable and why (§5.7).
Exam angle · the safest way to lose four marks

Under +4/−1, a question you answer correctly is worth 4 and one you get wrong costs 1 — so guessing pays whenever you can eliminate even one option (expected value of a blind 1-in-4 guess is 0.25, and it rises fast as you eliminate). The real risk is a confident wrong answer on a trap question, because you will not flag it for review. The four numerical traps that produce confident wrong answers: forgetting the (1+g) in a terminal value; using after-tax Kd with CCF; forgetting that the borrowed cash stays in the firm; and testing β against 0 when the question asks about 1.

7.2 The trap sheet — twenty ways to lose marks, ranked

  1. Adjusting things that need no adjustment. Six of seven in his Quiz 3 list were "not needed". Ask every time: is this asset's income already in my cash flow?
  2. Growth with no reinvestment. g = ROIC × IR must hold, especially in the terminal year.
  3. Forgetting the (1+g) in the terminal value, or discounting it by N+1 periods instead of N.
  4. Using after-tax Kd with CCF. CCF already contains the shield; pair it with the pre-tax WACC.
  5. Discounting FCFE at WACC. Equity flows go with Ke. Always.
  6. Growing FCFE while holding debt fixed in rupees. Constant WACC ⟹ constant D/E ⟹ debt grows at g.
  7. Forgetting that borrowed cash stays in the firm (the ₹50m in Quiz 1 Q4).
  8. Book-value weights in WACC. Never, silently.
  9. Coupon rate as the cost of debt. It is the YTM.
  10. Short-term borrowings inside working capital. They are debt.
  11. Cash treated one way in the flow and another way in the bridge. Pick a lane and stay in it.
  12. Testing β against 0 when the question asks about 1.
  13. Trusting a regression coefficient without looking at its t-statistic, especially with fewer than 15 observations.
  14. Adding a real-option value on top of a DCF that already grows. Double counting.
  15. Working VC dilution forwards. Always compute the exit-pie shares first, then gross up backwards by ÷(1 − dilution).
  16. Applying a DLOM to a strategic listed buyer. The discount compensates for your inability to sell.
  17. Forgetting the extra year in an EVA model. EVA at t needs OIC at t−1, so an N-year model needs the N+1 forecast.
  18. Mixing real cash flows with nominal discount rates, or rupee flows with dollar rates.
  19. Mixing units — ₹ crore in one row, ₹ million in the next.
  20. Using unadjusted prices for beta across a bonus, split or rights issue.
