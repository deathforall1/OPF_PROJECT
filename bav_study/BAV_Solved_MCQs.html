Q1★ ACTUAL PYQ · Terminal value
The EBITDA of a company in the last year of the explicit forecasting period is $200 million. The free cash flow for the same year is $80 million. The cost of capital is 12%. If the terminal growth rate is 5%, what is the terminal EBITDA multiple?
A8.6×
B6.0×
C5.7×
D15.0×
Ans: B
Working
TV = FCF × (1+g) ÷ (WACC − g) = 80 × 1.05 ÷ (0.12 − 0.05) = 84 ÷ 0.07 = $1,200m Terminal EBITDA multiple = TV ÷ EBITDA = 1,200 ÷ 200 = 6.0×

⚠ 5.7× is what you get by dropping the (1+g): 80/0.07 = 1,142.9 ÷ 200. 15.0× divides by the FCF instead of the EBITDA.

This is the multiplier consistency check in reverse: your Gordon terminal value implies a multiple, and 6.0× is what a mature, modestly growing business should look like. If it had come out at 25×, the g or the WACC would be wrong.

Q2★ ACTUAL PYQ · VC method
After Series A the cap table is: founders 1,000,000 shares, Series A 250,000 shares (total 1,250,000). The Series A investor holds 20%. At Series B the pre-money is $50 million and a new investor puts in $20 million. If Series A wants to hold 20% after Series B, approximately how much must it invest in Series B?
A$14 million
B$4 million
C$3 million
D$5 million
Ans: D
Working
Price per share = $50m ÷ 1.25m shares = $40.00 Series A's existing stake = 250,000 × 40 = $10m Let x = what Series A puts in. Post-money = 50 + 20 + x = 70 + x (10 + x) ÷ (70 + x) = 0.20 10 + x = 14 + 0.2x ⟹ 0.8x = 4 ⟹ x = $5m

⚠ $4m is the trap: 20% of the $70m post-money is $14m, less the $10m already held. That ignores the fact that your own investment enlarges the post-money too. You must solve for x on both sides.

Check: A buys 5 ÷ 40 = 125,000 shares → holds 375,000 of 1,250,000 + 500,000 + 125,000 = 1,875,000 = 20.0% ✓

Q3★ ACTUAL PYQ · Quant
An analyst regressed the P/E of 10 companies in a sector on their projected growth rate in EPS. Figures in brackets are t-statistics:
P/E = 16.38 + 1.2 × g(EPS) (3.24) (0.8)Which is the most likely interpretation?
AThe ROE of an average company in the industry is lower than the required rate of return
BCompanies with a higher projected growth in EPS do necessarily trade at a higher P/E
CNone of the above
DFactors other than growth in EPS have no impact on the P/E of these companies
Ans: C
Working
n = 10, k = 2 ⟹ df = 8, 5% critical t ≈ 2.31 intercept t = 3.24 ⟹ significant slope t = 0.8 ⟹ NOT significant — growth does not explain P/E in this sample

Take the options one at a time. The growth option is contradicted by the insignificant slope. "Other factors have no impact" overclaims — an insignificant growth coefficient says growth does not explain P/E, not that nothing else does. The ROE statement is not addressable from this regression at all. So none of the above.

⚠ The whole question turns on reading the bracketed numbers as t-statistics, not standard errors. Rule of thumb |t| > 2; with only 10 observations the bar is higher still at 2.31.

Q4Multiples
A company earns an ROE of 15%, has a cost of equity of 15%, and grows at 6% a year. What is its justified price-to-book ratio?
A1.5×
B2.5×
CCannot be found without the payout ratio
D1.0×
Ans: D
Working
P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (K_e − g) = (0.15 − 0.06) ÷ (0.15 − 0.06) = 1.0×

When ROE = K_e the g cancels — P/B is 1.0 whatever the growth rate. The company earns exactly what its shareholders demand, so growth creates nothing (NPVGO = 0).

Q5Multiples
A firm is expected to grow at 0% and trades at a P/E of 12.5×. What is its cost of equity?
A8.0%
B12.5%
C6.25%
DCannot be found without ROE
Ans: A
Working
At g = 0: P/E = 1 ÷ K_e ⟹ K_e = 1 ÷ 12.5 = 8.0%

⚠ The ROE term vanishes at zero growth — which is exactly why P/E tells you nothing about ROE there, and P/B does.

Q6Multiples
Company A trades at 15× with 10% growth; Company B at 24× with 20% growth. Which looks cheaper on PEG, and what is the objection to relying on it?
AA at 1.5 vs B at 1.2 — PEG always favours low growth
BB at 1.2 vs A at 1.5 — but PEG assumes P/E is linear in g and ignores risk entirely
CThey are identical once growth is netted off
DPEG cannot be compared across different growth rates
Ans: B
Working
PEG_A = 15 ÷ 10 = 1.5 PEG_B = 24 ÷ 20 = 1.2 ⟹ B looks cheaper

But P/E is convex in g — g sits in the numerator through the payout term and in the denominator through (K_e − g). So dividing by g systematically flatters fast growers. PEG also carries no risk term: same PEG, very different betas, not equally attractive.

Q8DCF
EBIT ₹300m in perpetuity, tax 30%, cost of equity 18%, debt ₹200m at 10%, zero growth. What is the equity value by the FCFE route?
A₹980.0m
B₹1,088.9m
C₹1,166.7m
D₹1,288.9m
Ans: B
Working
Interest = 200 × 10% = 20 PAT = (300 − 20) × 0.7 = 196 (g = 0, so FCFE = PAT) Equity = 196 ÷ 0.18 = ₹1,088.9m EV = 1,088.9 + 200 = ₹1,288.9m

Note the order: FCFE needs no weights, so equity comes first — and only then can you build market-value weights for the WACC. That is the escape from circularity.

Q9DCF
For the same firm, what is the capital cash flow and the rate it must be discounted at?
ACCF = 216 at a WACC of 16.29%
BCCF = 216 at a pre-tax WACC of 16.76%
CCCF = 210 at a WACC of 16.29%
DCCF = 210 at a pre-tax WACC of 16.76%
Ans: B
Working
FCF = 300 × 0.7 = 210 CCF = FCF + t·K_d·D = 210 + (0.30 × 20) = 216 pre-tax WACC = (1088.9/1288.9)(18%) + (200/1288.9)(10%) = 16.759% check: 216 ÷ 0.16759 = 1,288.9 ✓ same EV

⚠ CCF already contains the tax shield, so the discount rate must NOT carry the (1−t). Using the after-tax WACC of 16.29% counts the shield twice and overstates value.

Q10DCF
A levered firm has K_e = 20%, K_d = 10%, D/E = 0.5 and a tax rate of 30%. What is ρ, the unlevered cost of equity?
A15.00%
B16.67%
C18.18%
D17.41%
Ans: D
Working
K_e = ρ + (ρ − K_d)(1 − t)(D/E) 20 = ρ + (ρ − 10)(0.7)(0.5) = ρ + 0.35ρ − 3.5 23.5 = 1.35ρ ⟹ ρ = 17.407%

ρ is pure business risk — what shareholders would demand with no debt at all. It is the starting point for every APV valuation.

Q11DCF
Year-5 free cash flow is ₹240m, terminal growth 5%, WACC 12%. What is the present value of the terminal value today?
A₹3,600.0m
B₹1,823.9m
C₹2,042.7m
D₹2,144.9m
Ans: C
Working
TV at end of yr 5 = 240 × 1.05 ÷ (0.12 − 0.05) = 252 ÷ 0.07 = ₹3,600m PV = 3,600 ÷ 1.12⁵ = 3,600 ÷ 1.76234 = ₹2,042.7m

⚠ Two slips live here. ₹1,823.9m drops the (1+g); discounting six periods instead of five gives ₹1,823.8m as well. The TV sits at the end of year 5, so it comes back exactly five periods.

Q12DCF
Your FCF model gives an equity value of ₹500 cr and your FCFE model ₹460 cr. The model uses a constant WACC, growth of 6%, and holds debt fixed at ₹200 cr. What is the most likely cause?
AThe tax rate has been applied to PBT instead of EBIT
BThe terminal value has been discounted one period too many
CThe beta was estimated over the wrong window
DNet new borrowing of g × D = ₹12 cr a year is missing from FCFE
Ans: D
Working
constant WACC ⟹ constant D/E ⟹ debt must grow at g net new borrowing = g × D = 0.06 × 200 = ₹12 cr each year FCFE = FCF − Interest(1−t) + net new borrowing

Nine times out of ten this is the cause of an FCF/FCFE mismatch. Either let the debt grow at g and keep the rates constant, or hold debt fixed and switch to APV.

Q13DCF
Enterprise value is ₹1,000 cr. The firm has ₹200 cr of debt, ₹100 cr of preference capital, ₹50 cr of surplus cash excluded from the cash flows, and a contingent liability with an expected value of ₹20 cr. What is the equity value?
A₹730 cr
B₹750 cr
C₹700 cr
D₹630 cr
Ans: A
Working
1,000 + 50 surplus cash − 200 debt − 100 preference − 20 contingent = ₹730 cr

⚠ Preference capital is a claim ahead of equity — it goes into the WACC at its own cost with no (1−t), and it must come out at the bridge. Miss it and you hand the preference holders' money to the equity.

Q14Terminal value
A terminal year shows EBITDA of $250 million and free cash flow of $100 million. An exit multiple of 8× EBITDA is applied and terminal growth is 4%. What cost of capital is implied by that exit multiple?
A9.0%
B9.2%
C5.2%
D13.2%
Ans: B
Working
TV = 8 × 250 = $2,000m TV = FCF(1+g) ÷ (WACC − g) ⟹ 2,000 = 104 ÷ (WACC − 0.04) WACC − 0.04 = 104 ÷ 2,000 = 0.052 ⟹ WACC = 9.2%

The mirror of the PYQ: there you were given the WACC and asked for the multiple; here you are given the multiple and asked for the rate. Same identity, rearranged. If the WACC your model actually uses is 13%, then an 8× exit multiple is too generous.

Q16Beta
The covariance between a stock's returns and the index is 0.0288 and the variance of the index is 0.0225. What is the stock's beta?
A0.64
B1.28
C0.78
D1.20
Ans: B
Working
β = Cov(r_i, r_m) ÷ Var(r_m) = 0.0288 ÷ 0.0225 = 1.28

Equivalently β = ρ × (σ_i ÷ σ_m). Here σ_m = √0.0225 = 15%. If the stock's σ were 24%, then ρ = 1.28 × 15/24 = 0.80 and R² = 0.64.

Q17Beta
A comparable company has a levered beta of 1.30, a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.80 and a tax rate of 25%. What is its unlevered beta?
A1.04
B0.81
C0.72
D0.87
Ans: B
Working
β_U = β_L ÷ [1 + (1 − t)(D/E)] = 1.30 ÷ [1 + 0.75 × 0.80] = 1.30 ÷ 1.60 = 0.8125

Unlever each peer with its own D/E and tax rate, take the median (robust to outliers in a small sample), then relever at your company's target D/E.

Q19Cost of capital

Q21ROIC

A15.0%

Q26Anti-dilution
Series A holds 8m shares with a conversion price of $2.00. Total shares outstanding before the new round are 20m. The company raises $6m by issuing 5m new shares. Under weighted-average anti-dilution, what is Series A's new conversion price?
A$1.53
B$2.00
C$1.20
D$1.84
Ans: D
Working
NCP = [(OB × OCP) + New$] ÷ OA = [(20m × $2.00) + $6m] ÷ (20m + 5m) = ($40m + $6m) ÷ 25m = $1.84

Series A now converts into 8m × (2.00 ÷ 1.84) = 8.70m shares. The new investor's percentage is unchanged — the protection transfers value from the founders to the earlier investor. A full ratchet would reset the price all the way to the new round's price.

Q28Private valuation
An owner-manager draws ₹6 lakh a year; the market salary for the role is ₹30 lakh. Reported PAT is ₹2.40 cr and the tax rate is 25%. What is normalised PAT?
A₹2.16 cr
B₹2.40 cr
C₹1.96 cr
D₹2.22 cr
Ans: D
Working

Q29Private valuation
A comparable has a beta of 0.90 and its regression against the index has an R² of 0.36. With a risk-free rate of 7% and an equity risk premium of 7%, what cost of equity applies to a completely undiversified owner?
A17.5%
B13.3%
C24.5%
D10.5%

Q32Real options
A patent gives exclusive rights for 8 years. Developing the drug costs ₹800 cr today and the PV of the cash flows if developed is ₹700 cr. Outcomes are highly uncertain. Is the patent worth nothing?
AIt has value only once the NPV turns positive

Ans: B
Working

Q35Quant
EV/EBITDA is regressed on ROIC for 12 companies: EV/EBITDA = 4.2 + 0.35 × ROIC(%), with t-statistics of 2.9 and 2.6 and an R² of 41%. What is the best interpretation?
AROIC fully determines EV/EBITDA in this sector
BThe slope is insignificant, so ROIC does not affect the multiple
CBoth coefficients are significant, and ROIC explains 41% of the cross-sectional variation — the other 59% is everything else
D41% of the companies in the sample are correctly valued
Ans: C
Working
n = 12, k = 2 ⟹ df = 10, 5% critical t ≈ 2.23 2.9 and 2.6 both exceed it ⟹ both coefficients significant R² = 41% ⟹ 41% of the VARIATION explained, not 41% of the companies

Contrast with the P/E PYQ, where the slope's t of 0.8 killed it. Here the relationship is real — which is exactly the logic behind Laura Martin's ROIC target price, where the same regression on cable companies had R² = 70%.

⚠ R² is about variance explained. It never says anything about how many companies are "correctly valued", and a significant slope never means a variable is the only thing that matters.

Q36Guest lecture

Q38Guest lecture
A target's trade creditors are on 60-day terms but it is actually paying at 165 days. How should this be handled in the bridge from enterprise value to equity value?
ALeave it entirely inside working capital
BThe excess is a stretch creditor — a debt-like item to be subtracted
CAdd it as a surplus asset
DIgnore it, since trade credit carries no interest
Ans: B
Working
normal terms = 60 days ⟶ stays in operating working capital actual = 165 days excess 105 days ⟶ disguised borrowing ⟶ debt-like item, SUBTRACT

The full debt-like list: borrowings · unfunded gratuity and leave encashment · stretch creditors · non-convertible preference shares · lease liabilities · contingent liabilities at estimated impact × probability · minority interest.

Q39Guest lecture
A database records a transaction at 6× EV/EBITDA. The advisor knows the deal was actually negotiated on net asset value plus two years' PAT as goodwill. What is the lesson for comparable transaction multiples?
ANAV-based deals cannot be compared at all
BThe multiple is fine because the price paid is the price paid
CThe multiple was back-solved from a price set on a different basis, so CTM data can be spurious
DThe database is simply wrong and should be corrected
Ans: C
Working
deal negotiated on NAV + goodwill ⟶ database stores a multiple ⟹ the 6× describes nothing about how the price was actually reached

The other database traps: a deal where 100% of equity was bought but only 60% of the consideration was disclosed; period adjustments to revenue and margins; management replacement cost; actuarial gratuity provisioning — all inside a real 'adjusted EBITDA' and none of them visible in the database. Different databases also compute the same company's multiple differently.

Q40Guest lecture
A business is midway through a heavy capital-expenditure programme and is growing at 22%, well above its long-run rate. Which terminal-value method is most appropriate?
AA zero-growth perpetuity
BThe H-model, in which growth fades from the horizon rate to the perpetual rate
CGordon growth with a higher terminal g to reflect the capex
DAn exit multiple applied to current-year EBITDA
Ans: B
Working
Gordon assumes growth snaps from 22% to ~4% in a single year — not credible H-model: growth fades linearly to the perpetual rate, then continues forever

Three terminal-value methods are on the syllabus: Gordon growth, the H-model, and exit multiples (EV/EBITDA or EV/EBIT on terminal-year earnings). The alternative to the H-model is simply to lengthen the explicit forecast period until steady state is credible — which is the same idea by another route.
